Timothy had a new bookbag, and Ashleigh wasn’t there.

“He’s been biking,” the younger boy explained, from across the aisle.

It was Monday, the start of the last full week of school before Thanksgiving. The weather was turning properly cold, now, and most of the leaves that were going to fall had fallen. Conor had spent the day before with his parents—the third Sunday in a row—and some part of him begrudgingly acknowledged that it was doing something. He felt—

Uncaffeinated?

—smoothed out, somehow, everything a little less staticky. Maybe it was just the sheer number of minutes in which nothing in particular happened, some part of his brain that had come to believe that parents are for meddling slowly unlearning. They’d been reading by the fireplace, and he had gotten up to make hot chocolate for himself, and had been slightly surprised to discover that he’d made three cups without thinking, and somehow not surprised that his mother and father received them and drank them without a word, all of their appreciation conveyed in warm smiles and gentle nods. He’d gone to bed at eight—not out of exhaustion or fatigue, but simply because he’d felt sleepy, and ready for the day to be done.

He’d woken up an hour before his alarm, padded softly around the house, eaten his frozen Eggos in the silence of the empty kitchen and decided, spur of the moment, that today would be the day he tried taking the bus again. That he would sit down next to Ashleigh, if the other boy let him, and just…see what happened.

And now Ashleigh was absent, and the whole vibe was different, and there was a way in which Conor could feel that he could spiral about it, if he wanted to—his brain offering him the option to brood and fret and ruminate.

But it really was an option—was a gentle tug instead of a black hole. He’d sat down across from Timothy, instead, and everything still felt fine.

“New bag,” he said vaguely—

—and then a split second later noticed that Timothy was also wearing new clothes—that his uniform bore none of the rips and tears that had been accumulating since the first day of school.

Furtively, hoping to appear casual, Conor glanced toward the back of the bus—

“He isn’t fucking with me anymore, if that’s what you’re wondering.”

Conor turned back to see Timothy looking…braced, somehow, in a way that reminded Conor of how it felt to be caught in a conversation he could not avoid and did not want to have.

“Good,” he said, deliberately shifting his gaze toward the front, letting his eyes wander.

(If Timothy didn’t feel like talking, Conor wasn’t about to make him.)

They rode in silence for a minute or two, long enough for Conor’s thoughts to drift. He would see Ashleigh in first period, and Guyler in fifth—

(The other boy had not yet given back the little leatherbound journal—had kept it, writing in it off and on throughout math on Friday and slipping it into his binder when the bell rang.)

—it felt like a lot, but Sunday’s peace was still with him, and he felt ready, steady, whatever word meant the opposite of fragile—

Robitussin.

Shh.

There was so much that was out of his hands, and something about that had metabolized into peace rather than panic, a sort of stoic shrug.

It’ll turn out however it turns out.

And in the meantime, there were other places for Conor to aim his agency, other targets for his will-to-change. He had begun to plan another mission, had only the central movement pinned down and was hoping to spend the fifteen-minute break between second and third periods assessing a possible escape route—

“It was Devon,” Timothy said, breaking his reverie, the younger boy’s voice low and reluctant.

“Hmm?”

“I got Devon to help me with Eichardt.”

Again Conor felt the potential for a spiral, like an option box in a video game—would you like to freak out about this?

“How?” he asked mildly.

“I did him some favors.”

“No, I meant, like…how did he get Eichardt to back off.”

Although also, what favors, that sounds…ominous.

Timothy shrugged, some small measure of wariness draining from his posture as Conor remained more-or-less nonchalant. “He did like a meditation thing—”

“Mediation?”

“Sure. Sat us both down and made us list our, whadyacallit, our grievances. Thought he was going to make us say sorry to each other or something, but at the end of it he just asked how big our beef was and what it would take to make it go away.”

“How big your—”

“Beef, yeah. Like, how mad were we, really.”

Conor’s head tilted. “How mad were you?”

Timothy’s cheeks flushed. “Uh. About two hundred dollars’ worth?”

Conor blinked.

“I…see,” he said.

(Which he sort of did, the words Timothy was saying technically made sense in that order, but—)

“Is that where the new bag came from?” he asked.

“And the shirt, yeah.”

Conor bit his lip. “So you owe Devon, what—like eight favors, now?”

(That didn’t sound good.)

“No, at thirty apiece that’d only be six and two-thirds. And he said he’d spot me the two-thirds because, uh, that shit was better than the worse half of Die Hard. So it was only six.” Timothy swallowed. “Uh. And also it was actually only two because if I paid him back for the whole two hundred then he wouldn’t’ve actually bought the beef.”

“Bought the beef.”

The younger boy squirmed, but didn’t break eye contact. “Yeah. Um. He said it was, uh. His beef, now. Bought and paid for.”

(That definitely didn’t sound good.)

“What favors, Timothy?” He couldn’t quite keep an edge of tension from creeping into his tone. I said I’d look after you…

Well, you didn’t.

“Nothing. Weird shit.”

“Like what, Timothy?”

“Like spill my tray in the lunch line.”

Huh?

Timothy mumbled something under his breath.

“What was that?”

The younger boy sighed. “And carry a bag and hide it somewhere, okay? But don’t flip out, I’m not stupid. I looked. He said I could look. It wasn’t drugs and it wasn’t guns.”

“What was it?”

“Secret.”

“Timothy—”

“What?” the younger boy shot back, defiance poking out through the embarrassment. “What? You going to sit there and tell me that a two-minute fetch quest and a ten-second distraction weren’t worth a clean uniform and getting that fucking gorilla off my back?”

You INVITED the fucking gorilla—

“A distraction from what, though?” Conor asked.

“I don’t care.”

“Seriously?”

Timothy crossed his arms. “He’s not a bad guy.”

“We don’t know that.”

“Well, he doesn’t have to prove that he’s not a bad guy.”

“Timothy, come on, you can’t tell me you don’t hear what this sounds like—”

“It doesn’t matter what it sounds like, it matters what it is.”

Conor felt a chill run down his spine.

“That’s a quote, isn’t it?” he asked grimly. “That’s something Devon said to you.”

Timothy said nothing—just glowered, his expression mutinous.

“Timothy—”

“Shut up,” the younger boy snapped. “You’re not the boss of me. I didn’t have to tell you any of this. It’s none of your business.”

Conor opened his mouth—

“I’m not kidding. Don’t fuck with me.”

Conor’s mouth shut with a click, his emotions swirling, worry and frustration and—if he was honest—a tiny prickle of fear. Not fear fear, it wasn’t like Timothy would do anything bad, probably, not to Conor…

But.

Slowly, he held up his hands. “Okay, okay,” he said. “I’m not the boss of you. But—”

The younger boy stiffened, eyes full of warning, and Conor raised his hands another inch. “Can I just say one thing?” he asked.

A dangerous stillness, like a coiled snake.

“Just…look, rule number one, okay? …please.”

There was graffiti spray-painted across forty feet of lockers, and a bored-sounding teacher on repeat:

“We’ll be repainting this afternoon; for now just ignore it. We’ll be repainting this afternoon; for now just ignore it. We’ll be repainting this afternoon…”

With the crush of students filling the hall, and people opening their lockers left and right, Conor couldn’t make out what the vandal had written. The bit on his own locker door looked like it might be a cursive i, or maybe a very loose interpretation of a dick. He stashed his bookbag, grabbing the stuff he needed for second and third periods and pausing for a moment to tuck the mask away into his pocket before heading for the bathroom.

Someone had shit in the urinal, and for good measure pissed on the floor; Conor breathed through his mouth while he waited for a stall and flushed with his foot, touching nothing. There were sounds coming from the next stall over that mostly made him think Seriously? and he wondered idly whether they were planning to be late or actually intended to wrap things up in the next two and a half minutes.

Back out in the hallway, he almost tripped over a ruined textbook, dodging a second and then giving up and just walking over the ripped-out pages of a third (since everyone else already was). Apparently someone had had their stuff thrown to the floor right before the stampede; either they hadn’t been there to see it in the first place, or they’d simply given up and walked away.

Conor half-closed his eyes, shutting out as much of the chaos as he could while still being able to navigate. It wasn’t that things were that much worse than usual, really, but it was all somehow louder, harder-hitting, less ignorable after a quiet Sunday with his parents.

He made it to the door of Ms. Palmano’s classroom with about a minute and a half to spare and found himself oddly torn—eager to escape into the relative calm but less ready for his planned encounter than he had been.

You can just not, if you need to. He can wait one more day.

But Conor didn’t actually want to postpone. It was nice to remind himself that he could—to take a little bit of the pressure off—but although his composure was wearing thin, the core intention still felt right.

It was time.

Conor stepped through the door.

The room was still only half-settled, most of the other students wandering around, chatting. Ms. Palmano was seated at her desk, rifling through papers, not yet ready to call them to order. There was nothing written on the board.

Ashleigh was sitting in his usual seat, talking to no one, his eyes unfocused. His desk was further in than Conor’s, his back to the door, and so he couldn’t see as Conor approached.

You’ve got maybe fifty seconds, just FYI.

Conor swallowed, his throat suddenly dry. He hadn’t planned anything—hadn’t felt right planning anything—had sort of assumed he would just…know what to say, in the moment. But he was four steps away, now, and he still had nothing.

He paused, hovering behind Ashleigh’s shoulder, and his eyes fell on Ms. Palmano. She was looking straight at him, her gaze steady, her face giving nothing away.

…little help?

For once, she didn’t read his mind—or if she did, her answer was simply to remain still, silent, watchful.

Forty seconds.

Conor took in a deep breath, through his nose—

Ashleigh turned, looking over his shoulder at the soft sound.

Their eyes met.

Conor opened his mouth—

“I’m sorry,” Ashleigh said, the words low and soft but quick, filling the space before Conor could say whatever it was he might have said. “I—Conor, I’m really, really sorry.”

He looked as though there was more—as though he had a thousand words lined up and was holding them back, waiting to see how Conor would respond, and it suddenly occurred to Conor that for once in his life, Ashleigh might have actually thought ahead—might have spent some part of the past two and a half weeks imagining this moment, and what he would say if Conor ever gave him the chance.

“I know,” Conor said quietly.

(Because he couldn’t quite bring himself to say It’s fine.)

Ashleigh swallowed, his eyes searching, hesitant. A long moment passed, their silence an island in the bustle and chatter of the classroom around them, and some part of Conor noticed that he was slowing, actually, slowing back down again. That the Sunday stillness—

(Which had been muddied by the conversation with Timothy, and then polluted still further by the chaos of the hallway.)

—had returned, a little. That he was feeling more grounded, not less, the longer he looked into the other boy’s eyes.

Yeah, almost like I’m your best friend, or something.

“If you don’t ever want to talk to me again, I’ll—”

“I don’t,” Conor interrupted. “Never want to talk to you again, I mean.”

Visible relief washed over the other boy’s face, followed by a sort of tightening as he sucked in a breath, seemed to muster his resolve. “I know I—I want to try to, to, I don’t know, make it up to you, somehow, I’m sorry, I don’t know how, I know that just saying sorry doesn’t—”

“Ash.”

The other boy’s jaw snapped shut, and that same slow, circumspect part of Conor noted that this was different, this new power that he suddenly had—the way the other boy was twitching like a half-trained dog on a leash.

(He didn’t like it. It didn’t feel right—didn’t feel like Ashleigh.)

Yeah, imagine how he feels about it.

It seemed to be Conor’s turn, so he cast about for something useful to say.

…unfortunately, there wasn’t much.

“I’m still mad,” he warned.

“I know,” the other boy replied breathlessly. “It—I know.”

Another silence. Conor again found himself thinking about the two and a half weeks that had passed since Halloween, found himself wondering—for the first time—what that stretch of time had been like, for Ashleigh. Conor had had Holt, and Timothy, and Ms. Palmano, and his parents…

He hung you out to dry for like a month.

But that didn’t matter. Or rather, it mattered, but it didn’t matter when it came to the question of whether or not Ashleigh had suffered, nor whether Conor cared. It wasn’t like one hurt canceled out the other, wasn’t like Conor wanted Ashleigh’s life to be worse just because Conor’s had been rough.

He did this to himself.

Yeah—and he seemed to have realized that, since the last time they’d talked.

Since you threw me out of your house, you mean.

“Do—” the real Ashleigh began, and then broke off, clearing his throat and starting over. “D’you want to, you know. Talk about it?”

Conor considered, shook his head. “Not yet.”

The other boy seemed to deflate just a little, but he nodded.

“But I wanted to say hi,” Conor added. “So, uh. Hi.”

Again a wash of relief, the tension in the other boy’s face halving, leaving him looking nearly like himself again. “Hi, Conor.”

They looked at one another for another long moment, and Conor began to feel a pull in the other direction—a sense that they had done enough, for this first reunion, and it was time to let the dust settle.

“Well,” he said, holding out a fist. “See ya.”

The other boy bumped it. “Thank you,” he whispered.

Conor was the first to break eye contact, turning away just as some other part of his brain kicked back into gear and whispered hey, that felt like a whole lot longer than forty sec—

“To your seats, kiddos,” came Ms. Palmano’s voice, slicing through the chatter. “We’ve got a lot to do this period, time to get started.”

Conor looked at his teacher—

—at the clock on the wall, which read 8:28—

—back at his teacher.

She winked.

Little help, indeed.

Conor walked over to his seat, drew a pencil and some paper out of his binder, and took another deep breath. Seven periods left in the day, not counting lunch and homeroom.

Maybe he would talk to Ashleigh again, in Activity.

The little leatherbound journal was waiting on his desk when he entered fifth period. Guyler was in his usual seat, looking toward the board at the front of the room in a way that felt a lot more like not-looking-in-Conor’s-direction than usual.

Conor picked up the book, considering.

Maybe…

Maybe he should hold off, actually. Focus on the lecture, see if there was any chance he might salvage his crashing grade. The twin encounters with the two Falhanners had taken a lot out of him, and Jennifer’s unanswered text was itching like a shirt tag, and whatever Guyler had written was unlikely to make any of that better…

He slipped the book into his binder.

Fifty minutes and—thankfully—only two public humiliations later, and he was weaving his way through the crowd toward the cafeteria, hopping over the broken glass from where someone had shattered one of the windows flanking the doors to the high school math wing.

(There was no blood that Conor could see, so it probably hadn’t been like that one time, when Chris Hubert shoved Zack Jones’s head through the security glass.)

Conor had eaten lunch with Holt and Eddie every day for the past week, and it now felt a little strange to be headed for the sparsely populated area by the silent lunch tables—like putting on a jacket for the first time since the previous winter, the fit unfamiliar and slightly off. But his new and fragile calm had been put through the wringer already, had not been improved by Mrs. Atkins’s interrogations, and he didn’t feel like finding out whether it could withstand a twenty-minute onslaught of Eddie being Eddie.

Setting his tray down on the table, he opened up the little leatherbound journal and slid it just underneath, so that the pages were held open. His own question, at the top, had fit into a single line:

What was that about?

Guyler’s answer filled the rest of the page and half of the next, the jagged handwriting triggering an entirely unconscious flinch. But Conor steeled himself, forced himself to bend over and begin reading.

I should just throw this fucking thing away. What are you gonna do about it, really.

…man, fuck you, and fuck your mom, too.

Conor leaned back in his chair, feeling his thoughts trying to race and holding them back, letting them through only one at a time.

No insults.

There weren’t any insults.

Well, okay—there were the fuck yous and the general aura of sneering disdain. But there was nothing—nothing targeted, nothing vicious. By their usual standard, it was downright civil. Guyler had simply…answered the question.

(Had answered it in detail. Had gone back to add detail—from the look of the handwriting, the way the lettering in each new clump of words was slightly different, the other boy hadn’t written them all at once, but had returned to the page over and over, adding postscripts one at a time across hours or days.)

Conor picked up his sandwich, took an absentminded bite.

That…

…meant something.

It meant something, that Guyler had come back to the little book, again and again. That he’d—that he had—

(Conor’s eyes fluttered closed.)

Wanted me to understand.

That he hadn’t been satisfied with his first answer. Had imagined Conor reading it, and imagined Conor’s reply, and preempted it, and still not been satisfied.

That meant something.

Sure. What, though?

But it wasn’t what he had been expecting.

(He felt his brow furrow, his lips tightening into a frown.)

Had he been expecting something?

…he didn’t know that, either. But he hadn’t been expecting this. He’d written the question on autopilot, on instinct, following some wordless intuition that a face-to-face conversation was unlikely to go well, but—

(His frown deepened.)

…but the book where he’s been tearing you a new one for weeks might do the trick?

Well. It had.

He looked up, sweeping his gaze across the cafeteria, his eyes finding the other boy. Guyler was facing away from him, eating alone as usual. For a moment, Conor felt a strange impulse to stand up, and go sit beside him…

Wait, seriously?

Conor chewed at the inside of his cheek. He didn’t like this—was unused to being this uncertain about his own actions, his own motivations. He pulled his eyes away from the other boy, sent them drifting, as if they might magically find answers elsewhere in the crowd. There was Eichardt, hooting and laughing with a cluster of goons. There were Holt and Eddie, at their usual table, Holt singing loudly into an imaginary microphone. There was that anomalous high schooler again, Owen or Oliver or whatever his name was—

It might have just been his brain, desperate for something to latch on to.

Oliver.

Bullied.

(His eyes shifted all on their own, settling on Guyler once again.)

…and Guyler, too.

He looked back down at the open book on the table, the cutting, angry letters. I’m not going to connect the dots for you—you can figure this one out.

(It was odd—he didn’t feel curious, had no discernible desire to be chasing down a mystery. It felt more like something that was happening to him—a reflex, a compulsion—like there was a sliver of food stuck between his teeth and he couldn’t stop his tongue from worrying it.)

Well, they’ve all gotten the shit kicked out of them recently.

Lonely?

Oliver. Timothy. Guyler Stevens.

And Mrs. Falhanner.

And Ashleigh.

(The feel of a snowball, rolling downhill, gathering mass and momentum.)

Oliver. Oliver had made him think of Timothy and Guyler, and then Guyler had made him think of Mrs. Falhanner, and Mrs. Falhanner had made him think of Ashleigh…

(His brain offered up a memory, a replay of his reunion with Ashleigh—his suspicion that the other boy had actually thought through what he would say in advance, maybe, in a rare and conspicuous break from his usual M.O.—)

And then it clicked.

Conor’s eyes opened, locking once more onto Guyler, who had been sitting on the fringes of his Tae Kwon Do classes since September, doing pushups the whole time. Who had been taking Tae Kwon Do classes for years—for longer, in fact, than Conor had known him, longer than the lifetime of their entire feud.

(He could feel the next thought coming, and it was silly, kindergarten, obvious—enough that his cheeks were already burning—but he went ahead and thought it anyway, let the words be heard inside his head.)

Guyler Stevens is real.

He was real, a real person, he existed even when Conor wasn’t looking, things were happening to him even when Conor wasn’t there.

(Awful things, sometimes.)

And Oliver—Conor knew, in an abstract sort of way, that high schoolers got bullied, too, had occasionally caught a glimpse of some fight in the hallways or on the bus. But from his perspective, they had always been randos—one-offs, anonymous nobodies, faces he’d never seen before and would never see again (or at least, faces he had no reason to remember).

But Oliver—

Conor was suddenly realizing that the Oliver in his head—

(Who was no more the real Oliver than his shoulder Ashleigh was the real Ashleigh.)

—the Oliver in his head lived in the cafeteria. Had only ever been visible there, was a timeless fixture like the giant bulldog painted on the back wall.

But the swirly. The swirly had broken the illusion—was the sort of thing that (obviously) had happened somewhere else. Because of course the real Oliver went other places, did other things—might be any sort of person at all, and was some sort of person, in truth. Had a whole, specific life that Conor knew absolutely nothing about—a life which had led him to sit all by himself next to middle schoolers on silent lunch for the past two and a half months.

(Obvious in retrospect, but now it was visceral, the realization hitting him in the parts that didn’t think in words. Like the difference between knowing he was capable of the monkey jump, and having actually done it.)

He looked down at Guyler’s answer again.

My sabumnim found out I got into a fight on purpose.

Guyler had been following them, on the last day of summer. Had followed them into the tree—had been looking to start something, and taken actual steps to make it happen. Was an agent, like Ms. Palmano talked about—someone with preferences, and plans to enact those preferences.

And then it had all gone wrong for him, not just the bruises and the stitches and the concussion but a whole other problem that Conor had never suspected, never imagined—and Guyler was doing something about that, too, was not just rolling over and letting it happen. Was doing a surprising thing. A strange thing.

A serious thing.

(It was the only thing I could think of, okay?)

Conor swallowed, the epiphany rolling over him, the dominoes falling—had to force himself to not start thinking about Holt, and Eddie, and Jennifer, and Devon, and Ms. Palmano, and his parents—fuck, my parents—his father was out there right now, somewhere, living and breathing and thinking and feeling things—

And then he could not help but imagine it, the picture unfolding in his mind without permission—the moment, two and a half weeks ago, when he had screamed at Ashleigh to get out, get out, get out of my house. Three times he had said it, and Ashleigh had gone—gone out the front door and started walking, what had that walk been like, what had that week been like, Conor couldn’t begin to imagine the inner lives of the five hundred people currently in earshot but he knew Ashleigh—

It was a greater test of his fragile serenity than anything else, a hard thing to contain without unraveling. But he did contain it—and more, he did so without flinching, turned fully toward it, letting himself feel it.

(It hurt.)

But that was okay, because he had made it through alive, Ashleigh had made it through alive, the flip side of the revelation that there were things out there that could destroy you was the fact that most of the time, they didn’t.

I’m so sorry, Ashleigh.

And he was. Not in a way that erased anything. Not in a way that made it his fault. But he was sorry, and that felt…good, and right, and clean in a way that hadn’t quite been true of that first rendezvous, in English. He would have to tell the other boy—in Activity, maybe, or maybe it was finally time to go back to Ashleigh’s house.

But first there was Guyler, the other other boy, whose raw emotion was splattered across the pages of their little book like blood. There was something there that didn’t feel right to leave unanswered, an open loop that would gnaw at him until he closed it. It didn’t need to be now, necessarily, but it did need to be next.

(Or so said his brain, anyway. He couldn’t have said why, if Ms. Palmano had asked him to explain. But he couldn’t say why for a lot of things. He’d never really been able to say why for Binder’s Gap, and he hadn’t let that stop him.)

(The lower rail was to stop toddlers from falling through, Conor figured, although that didn’t quite make sense—it seemed to him like a toddler would have no problem falling through anyway, if they really wanted to.)

Her face was practically all he could see, now—they had talked, had held hands, had worked on projects together, but they hadn’t ever been RIGHT IN FRONT OF EACH OTHER like this before—even when they had hugged, half an hour ago, at the start of the date, their heads had gone off to the side. The last time Conor’s face had been this close to someone else’s was…

(Flinch.)

“Do you—” he began, and then broke off.

“What?” she asked.

Her eyes were so big.

“Am I supposed to ask what I really want to know?” he said, his voice barely above a whisper.

She nodded. “Yep,” she said, her own voice utterly sure, without a hint of doubt. “That’s…kind of what I’ve been trying to get you to do this whole time. Seems like maybe that’s…hard for you?”

Conor swallowed, nodded. “Does it have to be about you? I mean, like—does it have to be about why you’re cool?”

She shook her head. “Nope. So long as we come back to that, eventually.”

He opened his mouth. “Do you—”

She squeezed his hands encouragingly.

It’s one of those things you can’t really half-ass—

“Do you want me to kiss you?”

“Yep,” she said, and leaned forward.

I won’t be able to hear you from down there, and we probably shouldn’t be shouting, anyway.

Conor stared at the clock app on his phone, the seconds ticking down.

But if you really can do it, like if you really can just go—

Fifty seconds left. Now forty-five. Now forty…

Then yeah, I’ll be ready. I’ll just…keep taking pictures, that whole minute.

Conor took a deep breath. On the horizon, the sun was half-set, the last rays of orange sending shadows straight sideways, making the auditorium rooftop seem wild and alien.

Thirty. Twenty-five…

He had done the climb faster than ever before, half to beat the oncoming darkness, half because he had wanted Jennifer to see, to be impressed. Then he’d waited, catching his breath as she walked around the building, as the appointed minute approached—

Ten. Nine. Eight…

Conor broke forward, a light jog—was farther back than usual, had not stepped forward from the pipe. Something in him had wanted to do it without looking, do the whole thing without looking—wanted to simply go, without prepping, trusting his body, his memory, and so he ran, his feet falling as always into the left of the twin paths, his path. At the halfway mark he sped up, and with a dozen paces to go, he sped up again—

Air.

He threw his arms wide, threw them back, drew his knees up under him, reveling in the moment, the vast expanse of time, it felt like he had left the other rooftop ten full seconds ago and he still had ten seconds left, the gravel was rushing up at him but slowly, so slowly, Conor had all the time in the world to bring his feet forward, move his hands into position—

Crash.

It was always, always jarring, and this time no less than the others, but it was familiar now, a pattern he knew inside and out, all of the pieces fitting him like well-worn shoes. He slammed into the other rooftop like a meteor, rolled, and found his feet, jogging toward the exit, the path back earthward. He would be on the ground in less than a minute, would be back over with Jennifer in less than two—

She held up her phone as he approached, the picture already loaded up. The two buildings loomed like the walls of a canyon, the fading sky clear and cloudless between. And there, in the middle—a dark silhouette, suspended like a fly in amber—

“Do you like it?”

Conor nodded.

“Do you really like it?”

He couldn’t speak, couldn’t even bring himself to nod again. It was one thing to be inside of it up there—to make the pilgrimage, complete the ritual, shed to awning to roof to tree to pipe, slowly leaving the world behind, ascending into the heavens. But to see it down here—to have the moment transposed, transported, a shard of sky at ground level, captured, preserved, real, indisputably real—

Conor was overcome.

Jennifer leaned in, wrapped her arms around him, whispered in his ear. “Then that’s the one I’m keeping.”

It happened the next day, and in the end, it almost seemed like an accident.

The mask was still in his pocket—not because he had planned for it to be, but because he had lingered in the cafeteria for an extra minute, trying (and failing) to finish a response to Guyler, and had been racing to get to his locker in time.

His hands were empty—again not for any particularly inevitable reason, but simply because his next period was Activity. Had he been headed to Art, he would have still been carrying the journal, his binder, his pencils.

And he was already running—not at full speed, not an all-out sprint, but enough to try to make up some time, to get to the bathrooms in the electives building with enough seconds to spare that he wouldn’t be late to class.

And the fact that the five minutes between classes were more than half over, so that the breezeways were mostly clear—and the fact that Conor had just so happened to have scoped out that exact spot earlier in the week—and the fact that he was in this exact mood when he saw what was happening, and was still far enough away to slip on the mask, moving quickly enough that even if someone was looking at his face they probably wouldn’t be able to pick him out of a lineup—

It wasn’t a decision. It was just…the way that Conor was, in that moment. Like water rolling downhill, like a shark following a trail of blood. Instinct, reflex.

Fate.

There were two of them—eighth graders, probably, by the look of them, and two victims that were Timothy’s size. The bullies held the younger kids’ bookbags in their hands, were laughing as they pulled out item after item, tossing them onto the sidewalk as everyone else skirted around, eyes down and hoping to be overlooked.

And the bullies’ own bookbags were just lying there, on the ground behind their feet—unguarded, forgotten, because what was there to fear? Who would dare?

Conor—

(The Tracer.)

—streaked into the space like a striking falcon, scooped up a bag in each hand without slowing, without missing a step. He was twenty feet away before the bullies even turned, had all the time in the world to swing the bags up, one-two, onto the breezeway awning.

And then they were coming, were yelling, outraged, murderous, but that was fine, there was plenty of time, Conor even paused for a moment to make sure he had the correct foot in front before taking the three quick steps toward the wall. One step against the brick, a rebound into the corner where his second foot pressed firmly into the windowsill, and then up to catch the lip of the roof, eleven feet off the ground.

There was still a moment when they might have caught him, his legs dangling within reach, eleven feet minus the length of his body, his outstretched arms.

But he had done a thousand topovers on Mr. Baker’s wall by that point, was able to go from hanging to standing in scarcely more than a second. The bullies’ fingers scrabbled impotently at the brick, their victims forgotten, as Conor calmly turned and stepped over onto the aluminum awning, scooping up the bags once more and making his way at a trot toward the office. He dropped them over the side at the front of the building, right at the glass double doors of the main entrance, and then he turned and jogged across the rooftops to the nearest known safe drop point.

Less than a minute, all told, and he was just another anonymous straggler running through the halls, his haste unsuspicious, explaining itself.

The coaches didn’t even notice he was late.
