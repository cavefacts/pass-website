Timothy had a new bookbag, and Ashleigh wasn’t there.

“He’s been biking,” the younger boy explained, from across the aisle.

It was Monday, the start of the last full week of school before Thanksgiving. The weather was turning properly cold, now, and most of the leaves that were going to fall had fallen. Conor had spent the day before with his parents—the third Sunday in a row—and some part of him begrudgingly acknowledged that it was doing something. He felt—

Uncaffeinated?

—smoothed out, somehow, everything a little less staticky. Maybe it was just the sheer number of minutes in which nothing in particular happened, some part of his brain that had come to believe that parents are for meddling slowly unlearning. They’d been reading by the fireplace, and he had gotten up to make hot chocolate for himself, and had been slightly surprised to discover that he’d made three cups without thinking, and somehow not surprised that his mother and father received them and drank them without a word, all of their appreciation conveyed in warm smiles and gentle nods. He’d gone to bed at eight—not out of exhaustion or fatigue, but simply because he’d felt sleepy, and ready for the day to be done.

He'd woken up an hour before his alarm, padded softly around the house, eaten his frozen Eggos in the silence of the empty kitchen and decided, spur of the moment, that today would be the day he tried taking the bus again. That he would sit down next to Ashleigh, if the other boy let him, and just…

See what happened.

And now Ashleigh was absent, and the whole vibe was different, and there was a way in which Conor could feel that he could spiral about it, if he wanted to—feel his brain offering him the option to brood and ruminate.

But it really was an option—was a gentle tug, not a black hole. He’d sat down across from Timothy, instead, and everything still felt fine.

“New bag,” he said vaguely—

—and then a split second later noticed that Timothy was also wearing new clothes—that there was no sign of the accumulated abuse that had been building up since the first day of school.

Furtively, hoping to appear casual, Conor glanced toward the back of the bus, looking for Eichardt…

“He isn’t fucking with me anymore, if that’s what you were wondering.”

Conor turned back to see Timothy looking…braced, somehow, in a way that reminded Conor of how it felt to be caught in a conversation he could not avoid and did not want to have.

“Good,” he said, deliberately shifting his gaze toward the front, letting his eyes wander.

(If Timothy didn’t feel like talking, Conor wasn’t about to make him.)

They rode in silence for a minute or two, long enough for Conor’s thoughts to wander. He would see Ashleigh in first period, and Guyler in fifth—

(The other boy had not yet given back the little leatherbound journal—had kept it, writing in it off and on throughout math on Friday and slipping it into his binder when the bell rang.)

—it felt like a lot, but Sunday’s peace was still with him, and he felt ready, steady, whatever word meant the opposite of fragile.

Robitussin.

Shut up, Ashleigh.

“It was Devon,” Timothy said, breaking his reverie, the younger boy’s voice reluctant.

“Hmm?”

“I got Devon to help me with Eichardt.”

Again Conor felt the potential for a spiral, like an option box in a video game—would you like to freak out about this?

“How?” he asked mildly.

“I did him some favors.”

“No, I meant, like…how did he get Eichardt to back off.”

Although also, what favors, that sounds…ominous.

“Sure. Sat us both down and made us list our, whadyacallit, our grievances. Thought he was going to make us say sorry to each other or something, but at the end of it he just asked how big our beef was and what it would take to make it go away.”

“How big your—”

Conor’s head tilted. “How mad were you?”

Timothy’s cheeks flushed a little. “Uh. About two hundred dollars’ worth?”

Conor blinked.

“I…see,” he said.

(Which he sort of did, the words Timothy was saying made sense in that order, but—)

Would you like to freak out about this?

(That didn’t sound good.)

And also it was actually only two because if I paid him back for the whole two hundred then he wouldn’t’ve actually bought the beef.”

But.

The younger boy stiffened, his eyes full of warning, and Conor raised his hands another inch. “Can I just say one thing?” he asked.

A dangerous stillness, like a coiled snake.

“Just…rule number one, okay? …please.”

There was graffiti spray-painted across forty feet of lockers, and a bored-sounding teacher on repeat:

“We’ll be repainting the lockers this afternoon; for now just ignore it. We’ll be repainting the lockers this afternoon; for now just ignore it. We’ll be repainting the lockers this afternoon…”

With the crush of students filling the hall, and people opening their own lockers left and right, Conor couldn’t make out what the vandal had actually written. The bit on his own locker door looked like it might be a cursive i, or maybe a very loose interpretation of a dick. He stashed his bookbag, grabbing the stuff he needed for second and third period and pausing for a moment to tuck the mask away into his pocket before heading for the bathroom.

Someone had shit in the urinal, and for good measure pissed on the floor; Conor breathed through his nose while he waited for a stall and flushed with his foot, touching nothing. There were sounds coming from the next stall over that mostly made him think seriously? and he wondered idly whether they were planning to be late or actually intended to wrap things up in the next two and a half minutes.

Back out in the hallway, he almost tripped over a ruined textbook, dodging a second and then giving up and just walking over the ripped-out pages of a third (since everyone else already was). Apparently someone had had their stuff thrown to the floor right before the stampede; either they hadn’t been there to see it in the first place, or they’d simply given up and walked away.

Conor half-closed his eyes, shutting out as much of the chaos as he could while still being able to navigate. It wasn’t that things were that much worse than usual, really, but it was all somehow louder, harder-hitting, less ignorable after a quiet Sunday with his parents.

He made it to the door of Ms. Palmano’s classroom with about a minute and a half to spare and found himself oddly torn—eager to escape into the relative calm but less ready for his planned encounter than he had been.

You can just not, if you need to. He can wait one more day.

But Conor didn’t actually want to postpone. It was nice to remind himself that he could—to take a little bit of the pressure off—but although his composure was wearing thin, the core intention still felt right.

It was time.

Conor stepped through the door.

The room was still only half-settled, the other students wandering around, chatting. Ms. Palmano was seated at her desk, ruffling through papers, not yet ready to call them to order. There was nothing written on the board.

Ashleigh was sitting in his usual seat, talking to no one, his eyes unfocused. His desk was further in than Conor’s, and so he couldn’t see the other boy as he approached.

You’ve got maybe fifty seconds, just FYI.

Conor swallowed, his throat suddenly dry. He hadn’t planned anything—hadn’t felt right planning anything—had sort of assumed he would know what to say, in the moment. But he was four steps away, now, and he still had nothing.

He paused, hovering behind Ashleigh’s shoulder, and his eyes fell on Ms. Palmano. She was looking straight at him, her gaze steady, her face giving nothing away.

…little help?

This time, she didn’t hear his thought—or if she did, her answer was to remain motionless, passive, watchful.

Forty seconds?

Conor took in a deep breath, through his nose—

Ashleigh turned, looking over his shoulder at the soft sound.

Conor opened his mouth—

“I’m sorry,” Ashleigh said, the words low and soft but quick, filling the space before Conor could say whatever it was he might have said. “I—Conor, I’m really, really sorry.”

He looked as though there was more—as though he had a thousand words lined up and was holding them back, waiting to see how Conor would respond, and it suddenly occurred to Conor that for once in his life, Ashleigh might have actually thought ahead—might have spent some of the past two and a half weeks imagining this moment, and what he would say, if Conor ever gave him the chance.

“I know,” Conor said quietly, because he couldn’t quite bring himself to say It’s fine.

Ashleigh swallowed, his eyes boring into Conor’s, searching, hesitant. A long moment passed, their silence an island in the bustle and chatter of the classroom around them, and some part of Conor noticed that he was slowing, that the Sunday stillness—

(Which had been muddied by the conversation with Timothy, and then polluted still further by the chaos of the hallway.)

—had returned, a little. That he was feeling more grounded, not less, the longer he looked into the other boy’s eyes.

Almost like he’s my best friend or something.

“If you don’t ever want to talk to me again, I’ll—”

“I don’t,” Conor interrupted. “Never want to talk to you again, I mean.”

Visible relief washed over the other boy’s face, followed by a sort of tightening as he took a breath, seemed to muster his resolve. “I know I—I want to try to, to, I don’t know, make it up to you, somehow, I’m sorry, I don’t know how, I know that just saying sorry doesn’t—”

“Ash.”

The other boy’s jaw clicked shut, and that same slow, circumspect part of Conor noted that this was different, this power that he suddenly had—the way that Ashleigh was twitching like a half-trained dog on a leash.

He didn’t like it. It didn’t feel right—didn’t feel like Ashleigh.

Yeah, imagine how he feels about it.

It seemed to be Conor’s turn, so he cast about for something useful to say.

Unfortunately, there wasn’t much.

“I’m still mad,” he warned.

“I know,” the other boy replied breathlessly. “It—I know.”

Another silence. Conor again found himself thinking about the two and a half weeks that had passed, found himself wondering—for the first time—what those two and a half weeks had been like, for Ashleigh. Conor had had Holt, and Timothy, and Ms. Palmano, and his parents…

He hung you out to dry for like a month.

But that didn’t matter. Or rather, it mattered, but it didn’t matter when it came to the question of whether or not Ashleigh had suffered, nor whether Conor cared. It wasn’t like one hurt canceled out the other, wasn’t like Conor wanted Ashleigh’s life to be worse just because Conor’s had been rough. Even if it was all ultimately Ashleigh’s fault…

He did this to himself.

Yeah—and he seemed to have realized that, at some point between Halloween and now.

“Do—” Ashleigh began, and then broke off, clearing his throat and starting over. “D’you want to, you know. Talk about it?”

Conor considered, shook his head. “Not yet.”

The other boy seemed to deflate just a little, but he nodded.

“But I wanted to say hi,” Conor added. “So, uh. Hi.”

Again a wash of relief, the tension in the other boy’s face halving. “Hi, Conor.”

The two of them looked at each other for another long moment, and Conor began to feel a pull in the other direction—a sense that they had done enough, for this first reunion, and it was time to let the dust settle.

“Well—see ya,” he said, holding out a fist.

The other boy bumped it. “Thank you,” he whispered.

Conor was the first to break eye contact, turning away just as some other part of his brain kicked back into gear and whispered hey, that felt like a whole lot longer than fifty sec—

“To your seats, kiddos,” came Ms. Palmano’s voice, slicing through the chatter. “We’ve got a lot to do this period, time to get started.”

Conor looked at his teacher—

—at the clock on the wall, which read 8:28—

—back at his teacher.

She winked.

Little help, indeed.

Conor walked over to his seat, drew a pencil and some paper out of his binder, and took another deep breath. Seven periods left in the day, not counting lunch and homeroom.

Maybe he would talk to Ashleigh again, in Activity.

The little leatherbound journal was waiting on Conor’s desk in fifth period. Guyler was in his usual seat, looking toward the front of the room in a way that felt a lot more like not-looking-in-Conor’s-direction than usual.

Conor picked up the book, considering.

Maybe…

Maybe he should hold off, actually. Focus on the lecture, see if there was any chance he might salvage his crashing grade. The twin encounters with Timothy and Ashleigh had taken a lot out of him, and whatever Guyler had written was unlikely to make things better…

He slipped the book into his binder.

Fifty minutes and—thankfully—only two public humiliations later, and he was weaving his way through the halls toward the cafeteria, hopping over the broken glass from where someone had shattered one of the windows flanking the doors to the high school math wing.

(There was no blood that Conor could see, so it probably hadn’t been like that one time, when Chris Hubert shoved Tyrone Jones’s head through the security glass.)

Conor had eaten lunch with the other boys every day for the past week, and it now felt a little strange to be headed for the sparsely populated area by the silent lunch tables—like putting on a jacket for the first time since the previous winter, the fit unfamiliar and slightly off.

But his new and fragile calm had been put through the wringer already, had not been improved by Mrs. Atkins’s interrogations, and he didn’t feel like finding out whether it could withstand a twenty-minute onslaught of Eddie being Eddie.

Setting his tray down on the table, he opened up the little leatherbound journal and slid it just underneath, so that the pages were held open. His own question, at the top, had fit into a single line:

What was that about?

Guyler’s answer filled the rest of the page and half of the next, the jagged handwriting triggering an entirely unconscious flinch. But Conor steeled himself, forced himself to bend over and begin reading.

Fine, fuck you. The way it works is, at my academy, if you’re late, you do ten pushups. If you’re goofing off, you do ten pushups. If you break the rules or show disrespect, you do ten pushups. I’m not going to connect the dots for you—you can figure this one out.

It was the only thing I could think of, okay?

Don’t fucking tell anybody. That should be obvious but I’m spelling it out.

I’ve been going there since fifth grade. Tell Ashleigh to kill himself.

Seriously, don’t tell.

I should just throw this fucking thing away. What are you gonna do about it, really.

…man, fuck you, and fuck your mom, too.

Conor leaned back in his chair, feeling his thoughts trying to race and holding them back, letting them through only one at a time.

No insults.

There weren’t any insults.

Well, okay—there were the fuck yous and the by-now-familiar aura of sneering disdain. But there was nothing targeted, nothing vicious. By their usual standard, it was downright civil. Guyler had simply…answered the question.

(Had answered it in detail—had gone back to add detail. From the look of the handwriting, the way the lettering in each new clump of words was slightly different, the other boy hadn’t written them all at once, but had returned to the page over and over, adding postscripts one at a time across hours or days.)

Conor picked up his sandwich, took an absentminded bite.

That…

It meant something, that Guyler had come back to the little book, again and again. That he had—that he had—

(Conor’s eyes fluttered closed.)

Wanted me to understand.

That he hadn’t been satisfied with his first answer. Had imagined Conor reading it, and imagined Conor’s reply, and pre-empted it, and still not been satisfied.

That meant something.

Sure. What, though?

But it wasn’t what he had been expecting.

Conor didn’t know that, either. He’d written his question on autopilot, on instinct, following a wordless intuition that a face-to-face conversation was unlikely to go well, but that...

He frowned.

That the book where he’s been tearing you a new one for weeks might do the trick?

…well, it had.

He looked up, sweeping his gaze across the cafeteria, his eyes finding the other boy. Guyler was facing away from him, and he, too, was eating alone. For a moment, Conor felt a strange impulse to stand up, and go sit beside him…

He pulled his eyes away from the other boy, sent them drifting as if there might somehow magically be answers elsewhere in the crowd. There was Holt and the other boys, at their usual table. There was Eichardt, hooting and laughing with a cluster of goons. There was the anomalous high schooler again, Owen or Oliver or whatever his name was—

Conor paused.

It might have just been his brain, desperate for something to latch on to.

But the boy—

Oliver.

—Oliver was wet.

Not obviously. Not soaked. The high schooler had done his best to hide it, had probably used up half a ton of paper towels drying off. But his hair was just a little bit damp, and there was an uncomfortably familiar semicircle of moisture still darkening the neck and shoulders of his sweatshirt.

(Conor had never been swirlied, but he’d seen the aftermath before.)

And now that he looked closer, the older boy’s eyes were rimmed with red, and his nose had a pale sort of look that made Conor think of nosebleeds…

…and Timothy.

Bullied.

Oliver was being bullied. Or had been bullied, anyway, at least once.

At least twice. You saw them knock him over right here in the cafeteria, remember?

Conor frowned. There was something there—something tickling, tantalizing, tip-of-the-tongue, something about Oliver and Timothy—

(His eyes shifted all on their own, settling on Guyler once again.)

His frown deepened.

Three boys that had all had the shit kicked out of them, except that wasn’t it, there was no—no recognition, no satisfying click, whatever it was that his brain was trying to clue him into it wasn’t about getting beaten up, or at least not only that…

(He closed his eyes again.)

Something about Oliver, and Timothy, and Guyler…

And Mrs. Falhanner.

(His frown deepened, but he let the thought breathe, let his mind run with it.)

Mrs. Falhanner, and—and Ashleigh—

But Ashleigh hasn’t been bullied.

Right, because it wasn’t about that, Conor still wasn’t quite sure what he was seeing but it had the feel of a snowball rolling downhill, growing larger, picking up speed. He remembered his reunion with Ashleigh, in Ms. Palmano’s class, the suspicion that the other boy had thought through what he would say, in a conspicuous break from his usual M.O.—

And then it clicked.

Conor’s eyes opened, locking once more onto Guyler—who had been sitting on the fringes of his Tae Kwon Do classes since September, doing pushups. Who had been taking Tae Kwon Do classes for years—for longer, in fact, than Conor had known him, longer than their whole history together.

Guyler Stevens was real.

He was real, a real person, he existed even when Conor wasn’t looking, things were happening to him even when Conor wasn’t there.

(Awful things, sometimes.)

And Oliver—Conor knew, in an abstract sort of way, that high schoolers got bullied, too, had occasionally witnessed the aftermath of some fight in the hallways or on the bus. But those had always been randos, from his perspective—one-offs, anonymous nobodies, faces he had never seen before and would never see again (or at least, faces he didn’t remember, didn’t bother to store).

But Oliver—

Conor was suddenly realizing that the Oliver in his head—

(Who was no more the real Oliver than his shoulder Ashleigh was the real Ashleigh.)

—the Oliver in his head lived in the cafeteria. Was only ever visible there, was a timeless fixture like the giant bulldog painted on the back wall. But the swirly—the swirly had happened somewhere else, the real Oliver went other places, did other things—might be any sort of person at all, and was some sort of person, in truth. Had a whole, specific life that Conor knew absolutely nothing about—a life which had led him to sit all by himself next to middle schoolers on silent lunch for the past two and a half months, and which today had included a swirly.

Conor looked back down at the jagged letters carved into the pages of the little black book.

My sabumnim found out I got into a fight on purpose.

Guyler had been following them, on the last day of summer. Had been looking to start something, and taken actual steps, had some kind of actual plan. And then it had all gone wrong for him, not just the bruises and the stitches and the concussion but a whole other problem that Conor had never suspected, never imagined—and Guyler was doing something about that, too. A surprising thing. A strange thing.

A serious thing.

(It was the only thing I could think of, okay?)

It was a lot, this snowball, and as it rolled over Conor he had to force himself to not start thinking about Holt, and Eddie, and Jennifer, and Devon, and Ms. Palmano, and the teachers sitting over at the staff table, and his parents—fuck, my parents—his parents were out there right now, living and breathing and thinking and feeling things—

And then he could not help but imagine it, the picture unfolding in his mind without permission—the moment, two and a half weeks ago, when he had screamed at Ashleigh to get out, get out, get out of his house, three times he had said it and Ashleigh had gone, gone out the front door and started walking, what had that walk been like, what had that week been like, Conor couldn’t begin to imagine the inner lives of the five hundred people currently in earshot but he knew Ashleigh—

It was a greater test of his fragile serenity than anything that had come before, was a hard thing to contain without unraveling. But he did contain it—and (somehow) he did so without flinching, without denial. He let himself feel it, and it hurt, and also it was okay, he had made it through alive, Ashleigh had made it through alive, the flip side of the revelation that there were things out there that could destroy you was the fact that most of the time, they didn’t.

I’m so sorry, Ashleigh.

And it was true. Not in a way that erased anything. Not in a way that was Conor’s fault. But he was sorry, and that felt…good, and right, and clean in a way that hadn’t quite been true of that first rendezvous, in English. He would have to tell the other boy—in Activity, maybe, or maybe it was finally time to turn his phone back on.

But first.

First there was Guyler, the other other boy, whose raw emotion was spattered across the pages of the little book like blood. There was something there that didn’t feel right to leave unanswered, an open loop that would gnaw at Conor until he closed it. It didn’t need to be soon, exactly, but it did need to be next.

(Or so said his brain, anyway. He couldn’t have said why, if Ms. Palmano had asked him to explain. But he couldn’t say why for a lot of things. He’d never really been able to say why for Binder’s Gap, and he hadn’t let that stop him.)

Taking a sip of his chocolate milk, Conor bent over the open book and began to write.

“You didn’t bring your little book with you.”

So thoroughly did this sentence demolish Conor’s thoughts that it took him multiple seconds to recover things as basic as where he was, or who it was who’d spoken.

(Jennifer, and it was third period, Science, the following morning. They had finished presenting their Mars projects the day before, and Conor had walked into the room to find the chairs arranged like an amphitheater, centered on a TV cart.)

“Sorry,” Jennifer said, as she slid into the chair next to him. “Didn’t mean to startle you.”

(He’d been thinking about something, surely. But whatever it was, it was gone, now, unrecoverable.)

They were at the back of the room, the rest of their classmates milling around, fighting over seats while at the front of the room their teacher was thumbing through a stack of DVDs.

“Uh,” said Conor, his brain still less than halfway back online. “What book?”

“The little black one,” she said—

(Because of course that was the one she meant; if she’d meant some other book it wouldn’t have rearranged his brain in the first place.)

“—I’ve been meaning to ask you about it for a while, now, actually. Is it a journal? A sketchbook?”

“It’s—”

There was something like a traffic jam in Conor’s mind, as half of him tried (and failed) to find the words to explain, and the other half scrambled to keep him from saying anything at all.

“—private,” he said, and then winced, because that just made it sound—

“Mysterious,” Jennifer murmured, fixing him with a smile. “Especially since I’m pretty sure I saw Guyler Stevens with it in Social Studies yesterday. And also last week.”

Conor wasn’t quite sure what was happening to his face, but whatever it was, it turned his classmate’s smile into a gentle laugh. “Relax!” she said, her eyes twinkling. “It’s cool, you can be mysterious. I like mysteries.”

She leaned back, making a little bit of extra room between them so she could reach out and nudge him on the shoulder with her fist.

What’s so fu—

Jennifer Eisley was talking to him.

Talking talking. Not like did you do your part of the project talking, but actually talking.

What do I—I mean, why is she—

They had gotten an A.

That’s seriously where your mind goes right now?

Jennifer Eisley is talking to me.

It happened three hours later, and in the end, it almost seemed like an accident.

The mask was still in his pocket—not because he had planned for it to be, but because he had lingered in the cafeteria for an extra minute, trying (and failing) to finish a response to Guyler, and had been racing to get to his locker in time.

His hands were empty—again not for any particularly inevitable reason, but simply because his next period was Activity. Had he been headed to Art, he would have still been carrying the journal, his binder, his pencils.

And he was already running—not at full speed, not an all-out sprint, but enough to try to make up some time, to get to the bathrooms in the electives building with enough seconds to spare that he wouldn’t be late to class.

And the fact that the five minutes between classes were more than half over, so that the breezeways were mostly clear—and the fact that Conor had just so happened to scope out that exact spot during morning break—and the fact that he was in this exact mood when he saw what was happening, and was still far enough away to slip on the mask, moving quickly enough that even if someone was looking at his face they probably wouldn’t be able to pick him out of a lineup—

It wasn’t a decision. It was just…the way that Conor was, in that moment. Like water rolling downhill, like a shark following a trail of blood. Instinct, reflex.

Fate.

There were two of them—eighth graders, probably, by the look of them, and two victims that were Timothy’s size. The bullies held the younger kids’ bookbags in their hands, were laughing as they pulled out item after item, tossing them onto the sidewalk as everyone else skirted around, eyes down and hoping to be overlooked.

And the bullies’ own bookbags were just lying there, on the ground behind their feet—unguarded, forgotten, because what was there to fear? Who would dare?

Conor—

(The Tracer.)

—streaked into the space like a striking falcon, scooped up a bag in each hand without slowing, without missing a step. He was twenty feet away before the bullies even turned, had all the time in the world to swing the bags up, one-two, onto the breezeway awning.

And then they were coming, were yelling, outraged, murderous, but that was fine, there was plenty of time, Conor even paused for a moment to make sure he had the correct foot in front before taking the three quick steps toward the wall. One step against the brick, a rebound into the corner where his second foot pressed firmly into the window sill, and then up to catch the lip of the roof, eleven feet off the ground.

There was still a moment when they might have caught him, his legs dangling within reach, eleven feet minus the length of his body, his outstretched arms.

But he had done a thousand topovers on Mr. Baker’s wall by that point, was able to go from hanging to standing in scarcely more than a second. The bullies’ fingers scrabbled impotently at the brick, their victims forgotten, as Conor calmly turned and stepped over onto the aluminum awning, scooping up the bags once more and making his way at a trot toward the office. He dropped them over the side at the front of the building, right at the glass double doors of the main entrance, and then he turned and jogged across the rooftops to the nearest known safe drop point.

Less than a minute, all told, and he was just another anonymous straggler running through the halls, his haste unsuspicious, explaining itself.

The coaches didn’t even notice he was late.
