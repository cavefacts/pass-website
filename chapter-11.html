It turned out there weren’t all that many ways to be a superhero.

To successfully be one—to actually, reliably, effectively intervene. For a brief and glorious moment, Conor had done the thing, been doing the thing—had been in it, felt it, lived it, the fulfillment of a lifetime of idle wishes and almost six months of focused preparation. It had been like breaking the Gap, only better—bolder, purer, cleaner. He had been right all along:

This is what life should be like.

(Not like Halloween.)

The buzz had stayed with him all the way through to the following week, when he had gotten off the bus on Monday morning, the mask in his pocket, full of fresh hope and determination…

…only to have it all drain away over the course of the day.

The trick with the bags had been serendipitous, perfect. But opportunities like that were few and far between. Most of the time, the crime was already over by the time Conor came across the aftermath, the perpetrator long gone and usually anonymous. And when he did see things happening in real time, there was often no obvious way to stop them.

Well, I mean…

Okay, sure—he could almost always have stopped them by just… inserting himself into the middle of things, the way he and Ashleigh had on the first day of school, while Eichardt was taking his revenge on Timothy.

But for one thing, that would mostly just mean getting his face stomped in, and for another, it wasn’t exactly a recipe for staying under the radar.

Plus they’d just get right back to it, after.

Eichardt hadn’t left Timothy alone—not until Devon did whatever-it-was he’d done. There were some crimes of opportunity where delaying things, bringing down more attention, might mean that the whole thing just ended, and didn’t restart. But there were plenty where trying to intervene would do nothing more than briefly inconvenience the bad guys—if that.

What if what you really need is, I dunno, Tae Kwon Do? Or CPR? Or how to hold your breath for two minutes?

Ashleigh’s words, half-remembered from the rooftop. They’d already hit hard, at the time, and now a full two school days had passed since the incident with the bags, and Conor had saved zero additional people.

He hadn’t saved the boy he’d seen handing over his lunch money to a ninth grader during break, his eyes dead and empty as if the extortion had become routine.

He hadn’t stopped the eighth grader he’d glimpsed through the doors, breaking into a locker that clearly wasn’t his.

He hadn’t done anything about the two drug deals he had seen in the bathroom—wasn’t even sure he ought to do anything about them, or about the various acts of vandalism that mostly targeted school property, broken mirrors and graffitied bathroom stalls and wads of gum crammed into the spout of the water fountain.

And now it was Tuesday—the end of the day, Conor emptying his things from his locker into his bookbag, and a few feet away a trio of boys were mocking and harassing a girl, hooting and whistling and shouting the worst things, awful things, it was happening right out in the open where everyone could hear and nobody was doing anything about it—

And that was it. That was the crux of it. There were more good people than bad—there always were—but someone had to step in first, be the first person to say no, and then it would be three bullies against you. There was nothing in it for a bystander, no incentive for them to swap places with you in the meat grinder.

Which was why Conor had carried the mask in his pocket all week—to maintain his secret identity, keep himself safe from reprisals. But he hadn’t done anything to earn any reprisals, had not stumbled across any more chances like the one he’d had the previous week. He was simply too small, too weak, the playing field too level—

(And then the thought, the one he had been flinching away from, because he still had not fully processed what had happened after Ashleigh had kissed him: You could just go up onto the roof and jump on people.)

Conor had seen the boy he’d hurt, the would-be rapist, walking through the hallway with his arm in a sling, and had been overwhelmed by—

By—

—by something, he hadn’t had words for it, had not been able to break down what he felt into distinct physical sensations, the way his father had taught him. He’d started sweating, panting, had turned and run the other way and—and awakened, almost, it felt like—had come back to himself in a supply closet near the gym, wrapped in the smell of dust and musty leather with no memory of how he’d gotten there. It had taken him upwards of ten minutes to stop trembling, and he’d been given silent lunch for being late to third period, had sat just three chairs away from Oliver and been unable to miss that the other boy’s head and shoulders were wet again.

It was all too much, and there was a part of him that wanted to just give up, let go of the dream, find a new hobby—

—but there was something about that that felt like dying, and so Conor hovered, tortured, irresolute, in the space between what was and what seemed like—

(Maybe.)

(If he could only figure out how.)

Talk to Ashleigh.

It was the option his mind kept returning to, over and over again. Of all the people on Earth, Ashleigh was the only one who had the context. Who would understand the question that Conor was asking without needing ten thousand words of background, first.

But their newly reforged connection was too fresh, too fragile. They were still figuring out what it even meant to be friends, after everything that had happened, and Conor did not want to risk getting into a fight with the other boy. This was the sort of topic that tended to bring out the worst in Ashleigh, and Conor was not at all sure how he himself would react to that.

(The thought was quiet, almost embarrassed.)

…no.

No, that was—too soon might not even be the right way to say it, it wasn’t like Conor had any reason to believe he would ever have that sort of conversation with Guyler.

But still. Too soon. They had passed the journal back and forth another half-dozen times since Guyler’s confession, the conversation slow and halting, both boys wary as they felt their way forward in unfamiliar territory. So far, the fragile peace was holding, but it sat atop a long, bitter enmity, and it would be all too easy to fall back into those well-worn grooves.

Which meant that yet again, Conor was alone. Alone in essence, even though he was less alone than he had been since September—alone in the thing that sat at the front and center of his attention, the problem that filled his thoughts in the shower—

(When they weren’t filled with other things.)
