“Ever thought about bringing someone else up here?”

“Oh, you must be Conor! Please, come in.”

The woman stepped back, opening the wide door wider, waving over Conor’s shoulder at his mother still sitting in her car in the driveway. Under her arm, down the bright, wood-paneled hallway, Conor caught a glimpse of Jennifer, sitting on a high stool in what looked to be the kitchen.

He followed the woman—Jennifer’s mother, surely—across the threshold, feeling vaguely uncomfortable. The foyer was cavernous, cathedral, the late afternoon sunlight streaming in through shattered-glass windows that stretched all the way up to the second-floor ceiling. He shucked his shoes instinctively, nudging them up against the wall by the door.

(There was no shoe rack. There were no other shoes in sight.)

“Oh, no need to do that,” Jennifer’s mother protested. “Please, it’s fine either way.”

Conor left his shoes off.

The woman led the way down the hallway, her own feet whispering on the polished hardwood floor. “Look who’s here, Jen,” she announced, as they entered the kitchen together.

Jennifer was seated at a large kitchen island with a marbled countertop, math book already open, spare paper and sharpened pencils arranged neatly in front of a second stool that stood empty beside her. She smiled—faintly—warmly, though—cupcakes—

Cupcakes?

“Can I get you anything?” the woman asked, reaching into a glass-fronted cabinet as Conor pulled himself up onto the stool, the countertop cool beneath the skin of his palm. “Tea? Soda? Champagne?”

He choked.

“Mom.”

Jennifer rolled her eyes, the faint smile still ghosting around her lips. “Sorry,” she murmured. “She’s incorrigible.”

In…corridge…

“Uh,” Conor said aloud. “Water’s fine.”

“Ice? We have crushed and cubed.”

“Cubed, please.”

A tall glass appeared in front of him.

“Thank you, Mrs. Eisley.”

The woman laughed, sounding almost exactly like Jennifer. “It’s Mrs. Payne now, actually. But you can call me Evelyn. Eisley is Dan’s last name.”

Right. Stepdad.

“Thanks, Mrs. Payne.”

Conor’s unease had thickened, was nearing the point of spawning actual second thoughts. He looked down at the stack of paper—dazzlingly white under the bright overhead lights—and realized that he’d forgotten to wash his hands, his knuckles splotched with dust and soot.

The woman laughed again. “He’s cute,” she said, sending a sly glance at Jennifer.

Conor’s cheeks flushed—

“Duh,” Jennifer replied.

A strangled sound emerged from somewhere inside Conor’s throat.

“Mom’s going to hang around a bit while we work,” Jennifer explained. “To make sure I’m not flirting at you too hard.”

“Wouldn’t want you to be uncomfortable,” Mrs. Payne joked.

A second sound, this one shading toward death rattle, and now it was Jennifer’s turn to laugh. “Sorry,” she repeated. “Couldn’t resist.”

…you PLANNED this?

(Conor said nothing, picking up the glass and taking a long gulp to cover the silence.)

“Anyway,” his new tutor continued. “Chapter four, yeah?”

She poked one of the pencils with her finger, sending it rolling across the marble. It fell off the edge, and Conor caught it by pure reflex, the ice in his glass rattling as he lunged.

“Ah, okay, so you are still in there. Good—I was starting to worry.”

If the way they felt from the inside was any measure, Conor’s cheeks were now on fire. In the back of his head, Ashleigh was cackling.

“Okay, so. I have your last quiz here. It looks like you started having trouble around question two…”

Just run.

Conor bit his tongue, unclenched his fingers, and tried to focus.

They had been working for—no, scratch that, Jennifer had been working for forty-five minutes straight, as Conor’s body and mind slowly lost their fundamental structure and he melted further and further into the marble.

Jennifer shook her head. “It’s not hopeless,” she replied, matter-of-fact. “We just haven’t figured out what’s wrong, yet.”

“It’s all wrong,” Conor countered, the words Just let me die playing themselves over and over in his head.

He had been so excited at the prospect of Jennifer tutoring him that he had forgotten it would involve math—math done in front of her—math done so badly in front of her that she’d arrived at full-blown confusion within the first ten minutes.

(Which would have been bad enough all on its own, but her mother was only a dozen steps away, had never left, was sitting at the massive kitchen table with a magazine and a croissant and had heard every single word.)

All of that, and Jennifer kept being distractingly—

(He happened to glance over just as she chewed at her lip, the tip of her tongue just barely poking out—)

Down, boy.

You could go for the door, Ashleigh whispered. You’re closer than either of them. No way they’d catch you.

“Sure. Do you remember how?”

God dammit.

“No.”

“What’s the first step?”

Conor squinted down at the page. “I’m supposed to add ten to forty-one,” he said.

“Why?”

Just let me DIE.

“Because I’m…”

“…I’m supposed to do the opposite of whatever’s there. Like, to undo it. Ten is minused, so I’m supposed to plus it, instead.”

“Why?”

“I don’t know.”

Jennifer nodded. “Do you know what X is?” she asked.

“Seventeen.”

“No, that’s—sorry, I don’t mean ‘what’s the answer?’ I mean, like, what is X. Like how the math book is a book. What sort of thing is X?”

“A…varible.”

“Vari-a-ble.” Jennifer frowned. “Wait. Did you really not know that, or were you, like. Fooling around?”

“I mean. I knew ‘varible’ was wrong.”

“But the word ‘variable.’ You actually couldn’t remember it?”

It’s hard to remember gibberish.

“Sorry?” he said.

(Over at the table, Mrs. Payne was watching them intently, her eyes like heat lamps, making Conor sweat.)

“Okay, look,” Jennifer said. “I’ve got a question, and I want you to answer it honestly, okay?”

Conor swallowed. “Sure.”

“Have you been trying?”

He blinked. “Huh?”

“Like, have you actually been trying, over the past forty-five minutes.”

Conor opened his mouth—

Huh.

“I think…maybe not?” he said, feeling his cheeks burning again. “Or like. It’s sort of been, like…I don’t know…how?”

Look on the bright side, whispered Ashleigh. Better than having her think that you’re smart for like three months, and then finding out.

“How to try, you mean?”

He nodded.

Jennifer’s frown deepened. “Can you move that pencil with your mind?”

“What?”

“That pencil. Can you try to move it with your mind?”

“I—”

“Yes or no.”

“It’s not going to—”

“Duh. But can you try anyway?”

It was as if he had been split into two, and the two Conors inside of him were wrestling for the steering wheel. On the one hand was what, what the fuck, what are you even talking about, and on the other—

(The scent of bubble gum.)

“I can,” he heard himself say.

“So what’s different?”

“Huh?”

“What’s different between trying to move the pencil with your mind and trying to figure out pre-algebra?”

Conor blinked. “I…know how?”

“To move the pencil?”

“Yeah. Or like. I don’t know how, obviously. It’s not going to work. But I…know how to do the thing that won’t work?”

Jennifer nodded tightly, her brow furrowed, her eyes focused on something a million miles away.

“Right,” she said, and her voice, too, no longer seemed to be pointed at Conor. “You don’t know what X is.”

…seventeen?

She slid off of her stool and down to the polished floor, padding around the island and vanishing into the walk-in pantry. There were rustling sounds—

Now’s your chance!

—and then she emerged a moment later, holding three huge cellophane-wrapped Rice Krispies treats.

“Are those for the bake sale?” Conor’s mouth asked.

“Shh.”

She bent over another sheet of notebook paper, folding it and then running her fingernail down the crease before tearing it, making a neat little slip. Picking up a pencil, she scribbled something on it, then pushed the paper and the three treats over toward him.

“Buy those from me.”

“Huh?”

“Pretend we’re in a store, and I’m the cashier. Buy them from me. Use the coupon.”

He looked down at the slip of paper.

$10 off your purchase.

“I—”

“Hi, how can I help you?”

Conor felt his jaw drop.

May I gently suggest getting your shit together?

“I—I—”

Jesus Christ, dude.

“Can I buy these?” he blurted.

“Sure!”

There was an expectant silence.

“You have to give them to me, to scan,” Jennifer said, as if explaining that the sky was blue.

Conor slid the treats back over toward her, blushing furiously.

“Beep,” said Jennifer. “Beep. Beep.”

What.

“Do you have any coupons you’d like to use today?”

It took Conor an embarrassingly long time to get the tiny slip of paper off the counter, his fingers scrabbling uselessly at the edge.

“Thanks,” said Jennifer, as he handed it over. “That’ll be forty-one dollars.”

“What?”

“Forty-one dollars, please.”

She held out her hand, palm up.

Conor stared.

Jennifer coughed, quietly—hm HM.

Conor dug into his pocket, pulled out his wallet, hesitated.

“Imaginary money is fine,” Jennifer said, still using her cashier-voice.

Conor opened the wallet, feeling helpless, confused, what—

He mimed pulling something out of the wallet.

“Okay, pause,” Jennifer said, her voice suddenly back to normal. “How much was each treat?”

“What?”

“How much does a treat cost?”

“What?”

“Focus.”

“I…”

Jennifer leaned over slightly, her face filling his vision. “Conor,” she said, her voice now two steps lower and absent all levity. “Focus.”

Conor focused.

Forty-one dollars…

He looked down at the three treats on the counter, and the slip of paper beside them.

Seventeen, suggested Ashleigh. You already did this one twice.

But that wasn’t what she was asking him for. She wasn’t asking him for a number, she was asking him to…

“The coupon,” he said slowly.

Jennifer said nothing.

“They’d cost fifty-one dollars, without the coupon.”

Jennifer said nothing.

Fifty-one dollars, and there’s three of them…

He struggled for a moment, and then gave up, because the point was not to divide fifty-one by three in his head. The point was…

“X is the price.”

“Which you didn’t know,” Jennifer said.

Conor nodded.

“But it was something,” she added. “There’s a specific number that is the price. You just didn’t know what it was.”

And it clicked.

(Or something clicked, anyway.)

Conor turned to look at the paper lying in the center of the island, covered in blood-red annotations. Jennifer had dragged him through six of the eight problems he’d originally gotten wrong, and the seventh…

(5z – 5)/2 = 15

“There’s some number,” he began.

(Again Jennifer was silent.)

“There’s some number where…if…if you multiply it by five, and then…and then you take away five, and then you divide that by two…”

(It was tenuous, fragile, the tower of thought wobbling.)

“…then it’s fifteen.”

Jennifer nodded—once, a swift, spare motion. “And if it’s fifteen after you divided by two, then…?”

There was sweat in new places, now—on his upper lip, on the small of his back. “Then it was thirty before,” he hazarded.

Another nod, and another silence.

“So…”

(Conor swallowed.)

(In the back of his head, the little copy of Ashleigh was saying things, but he wasn’t hearing them.)

“So five times Z, minus five, equals…”

He waited, but Jennifer gave him nothing—just watched, her eyes like chips of jasper.

“…thirty.”

Keep going.

He took a deep breath—

“Try writing that down.”

“Huh?”

“You don’t have to do it all in your head.”

“Oh. Right.”

He wrote down 5z – 5 = 30 and squinted at it. The next step was…

He wrote down 5z = 35.

“Why?” Jennifer asked.

“Because if five-Z minus five is thirty, then five-Z not minus five is, um. Five more than that, which is thirty-five.”

“Okay,” she said, and fell silent.

(The sweat on his back was cooling.)

If five of something was thirty-five, then one of it…

“Seven,” he said.

(His voice was not steady. It wasn’t exactly wobbly, but it wasn’t steady, either.)

“Is that right?” Jennifer asked.

How should I—

Conor squashed the thought. She was asking him, which meant he could know. There was a way forward, here, somewhere; he just had to find it…

There’s some number where…

“If I times five by seven,” he began, and then paused.

(Again, she gave him nothing.)

“If I times five by seven, and then minus five, and then divide by two, it should equal fifteen.”

More silence.

“If it does equal fifteen, then I did it right.”

So what are you waiting for?

He scribbled for a dozen seconds.

“It’s right.”

Jennifer smiled, and it was like diving into cold water on a hot summer day.

“Okay,” she said. “Now let’s go back to number two again.”

Conor took a deep breath.

“Hey, Ash, hold up a second?”

It was the end of Activity, very nearly the end of the day. Their class had been dismissed to the showers, the rest of the seventh graders streaming on ahead of them.

(It wasn’t great, in terms of timing. But the bus was too crowded, and going over to Ashleigh’s house after school felt too heavy, and Jennifer was in English, so that was out, too. It was either this or texting, and texting meant he wouldn’t be able to see the other boy’s face, and calibrate.)

Ashleigh paused, turned, looked back, his expression open and curious.

“There’s something I need to—”

Conor broke off.

God dammit.

(He was sick of this—sick of being tongue-tied, of his brain getting in the way of itself. For once, he wanted to just say what he had to say, without getting tangled up in predictions about how it would land, what he should say instead, how that would go wrong, too…)

“What you said,” Conor began again, forming the words a few at a time. “In my room. About us having—about me having, like. A separate life.”

Something changed behind Ashleigh’s eyes, but it was subtle, and Conor didn’t know what it meant, so he carried on.

“I just…I wanted to tell you. Like, actually tell you. That, um. Jennifer. I know you already…I mean, I know you saw her holding—you saw us. Holding hands. In English.”

(They had still had only the one real conversation so far, since the day after Halloween. The rest of their interactions had been cautious, tentative, low stakes—not the balsa-wood fakeness of October, but a silent, mutual effort to avoid putting too much weight on the newly-set bones. Now, though, Conor had the sense that this was a test the two of them could not postpone—that even if it might have been better in some abstract sense to give the other boy more time, the act of holding it back, holding it in, would be lethal—would be predeciding the outcome.)

If you have to leave half of yourself behind in order to preserve a friendship…

“And like. You didn’t say anything. And I know you probably—I know you maybe don’t—don’t want to hear this, or—or it’ll, I dunno, make you sad or mad or j—or whatever.”

Ashleigh’s expression was no longer open and curious, was now guarded and wary and braced for pain in a way that made Conor’s heart ache. But there was no way out except through.

“But like. I don’t want you to, um. Not be a part of it. Of my life, I mean. I don’t want it to be—to come between us.”

Come between me and your MOM.

But the real Ashleigh wasn’t joking, wasn’t laughing—was standing very, very still, as though terrified of what might happen if he moved.

“And—anyway, we also, um. We kissed. I mean, just once, but. She’s tutoring me in math. And like. I dunno, it seems like it’s probably…”

He trailed off, watching as his stilted, halting words landed, as the other boy’s face shifted, twisted—

Please.

“Thanks,” Ashleigh said finally, his voice like glass. “For telling me.”

There was a short silence. Conor’s brain was revving—felt like it was overheating, even, as he tried to drink in every detail of the other boy’s tone, his expression, his body language—

Not enough.

“Can I have a hug?” he asked.

(He could see it, in Ashleigh’s eyes—the wild, animal instinct, the urge to run, to hide, to disappear. It was a feeling he knew intimately, recognized immediately—)

“Please?” he added, before his friend could say no.

(It wasn’t fair. But Conor wasn’t trying to be fair. He was trying to win. Not against Ashleigh, exactly, but against a part of him—the stupid part, the obstinate part, the one that had beaten up Guyler and refused to come to the window and tried for months to drive Conor away. That part was still in the other boy, had not actually gone away, and Conor’s please was like a hook, a trap, catching him at the last possible second, a single heartbeat before he would have worked himself up to escape velocity.)

Ashleigh gnawed at his lip, screwed up his face, refused to meet Conor’s eyes. But he turned out his palms, allowed Conor to step forward and drag him into an embrace.

They stood there for a long time. Twenty seconds, maybe thirty. There was no melting, no sudden breakdown. Ashleigh’s body was stiff, and it stayed stiff, his muscles tense and trembling. It was awkward, and weird, and unpleasant.

But he stayed, until Conor let him go.

Conor was floating—like a leaf on a pond, not like a cloud. That morning, there had been a bus ride with Ashleigh (fine), ten minutes of math review with Jennifer during break (great), two moments in Social Studies when he’d actually known the answer Ms. Nease called on him to give, and a journal entry that had come back from Guyler with zero corrections of any kind. What?

Guyler came in, and he seemed normal, gave Conor no unusual attention. There was the ordinary chatter from the other kids as they streamed in from the hall, maybe a bit more excited than usual, the way they sounded whenever there was some particularly juicy piece of gossip—
