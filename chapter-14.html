Chapter 14 — Spiral

⚜

August 31st, 6:34PM

“…you’re not really brave at all, are you? You’re just usually not scared.”

•••

There were cameras in the hallways when they came back from break, and the students of East Binder Secondary School went to war.

(Slowly, at first—at first, there was just ordinary surprise, ordinary gossip, the collective shrug that was the usual response whenever the grownups did something cruel or capricious. It wasn’t that they took the massive intrusion in stride, exactly, so much as that there didn’t seem to be anything to do about it.)

But then someone ripped the camera outside of the cafeteria from its mount, leaving it smashed on the ground where more than half the school saw it as second lunch ended and third lunch began. And when Conor went to the electives building for Art at the end of the day, the camera just inside the double doors was mangled as if it had been hit with a sledgehammer—was dangling by twisted wires, its lens shattered.

By Wednesday, both had been replaced, and rumor had it that Mr. Sykes had suspended at least one high schooler in connection with “the incident”—

(As it was referred to over the intercom, during morning announcements.)

—but on Thursday morning, they arrived to find police cars waiting in the front circle, and every single camera across the entire campus destroyed or removed—thirty-four of them, in total. Some had been torn down, others carefully unscrewed from their mounts. Some had had their lenses broken, wires cut, casings cracked. A few on the high school side had been melted, or so everyone was saying, and a number had been vandalized, spray-painted, the tapes removed and the insides filled with caulk, with foam, with soda and shaving cream.

(“Look,” said Ashleigh. “Don’t take this the wrong way. But it wasn’t you, right?”)

(“I was going to ask if it was you.”)

It was all anyone could talk about for the rest of the day. “I looked it up,” Holt said grimly, at lunch. “Those things cost like $700 each, even with like educational discounts or whatever. That’s like twenty grand they lit on fire last night.”

They, because it clearly had not been a single person, could not possibly have been a single person—seemed like it couldn’t have been done with thirty people, since surely the buildings had locks, had alarms, wouldn’t the police have come once the very first camera went offline—

“I mean, that part’s not that crazy, really,” Jennifer had said, in the hallway after Science. “I don’t think they had anyone watching at night, do you? Probably they were just recording.”

Aren’t there remote security services, though? Conor had wondered. The sort where some rando in a call center a thousand miles away was watching a hundred screens from twenty different locations, and would call the cops if anything happened at any of them…

(Wondered, but not said aloud, because there had been more pressing matters to attend to, with only a minute or so to spare before he had to head to Social Studies and she to Math. The two of them had grown bolder, since Christmas—were sometimes willing to draw leers and whistles in the hallway, and rarely said more than a handful of words during their brief hallway rendezvous.)

Besides, it wasn’t like East Binder was flush with cash. Conor supposed it wouldn’t have been that weird for Mr. Sykes to have started off with the discount package…

…started off being the operative term, because over the weekend the cameras were replaced, the new ones now surrounded by metal cages of eighth-inch wire mesh.

“Mistake,” Ashleigh had murmured, as the two of them walked to homeroom on Monday morning, and Conor had privately agreed. Simply replacing the cameras would have been boring, indifferent—would have signaled indifference, robbed the rebellion of its power.

(No sword so sharp it can’t be dulled by apathy, as Ms. Palmano had once quipped.)

But the cages were a clear escalation. Were an admission, a challenge, the sort of defiance that everyone besides Timothy learned not to try in their very first week. They were proof that the vandals had got under the grownups’ skin, and now there was blood in the water.

It was hard for Conor to put his finger on what exactly he was picking up on, as he walked through the hallways, through the bathrooms, caught snippets of chatter in the courtyard and the cafeteria. It was a vibe, more than anything else. Something sharper-than-usual in the air—an extra note of resentment, the feeling of cut-off sentences and elbows held in check.

And don’t forget the uniforms.

All through the autumn, the Central students had quietly boycotted the East Binder uniforms, stubborn splashes of color in a sea of blue and gold. There had been an official grace period during the first two weeks, in which they had been supposed to be buying their sweatshirts and khakis, but the third week had come and gone and almost no one had switched over, and by then there was inertia. There had been a couple of suspensions, but they hadn’t produced any lasting effects—

And Mr. Sykes was pretty busy back then, anyway.

That had been at the peak of the Eagle-Bulldog war, and the administration had apparently had bigger fish to fry. But now, in January, the defiance was suddenly dissolving, the out-of-place colors growing rarer and rarer as if word was slowly spreading. Part of it seemed to be simple solidarity, a symbolic drawing-together in the face of a shared enemy. But part of it—

(“Lot harder to figure out who busted up a camera when they’re wearing the same shit as everybody else,” Eddie had pointed out, serious for once.)

Mr. Sykes was scary and competent, but most of the threat lay in his ability to focus power. He could throw you in detention, call your parents, kick you out of school—but only if he knew who you were.

They couldn’t suspend everybody.

And everybody—it seemed—had noticed. Had come together, gotten on board, gotten on the same page. It was impossible that no one knew who had destroyed all the cameras overnight, and yet no one was talking.

And then—

—slowly—

—the cameras began breaking again.

Not all at once, this time. Not in a dramatic show of defiance.

But there were wads of paper stuffed into the wire cage of the one outside the cafeteria when Conor walked past it during break, Mr. Sykes standing there looking stern as the janitor stood on a ladder, cleaning it out. And—as Conor would learn later—at that very moment, two different students were simultaneously shoving pool cues through the cages of the cameras at either end of the high school math wing, shattering their lenses. They had covered their faces, had simply dropped the pool cues to the ground when they were done, had blended back in with the crowd and gone on to the next class without anyone ratting.

A day later, there was plexiglass fused to the front of every cage.

And by the end of the day after that, something like a quarter of the plexiglass shields had been ruined in one way or another—had been painted or sharpie’d or scratched to opacity.

It wasn’t that the grownups weren’t trying—there were teachers in the hallway during every class change, now. But the students were trying, too, and if there was one thing the denizens of East Binder knew how to do, it was create situations that absolutely demanded the full attention of any nearby responsible adult. With more than thirty kids per teacher, there simply weren’t enough eyes to go around.

(“Should we be doing something?” Conor wondered aloud. “Like, trying to stop this?”)

(“Should we be helping?” Ashleigh countered.)

It was a good question. It wasn’t like anyone wanted East Binder to be worse than it already was—

Well, that’s probably not true, actually, pointed out Conor’s shoulder Jennifer.

Okay, fair. It wasn’t like most of the students wouldn’t have welcomed some relief from the chaos, some increased sense of order and safety.

But there seemed to be near-universal agreement that the cameras were not about that. Were not going to fix anything—were for the grownups, not the kids—would at most mildly inconvenience the bad guys, shove everything two inches further under the rug.

(It wasn’t like there were cameras in the bathrooms, after all.)

And—as Ms. Palmano pointed out, in a lecture that was barely even pretending to be about something else—it wasn’t the case that you could bully people into good behavior. For a little while, maybe, but not in the long run. There were always loopholes, blindspots—ways to appear compliant while continuing to do whatever you wanted. Rules only worked if people believed that following them was better than not—that cooperation and kindness would pay off more, in the end.

And that…

…wasn’t true.

That just wasn’t true, at East Binder.

Bullying, and cheating, and theft, and vandalism—they all just worked. Were proven, viable strategies. There were plenty of people who didn’t, who wouldn’t, who refused—

(Like Jennifer, like Holt.)

—but those people held back for their own reasons, not because they couldn’t get away with it. Getting away with it was trivially easy, would only be a tiny bit harder with cameras in the halls, and so the whole thing was just a headache, a nuisance, a sad little joke. Like having to take off your shoes and dump out your water at the airport—it solved nothing, and only made things that much more unpleasant for all the normal people who were keeping their heads down and just trying to get through the day.

“Still, though,” Conor had pressed. “We should at least think about it, right?”

And Ashleigh had agreed, or agreed to humor him at least, and so the two of them had scooped up Timothy for a strategy meeting one Saturday morning, only for the younger boy to cheerfully confess that he had destroyed two cameras himself, and helped out with distractions in six other cases.

“By the way,” Timothy had added, clearly enjoying Conor’s stricken look, “they do have offsite security guards on payroll. They don’t come if you’re just on the roof or whatever, but they show up in about six minutes if you open a door or break a window.”

(Were those new, some part of Conor had wondered, or had that first round of cameras somehow been destroyed in a way that didn’t trip any alarms?)

Further interrogation—requiring no blood, thank god—had revealed that yes, Timothy had used the masks, duh, obviously, what kind of a question was that, it would’ve been stupid not to use them—

—at which point Conor, panicking, had tried to pull the plug on the whole thing, only for the younger boy to shrug and say all right, then, I can probably scrounge up two or three but a lot of them are gone.

“Gone?” Conor had spluttered.

Again the younger boy was unashamed, unembarrassed, had looked him straight in the eye without flinching. “I gave away seven,” he said calmly. “Four of them to one person, who definitely gave away the extras, don’t ask me to who because I don’t know on purpose. And probably most of the ones we hid are still there, but the one behind the drainpipe by the teacher’s lounge is definitely gone, and I kinda don’t think it was a teacher who found it.”

It was at that point that Conor’s brain had fully seized up, had overextended itself trying to track all of the implications, the possibilities, everything that you would expect to go wrong with ten or fifteen masks floating around on top of everything else. He had spent upwards of thirty seconds frozen, glitching, utterly unable to think, or speak, or even see—

(At least, insofar as “seeing” implied something like actually processing the light his eyes were absorbing. It was the visual equivalent of what would have happened if everyone around him suddenly started speaking Japanese—it wasn’t that his eyes stopped working so much as that his brain was unable to do anything at all with what they were sending it.)

He’d come back to earth at the sound of the front door slamming, had shaken himself awake to find that Timothy was gone and he and Ashleigh were alone in the living room.

“I sent him away so you wouldn’t, uh, kill him,” Ashleigh had said quietly. “But also, I think he’s right.”

And then Ashleigh, who was Conor’s best friend in the entire world—

(But who was also insane, and reckless, and did not know how things worked.)

—Ashleigh had gone on to explain that, in his view, the role of hero did not belong to them, was not theirs to control, that the other students had every bit as much of a right to try to break free as the three of them did, and that the camera war was proof—

(“If December wasn’t enough already.”)

—that they couldn’t do everything that needed to be done, all by themselves. That there were simply too many problems, that they couldn’t count on being where they needed to be at the exact moment they needed to be there, that the opportunities for heroism were too scattered and the three of them spread too thin to make any real difference.

(Which had hit uncomfortably hard, uncomfortably close, was an echo of the thoughts that Conor himself had already been having, but the answer to all of that wasn’t this, surely, could not possibly be this—)

“Why not?” Ashleigh had argued, reading the expression on Conor’s face. “I mean, isn’t this better? Like, setting aside the mask thing—and it’s not like they’re even our masks—I mean, they are, they’re yours, but like, you get it, right, they aren’t Tracer, they aren’t Siphon. So like, setting that aside. If Timothy hadn’t stolen them, if he’d just come up with it himself, or like bought a bunch of Halloween masks, or whatever. Isn’t this, I dunno. More awesome?”

And whether because Conor’s brain was still only half online—

—or because he had been spending more and more time around people like Jennifer and Guyler and his parents—

—or just because he still wasn’t over it, not all the way, there was shit that had happened in the past four months that Ashleigh should still feel bad about, he should be more—more fucking demure, or something, that probably wasn’t the right word but seriously—

For whatever reason, it was at that point that Conor had fixed the other boy with a look, as if he could pin him to the wall with the force of sheer disapproval, and had said, with all the cold fury he could muster:

“You’re just looking for a fucking distraction.”

There had been a heavy silence, at the end of which Ashleigh had given a sad little shrug.

“I mean, yeah,” he said softly. “But that doesn’t mean I’m wrong.”

And then Conor had had to hold himself very, very still, because the feeling that washed over him was betrayal—and on the heels of the betrayal, confusion—and the conversation had fizzled until the pair of them awkwardly moved on to other things, lunch and Nerf guns and the rusty trampoline in Ashleigh’s backyard.

It wasn’t until later—much later, in bed that night—that Conor had been able to unravel the tangle, figure out that he’d felt betrayed because Ashleigh had forgotten, because it was like he didn’t care, after they had talked through everything on the rooftop at Rosemary Conor had thought that they were together in something, that they had both seen, both agreed—

This isn’t a game.

It wasn’t a game, it was real, there were real consequences, you couldn’t actually run around on rooftops trying to stop bullies without—without colliding, smashing into things, into people, sooner or later there was bound to be some kind of—of—of incident, and people were fragile, breakable—

(The sound of collarbones cracking, a scream that went on and on and on and on.)

—even if you took it seriously, genuinely did your best to prepare, things could still go sideways in an instant and Ashleigh and Timothy weren’t taking it seriously.

Somebody was going to get hurt.

Somebody was going to get hurt.

Somebody could die, even—

Really?

It was a quiet thought, almost timid, but it pulled him up short, brought the runaway train of his thoughts to a halt once more.

I mean, do you really actually think somebody’s going to DIE? Is that, I dunno. An exaggeration.

It was Ashleigh’s voice, his shoulder Ashleigh, but for once it wasn’t flippant, wasn’t joking, seemed to be honestly asking the question.

(Conor took several deep breaths.)

He looked up at the glow-in-the-dark stars on the ceiling, trying his best to think.

Okay.

Okay, no. Probably no one was going to die.

But somebody could, somebody really actually could, and even if they didn’t that didn’t make it all fine, didn’t make it safe, they were playing with fire, all of them, and ending up in an ambulance was better than ending up in a grave but it still wasn’t good, look at what had happened to Guyler—

Slow down.

He slowed. Breathed. Shouldered aside the clamor and tried to make space inside of his head. And into that space…

If somebody ends up in an ambulance, that’s not your fault.

What? Of course it was his fault—would be his fault—he was the one who had started all this, those were his masks, they had been his missions, he was the reason Timothy had—

Jesus Christ, dude.

Again he forced himself to slow.

If somebody ended up in an ambulance—

Then it’s not your fault.

(Slow.)

It…would be, though?

Wouldn’t it?

What do you think Ms. Palmano would have to say about that?

He frowned.

What would his teacher have to say, about whose fault it was, if the other students went off and did things that were dangerous, that were their own choices, and ended up getting hurt…

He shook his head. There was something wrong with that, something sneaky—

I don’t trust her, Jennifer had said. It’s Trojan horses all the way down.

But that wasn’t fair. The real Ms. Palmano shouldn’t be on the hook for stuff the made-up version of her in Conor’s head had to say…

What? Who do you think put her there? objected Jennifer. And on purpose, too.

He squeezed his eyes shut, because going around and around and around was making him dizzy, it was all too confusing, too much to juggle, he was just a seventh grader, he shouldn’t have to deal with this, this was crazy—

Look, not to be an asshole, but did you not literally volunteer for this?

—and as the snowflakes swirled behind his eyelids, he felt the firewall slip, lost his grip on the things he wasn’t thinking and suddenly it all came rushing back, his face shoved up against the filthy stall, the smell of piss and shit filling his nostrils as he wet himself, the hand of someone bigger, stronger—

(So much stronger.)

—gripping his hair so hard it hurt, utterly helpless as a stranger touched him, pawed at him, dug through his pockets and threw his things into the toilet one after another—

(This. This was the thing that Ashleigh did not understand.)

(Well, yeah, because you didn’t TELL me.)

—Conor had never even seen the high schooler’s face, it was a miracle that he’d gotten out even relatively unscathed, he had been completely and utterly helpless and if they had wanted to hurt him he would have been hurt—

Breathe, bud.

His father’s voice, this time, and he breathed—long and deep, willing his heart to slow.

Dangerous.

It was dangerous, East Binder was already dangerous and trying to fix it meant running straight toward the danger and that was dangerous.

And Conor didn’t understand why the Falhanner boys didn’t seem to see it. What it was that they were missing, or ignoring—or what it was that Conor was ignoring, some thing that made it all make sense, made it all okay—

Maybe they just don’t give a fuck.

Conor paused in his breathing.

Maybe they know, and it just doesn’t bother them.

Slowly, he took a deep breath in, filling his lungs as full as they would go.

Imagine if you’d always been blind, and then one day you could see…

Things had changed, for Conor, on the day they’d hauled Guyler off in an ambulance. Something had fallen away, and it had no longer felt—felt right, felt sustainable, to be in that same mode. To behave the way he had, back when he’d thought the world was safe.

…but my face wouldn’t be different. You’d just be different about it.

He opened his eyes, his gaze fixing on a single star.

…he hadn’t been able to go back to normal, after Guyler.

But Ashleigh had.

(Again the sense of betrayal, of outrage, like standing knee-deep in ocean surf.)

Because the other boy was refusing to look at the real world? Or because he’d already been there, the whole time?

Ashleigh wants to make it more.

That was the key. That was the thing that broke the symmetry between them—that Ashleigh wanted more of what was happening—more masks, more chaos—their school was spiraling, working itself up to something, and Ashleigh thought they should add fuel to the fire.

The thing that was happening with the cameras was new, it was new and it was worse and Conor was feeling dread about it, a premonition of something awful lurking, drawing ever closer. And Ashleigh’s position—his sincere, honest advice—was that Conor simply not care. That he wash his hands of it, just let it happen—that he do things Ashleigh’s way, with zero fucks given, instead of following his own instincts.

No, dumbass. I’m trying to get you to see that you CAN’T STOP IT, and that if you try to you’re just going to fuck yourself up.

You’re just a voice in my head!

You say that like you’re not.

Conor shivered. His eyes were pointed at a star, but he couldn’t tell if it was the same one he’d been looking at before.

(It wasn’t normal, could not be normal to have thoughts like this, he didn’t feel unbalanced the way he had on the morning after Halloween but it still seemed like there had to be something wrong in his brain, for this to be the way it worked—)

Too much.

They meant too much to each other, him and Ashleigh.

(It was a new thought, a strange thought, arriving abruptly, tricky and complicated and too grown-up. Conor almost couldn’t hold it, lying there in the dark, looking up at the glow-in-the-dark stars—could feel himself stretching to contain it.)

It wasn’t just that Conor meant too much to Ashleigh—Ashleigh meant too much to Conor, too, it was fucked up and it was fucking them up. Was fucking both of them up, not just Ashleigh. Guyler had tried to show Conor, but it had been too soon, he’d been too close, hadn’t been ready for it. The crazy part wasn’t how much Ashleigh cared about Conor, it was how little he cared about everything else, and the fact that that had somehow never bothered Conor before.

(Because it was Ashleigh.)

Because they—it wasn’t that they could do no wrong, exactly, in one another’s eyes, but more that the wrongness didn’t matter, was not allowed to matter, they were friends above all else, that was the deal, the way it worked, you had each other’s backs.

(Until Guyler.)

Conor fell asleep, then, and dreamed dreams that crumbled into ash when his father shook him awake, dreams that left him tired and troubled even though he couldn’t remember them. It was Sunday, and he spent the day with his parents as usual—

(Even though his punishment was long over, had ended almost a month ago.)

—and in that soft and private space he thought, and thought, and thought, and came at last to some conclusions.

First: that there was indeed a thing here for Ashleigh to learn—a thing the other boy needed to learn, as Conor had needed to learn it.

Second: that it wasn’t something Conor could teach him.

Conor had learned it from what Ashleigh had done to Guyler, and from the would-be rapist on Halloween, and from all the painful mistakes he’d made in half a year of training, and yes, even from the assault in the bathroom—it had been terrifying, it had been traumatizing, but he wouldn’t undo it, given the option, because it was a lesson he still hadn’t gotten all the way down into his bones, where it needed to be, where it belonged—that there was danger out there, real danger, there were things that were bigger than him, that he could not reason with, could not win against, could only do his best to predict and avoid.

When trouble comes, Ms. Palmano had quoted, do not be there.

(And he wondered, now, as he would not have, before—wondered why she had said it, what she had been trying to do, what change his teacher had been working in him, with the words.)

Which led him to his third conclusion: that the situation at school was indeed well and truly out of his hands, at this point. They had made twenty-eight masks, and Timothy had given away seven already—had no doubt left that conversation and gone to grab the rest, to pick up more of the ones they had squirrelled away and put them somewhere Conor couldn’t reach. It was too late, the fuse was lit, the snowball rolled—

—and it was not, in fact, Conor’s fault.

Not in the sense that he should have done things differently, that he really should have known better. There were places, maybe, where he had been stupid, and could try to be less stupid next time, but it was like leaving Ashleigh alone for five minutes and coming back to find Guyler unconscious and bleeding. Sometimes things just…went wrong, and there wasn’t really anything you could have done to stop it—wasn’t any way for you to have known, in advance, that it was coming, and done something else instead.

The best you could do—

(Conor thought, as he packed up his bookbag on Monday morning, went outside to wait for the bus, his breath misting in the cold January air.)

—was try to be ready. As ready as you could be, in as many ways as you could manage, for whatever might ultimately come.

He had already been doing that, as well as he knew how. But—

(He was embarrassed to admit.)

—it was just now occurring to him, for the very first time, that there was a huge difference between being ready himself—

(Being competent, himself; being capable; himself; being able to handle himself, carry his own weight without becoming a burden on anyone else.)

—and being ready for others. Ready on behalf of others, ready to take the weight of others, to carry them out of danger. Being sufficiently strong, sufficiently skilled, to make up for others’ unreadiness, their recklessness.

For Ashleigh’s recklessness.

And Timothy’s.

And everyone else’s, too, there were dozens of people involved, now, they were all playing games on the precipice and seemed not to care, seemed not to know.

Some of them were sixth graders.

Children.

Children, like Timothy was a child—Timothy had gotten in over his head with Eichardt through childishness, he’d gotten himself stuck and it had taken an even bigger monster to get him out and now he was smashing up school cameras in his spare time…

(Which, come to think of it, Devon was almost certainly mixed up in all of this, and at the very least knew something about it.)

And sure, maybe it would all turn out fine. Maybe Conor was just worrying, psyching himself out, letting his anxiety run wild. Maybe nothing would go wrong.

But.

If it tried to.

If things started to go wrong for real, started snowballing in a bad direction—

(Started? scoffed an imaginary Holt.)

—from where Conor stood, it didn’t look like there was anything out there that would stop them. No walls, no nets, no guardrails.

There was nothing.

They were on their own.

“Can I, um.”

Ms. Palmano waited, polite, patient, as the other students streamed out of the classroom, Ashleigh and Jennifer both hovering by the door.

She literally told you to ask for things.

“Can I come here for lunch?” Conor blurted. “And—maybe math? Like last time?”

“Ah,” said his teacher. “I’ve got meetings all through planning today and tomorrow, unfortunately. You’re certainly welcome to use the room—I’ll leave it unlocked. But if you wanted to chat it’ll have to wait until Wednesday.”

Conor shifted his weight from one foot to the other. Ms. Palmano frowned slightly, giving him a close, searching look—

“Unless I should reschedule?” she asked. “If it’s something important—”

“Wednesday’s fine,” Conor said hastily, shaking his head. “It’s not—I mean, Wednesday’s soon enough.”

(Probably.)

She held his gaze for a long moment.

“Are you sure?” she asked, her voice now soft—now her other voice.

Conor felt his cheeks flush.

“I’m sure,” he said, trying to sound it. “It—I’ll see you tomorrow.”

“Wait,” she said, as he turned to leave. “Do you want me to write you a pass for Mrs. Atkins? So you can use the room?”

“No, I’ll just—I’ll wait.”

“Okay,” she said, sounding skeptical. “See you tomorrow, Conor.”

“Can you teach me how to fight?”

Guyler’s eyes narrowed.

“I mean, not like—I mean like self-defense, like can you teach me how to, I don’t know, not get beat up. Break out of joint locks or—or whatever.”

Guyler’s eyes stayed narrow. Around them, the cafeteria was a kaleidoscope of blue and gold, the roar of five hundred conversations like a never-ending explosion.

“I’m a green belt,” the other boy said levelly. “And I lost the last fight I was in.”

“Is that a no?” Conor asked.

“It’s not a yes,” Guyler said. “More of a what the fuck.” His head tilted slightly. “Did something else happen?”

There was something in the other boy’s gaze that Conor did not want to meet, and he dropped his eyes to the table, where his hands were currently curled into fists. He forced them to relax, spreading his palms across the cool laminate. “No,” he said. The opposite, in fact.

But he didn’t know how to fit it all into words in real time, and the little leatherbound journal was gone, and—

Hey, quick question. Why didn’t you just ask your mom for anoth—

“Conor.”

Conor’s eyes snapped up.

(He wasn’t sure, but he thought that might have been the first time the other boy had ever said his name.)

“Did something else happen?” Guyler repeated.

“No,” Conor said again. “I just—”

Want to be ready.

The silence stretched.

This was a mistake.

Yeah, no sh—

“Where’s your house?” Guyler asked abruptly.

“What?”

“Where’s your house?”

Conor gave him the address.

“I’ll come over tomorrow,” Guyler said. “After school. To talk about it.”

Gratitude.

“I didn’t say yes,” he cautioned. “I said I’d come talk about it.”

Still.

•••

Conor stopped cold on his way to the gym, a couple of other students bumping into him before the flow split around him like a rock in a stream.

The plexiglass in front of the camera over the double doors of the seventh grade wing was painted black.

It hadn’t been painted black at 1:05, when he’d left Math for lunch.

…well, yeah? That’s how, like, things happen.

But this was different. This wasn’t like showing up to see that something had happened—this was (almost) seeing it happen in real time. Conor had looked at that camera, had looked up and into its lens barely half an hour ago, when it was unruined and working.

Didn’t you see the cafeteria one get fucked up in real time too?

It wasn’t the same.

…I don’t get it.

Slowly, Conor began walking again, letting the crush of bodies sweep him up and carry him out into the breezeway.

•••

“Devon.”

“Uluru, my man, what is the tea?”

The older boy slid over a few inches, making space for Conor to drop into the seat.

“I don’t know what that means,” Conor said bluntly.

“They say the first step on the path to enlightenment is acknowledging your ignorance.”

Conor bit back a sigh, feeling mulish. “I don’t know what that means, either,” he grumbled. “Can we talk?”

They were on bus 134, heading home for the day, the two of them only a few seats away from the very back, deep in what Conor could not help but think of as enemy territory.
