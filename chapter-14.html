Chapter 14 – Spiral

⚜︎

September 1st, 6:34 PM

“…you’re not really brave at all, are you? You’re just usually not scared.”

•••

There were cameras in the hallways when they came back from break, and the students of East Binder Secondary School went to war.

Not right away—at first, there was just ordinary surprise, ordinary gossip, the collective shrug that was the usual response whenever the grownups did something cruel or uncaring or capricious. It wasn’t that the students took the massive intrusion in stride, exactly, so much as that there didn’t seem to be anything they could do, in response.

But then someone ripped the camera outside of the cafeteria from its mount, leaving it smashed on the ground where more than half the school saw it as second lunch ended and third lunch began. And when Conor went to the electives building for Art at the end of the day, the camera just inside the double doors was mangled as if it had been hit with a sledgehammer—was dangling by twisted wires, its lens shattered.

By Wednesday, both had been replaced, and rumor had it that Mr. Sykes had suspended at least one high schooler in connection with “the incident”—

(As it was referred to over the intercom, during morning announcements.)

—but on Thursday morning, they arrived to find police cars in the front circle again, and every single camera across the entire campus destroyed or removed.

Thirty-four—that was the number that spread through the grapevine. Thirty-four cameras. Some had been torn down, others carefully unscrewed from their mounts. Some had had their lenses broken, wires cut, casings cracked. A few on the high school side had been melted—or so everyone was saying—and a number had been vandalized, spray-painted, the tapes removed and the insides filled with caulk, with foam, with soda and shaving cream.

(“Look,” said Ashleigh. “Don’t take this the wrong way. But it wasn’t you, right?”)

(“I was going to ask if it was you.”)

It was all anyone could talk about for the rest of the day. “I looked it up,” Holt said grimly, at lunch. “Those things cost around $700 each, even with like educational discounts or whatever. That’s like twenty grand they lit on fire last night.”

They, because it clearly had not been a single person, could not possibly have been a single person—seemed like it couldn’t have been done with thirty people, since surely the buildings had locks, had alarms, wouldn’t the police have come once the very first camera went offline—

“I mean, that part’s not that crazy, really,” Jennifer had reasoned, in the hallway after Science. “I don’t think they had anyone watching at night, do you? Probably they were just recording.”

Aren’t there remote security services, though? Conor had wondered. The sort where some rando in a call center a thousand miles away was watching a hundred screens from twenty different locations, and would call the cops if anything happened at any of them…

(Wondered, but not said aloud, because with only a minute or so to spare before he had to head to Social Studies and she to Math, there were more pressing matters to attend to. The two of them had grown bolder, since Christmas—were sometimes willing to draw jeers and whistles in the hallway, and rarely said more than a handful of actual words during their brief between-class rendezvous.)

Besides, it wasn’t like East Binder was flush with cash. Conor supposed it wouldn’t have been that strange for Mr. Sykes to have started off with the discount package…

…started off being the operative term, because over the weekend the cameras were replaced, the new ones now surrounded by metal cages of eighth-inch wire mesh.

“Mistake,” Ashleigh had murmured, as the two of them walked to homeroom on Monday morning, and Conor had privately agreed. Simply replacing the cameras would have been boring, indifferent—would have signaled indifference, robbed the rebellion of its power.

(No sword so sharp it can’t be dulled by apathy, as Ms. Palmano had once quipped.)

But the cages were a clear escalation. Were an admission, a challenge, the sort of defiance that everyone besides Timothy learned not to try in their very first week. They were proof that the vandals had gotten under the grownups’ skin, and now there was blood in the water.

It was hard for Conor to put his finger on what exactly he was picking up on, as he walked through the hallways, through the bathrooms, caught snippets of chatter in the courtyard and the cafeteria. It was a vibe, more than anything else. Something sharper-than-usual in the air—an extra note of resentment, the feel of cut-off sentences and elbows held in check.

And don’t forget the uniforms.

All through the autumn, the Central students had quietly boycotted the East Binder uniforms, stubborn splashes of color in a sea of blue and gold. There had been an official grace period during the first two weeks, in which they had been supposed to be buying their sweatshirts and khakis, but the third week had come and gone and almost no one had switched over, and by then there was inertia. There had been a couple of suspensions, but they hadn’t produced any lasting effects—

And Mr. Sykes was pretty busy back then, anyway.

That had been at the peak of the Eagle-Bulldog war, and the administration had apparently had bigger fish to fry. But now, in January, the defiance was suddenly dissolving, the out-of-place colors growing rarer and rarer, as if word was slowly spreading. Part of it seemed to be simple solidarity, a symbolic drawing-together in the face of a shared enemy. But part of it…

(“Lot harder to figure out who busted up a camera when they’re wearing the same shit as everybody else,” Eddie had pointed out, serious for once.)

Mr. Sykes was scary and competent, but most of the threat lay in his ability to focus power. He could throw you in detention, call your parents, kick you out of school—but only if he knew who you were. They couldn’t suspend everybody.

And everybody—it seemed—had noticed. Had come together, gotten on board, raised a single flag. It was impossible that no one knew who had destroyed all the cameras overnight, and yet no one was talking.

And then—

—slowly—

—the cameras began breaking again.

Not all at once, this time. Not in a dramatic show of defiance.

But there were wads of paper stuffed into the wire cage of the one outside the cafeteria when Conor walked past it during break, Mr. Sykes standing there looking stern as the janitor stood on a ladder, cleaning it out. And—as Conor would learn later—at that very moment, two different students were simultaneously shoving pool cues through the cages of the cameras at either end of the high school math wing, shattering their lenses. They had covered their faces, had simply dropped the pool cues to the ground when they were done, had blended back in with the crowd and gone on to the next class without anyone ratting.

A day later, there was plexiglass fused to the front of every cage.

And by the end of the day after that, something like a quarter of the plexiglass shields had been ruined in one way or another—painted or sharpie’d or scratched to opacity.

It wasn’t that the faculty weren’t trying. There were teachers in the hallway during every class change, now. But the students were trying, too, and if there was one thing the denizens of East Binder knew how to do, it was create situations that absolutely demanded the full attention of any nearby responsible adult. With more than thirty kids per grownup, there simply weren’t enough eyes to go around.

(“Should we be doing something?” Conor wondered aloud. “Like, trying to stop this?”)

(“Should we be helping?” Ashleigh countered.)

It was a good question. It wasn’t like anyone wanted East Binder to be worse than it already was—

Well, that’s probably not true, actually, pointed out Conor’s shoulder Jennifer.

Okay, fair. It wasn’t like most of the students wouldn’t have welcomed some relief from the chaos, some increased sense of order and safety.

But there seemed to be near-universal agreement that the cameras were not about that. Were not going to fix anything—were for the grownups, not the kids—would at most mildly inconvenience the bad guys, shove everything two inches further under the rug.

(It wasn’t like there were cameras in the bathrooms, after all.)

And—as Ms. Palmano pointed out, in a lecture that was barely even pretending to be about something else—it wasn’t the case that you could bully people into good behavior. For a little while, maybe, but not in the long run—

(Or, as she said, sending a chill down Conor’s spine: “People don’t follow the lines on the road because they’re scared of tickets.”)

—there were always loopholes, blindspots—ways to appear compliant while continuing to do whatever you wanted. Rules only worked if people genuinely believed that following them was better than not—that cooperation and kindness would pay off more, in the end.

And that…

…wasn’t true.

That just wasn’t true, at East Binder.

Bullying, and cheating, and theft, and vandalism—they all just worked, were proven, viable strategies. There were plenty of people who didn’t, who wouldn’t, who flatly refused—

(Like Jennifer. Like Holt.)

—but those people held back for their own reasons, not because they couldn’t get away with it. Getting away with it was trivially easy, would only be a tiny bit harder with cameras in the halls, and so the whole thing was just a headache, a nuisance, a sad little joke. Like having to take off your shoes and dump out your water at the airport—it solved nothing, and only made things that much harder for all the normal people who were keeping their heads down and just trying to get through the day.

“Still, though,” Conor had pressed. “We should think about it, right?”

And Ashleigh had agreed—or agreed to humor him, at least—and so the two of them had scooped up Timothy for a strategy meeting one Saturday morning, only for the younger boy to cheerfully confess that he had destroyed two cameras himself, and helped out with distractions in six other cases.

“By the way,” Timothy had added, clearly enjoying Conor’s stricken look, “they do have offsite security guards on payroll. They don’t come if you’re just on the roof or whatever, but they show up in about six minutes if you open a door or break a window.”

(Were those new, some part of Conor had wondered, or had that first round of cameras somehow been destroyed in a way that didn’t trip any alarms?)

Further interrogation—requiring no blood, thankfully—had revealed that yes, Timothy had used the masks, duh, obviously, what kind of a question was that, it would’ve been stupid not to use them—

—at which point Conor, panicking, had tried to pull the plug on the whole thing, only for the younger boy to shrug and say all right, then, I can probably scrounge up two or three but a lot of them are gone.

“Gone?” Conor had spluttered.

Again the younger boy was unashamed, unembarrassed, had looked him straight in the eye without flinching. “I gave away seven,” he said calmly. “Four of them to one person, who definitely gave away the extras, don’t ask me to who because I don’t know on purpose. And probably most of the ones we hid are still there, but the one behind the drainpipe by the teacher’s lounge is definitely gone, and I kinda don’t think it was a teacher who found it.”

It was at that point that Conor’s brain had fully seized up, had overextended itself trying to track all of the implications, the possibilities, everything that you would expect to go wrong with ten or fifteen masks floating around on top of everything else. He had spent upwards of thirty seconds frozen, glitching, utterly unable to think or speak or even see—

(At least, insofar as “seeing” implied something like actually processing the light his eyes were absorbing. It was the visual equivalent of what would have happened if everyone around him suddenly started speaking Japanese—it wasn’t that his eyes stopped working so much as that his brain was unable to do anything at all with the signals they were sending it.)

He’d come back to earth at the sound of the front door slamming, had shaken himself out of his paralysis to find that Timothy was gone and he and Ashleigh were alone in the living room.

“I sent him away so you wouldn’t, uh, kill him,” Ashleigh had said quietly. “But also, I think he’s right.”

And then Ashleigh, who was Conor’s best friend in the entire world—

(But who was also insane, and reckless, and did not understand how things worked.)

—Ashleigh had gone on to explain that, in his view, the role of hero did not belong to them, was not theirs to control, that the other students had just as much of a right to try to break free as the three of them did, and that the camera war was proof—

(“If December wasn’t enough already.”)

—that they couldn’t do everything that needed to be done, all by themselves. That there were simply too many problems, too much ground to cover—that they couldn’t count on being where they needed to be at the exact moment they needed to be there, and were spread too thin to make any real difference.

(Which had hit uncomfortably hard, uncomfortably close, was an echo of the thoughts that Conor himself had already been having, but the answer to all of that wasn’t this, surely, it could not possibly be this—)

“Why not?” Ashleigh had argued, reading the expression on Conor’s face like a book. “I mean, isn’t this better? Like, setting aside the mask thing—and it’s not like they’re even our masks—I mean, they are, they’re yours, but like, you get it, right, they aren’t Tracer, they aren’t Siphon. So like, setting that aside. If Timothy hadn’t stolen them, if he’d just come up with it himself, or like bought a bunch of Halloween masks, or whatever. Isn’t this, I dunno. More awesome?”

And whether because Conor’s brain was still only half online—

—or because he had been spending more and more time around people like Jennifer and Guyler and his parents—

—or just because he still wasn’t over it, not all the way, there was shit that had happened in the past four months that Ashleigh should still feel bad about, he should be more—more fucking demure, or something, that probably wasn’t the right word but seriously—

For whatever reason, it was at that point that Conor had fixed the other boy with a look, as if he could pin him to the wall with the force of sheer disapproval, and had said, with all the cold fury he could muster:

“You’re just looking for a fucking distraction.”

There had been a heavy silence, at the end of which Ashleigh had given a sad little shrug.

“I mean, yeah,” he said softly. “But that doesn’t mean I’m wrong.”

And then Conor had had to hold himself very, very still, because the feeling that washed over him was betrayal—

(And close on its heels, confusion.)

—and the conversation had fizzled until the pair of them awkwardly moved on to other things, lunch and Nerf guns and the rusty trampoline in Ashleigh’s backyard.

It wasn’t until later—much later, in bed that night—that Conor had been able to unravel the tangle, figure out that he’d felt betrayed because Ashleigh had forgotten, because it was like he didn’t care, after they had talked through everything on the rooftop at Rosemary, Conor had thought that—that—that they were together in something. That they had both seen, both agreed—

This isn’t a fucking game.

It wasn’t a game, it was real, there were real consequences, you couldn’t actually run around on rooftops trying to stop bullies without—

(The memory of bones breaking underneath his feet.)

—without colliding, smashing into things, into people, sooner or later there was bound to be some kind of—of—of incident, and people were far more fragile than Conor had once believed. Even if you took it seriously, even if you genuinely did your very best to prepare, things could still go sideways in an instant and Ashleigh and Timothy were not taking it seriously.

Somebody was going to get hurt.

Somebody was going to get hurt.

Somebody could die, even—

Really?

It was a quiet thought, almost timid, but it pulled him up short, brought the runaway train of his thoughts to a halt once more.

I mean, do you really actually think somebody’s going to DIE? Is that, I dunno. An exaggeration.

It was Ashleigh’s voice, his shoulder Ashleigh, but for once it wasn’t flippant, wasn’t joking, seemed to be honestly asking the question.

(Conor took several deep breaths.)

He looked up at the glow-in-the-dark stars on the ceiling, trying his best to think.

Okay.

Okay, no. Probably no one was going to die.

But somebody could, somebody really actually could, and even if they didn’t that didn’t make it all fine, didn’t make it safe, they were playing with fire, all of them, and ending up in an ambulance was better than ending up underground but it still wasn’t good, look at what had happened to Guyler—

Slow down.

He slowed. Breathed. Shouldered aside the clamor and tried to make space inside of his head. And into that space…

If somebody ends up in an ambulance, that’s not your fault.

What? Of course it was his fault—would be his fault—he was the one who had started all this, those were his masks, they had been his missions, he was the reason Timothy had—

Jesus Christ, dude.

(Again he forced himself to slow.)

If somebody ended up in an ambulance—

Then it’s not your fault!

(Slow.)

It…would be, though?

(Wouldn’t it?)

What do you think Ms. Palmano would have to say about that?

He frowned.

What would his teacher have to say, about whose fault it was, if the other students went off and did things that were dangerous, that were their own choices, and ended up getting hurt…

He shook his head. There was something wrong with that thought, something sneaky—

(I don’t trust her, Jennifer had said. It’s Trojan horses all the way down.)

But that wasn’t fair. The real Ms. Palmano shouldn’t be on the hook for stuff the made-up version of her in Conor’s head had to say…

Who do you think put her there? objected Jennifer. And on purpose, too.

He squeezed his eyes shut, because going around and around and around like this was making him dizzy, it was all too confusing, too much to juggle, he was just a seventh grader, he shouldn’t have to deal with this, this was crazy—

Look, not to be an asshole, but did you not literally volunteer for this?

—and as the snowflakes swirled behind his eyelids, he felt the firewall slip, lost his grip on the things he wasn’t thinking and suddenly it all came rushing back, his face shoved up against the filthy stall, the smell of piss and shit filling his nostrils as he wet himself, the hand of someone bigger, stronger—

(So much stronger.)

—gripping his hair so hard it hurt, utterly helpless as a stranger touched him, pawed at him, dug through his pockets and threw his things into the toilet one after another—

(This. This was the thing that Ashleigh did not understand.)

(Well, yeah, because you didn’t TELL me.)

—Conor had never even seen the high schooler’s face, it was a miracle that he’d gotten out even relatively unscathed, he had been completely and utterly helpless and if they had wanted to hurt him he would have been hurt—

Breathe, bud.

His father’s voice, this time, and he breathed—long and deep, willing his heart to slow.

Dangerous.

It was dangerous, East Binder was already dangerous and trying to fix it meant running straight toward the danger and that was dangerous.

And Conor didn’t understand why the Falhanner boys didn’t seem to see it. What it was that they were missing, or ignoring—or what it was that Conor was ignoring, some thing that made it all make sense, made it all okay—

Maybe they just don’t give a fuck.

Conor paused in his breathing.

Maybe they know, and it just doesn’t bother them.

Slowly, he took a deep breath in, filling his lungs as full as they would go.

Imagine if you’d always been blind, and then one day you could see…

Things had changed, for Conor, on the day they’d hauled Guyler off in an ambulance. Something had fallen away, and it had no longer felt—felt right, felt sustainable, to be in that same mode. To behave the way he had, back when he’d thought the world was safe.

…but my face wouldn’t be different. You’d just be different about it.

He opened his eyes, his gaze fixing on a single star.

…he hadn’t been able to go back to normal, after Guyler.

But Ashleigh had.

(Again the sense of betrayal, of outrage, like standing knee-deep in ocean surf.)

…because the other boy was refusing to look at the real world?

Or because he’d already been there, the whole time?

Ashleigh wants to make it more. Wants there to be more of…all this.

That was the key. That was the thing that broke the symmetry between them—that his friend wanted more of what was happening, more masks, more chaos, more of what he called awesomeness—their school was spiraling, working itself into a frenzy, and Ashleigh truly, honestly believed that they should be adding fuel to the fire.

No, dumbass. I’m trying to get you to see that you CAN’T STOP IT, and that if you try to you’re just going to fuck yourself up.

You’re just a voice in my head!

You say that like you’re not.

Conor shivered. His eyes were pointed at a star, but he couldn’t tell if it was the same one he’d been looking at before.

(It wasn’t normal, could not be normal to have thoughts like this in your bed in the middle of the night, he didn’t feel unbalanced the way he had on the morning after Halloween but it still seemed like there had to be something wrong in his brain, for this to be the way it worked—)

Too much.

They meant too much to each other, him and Ashleigh.

It was a new thought, a strange thought, arriving abruptly, tricky and complicated and too grown-up. Conor almost couldn’t hold it, lying there in the dark, looking up at the glow-in-the-dark stars—could feel himself stretching, struggling to contain it…

It wasn’t just that Conor meant too much to Ashleigh. Ashleigh meant too much to Conor, too, it was fucked up and it was fucking them up. Was fucking both of them up, not just Ashleigh. Guyler had tried to show Conor, but it had been too soon, he’d been too close, hadn’t been ready for it. The crazy part wasn’t how much Ashleigh cared about Conor, it was how little he cared about everything else, and the fact that that had somehow never bothered Conor before.

(Because it was Ashleigh.)

Because they—it wasn’t that they could do no wrong, exactly, in one another’s eyes, but more that the wrongness didn’t matter, was not allowed to matter, they were friends above all else, that was the deal, that was the way it worked, you had each other’s backs, period.

(Until Guyler. Until Ashleigh went too far.)

Conor fell asleep, then, and dreamed dreams that crumbled into ash when his father shook him awake, dreams that left him tired and troubled even though he couldn’t remember them. It was Sunday, and he spent the day with his parents as usual—

(Despite the fact that his so-called punishment was long over, had ended almost a month ago.)

—and in that soft and private space he thought, and thought, and thought, and came at last to some conclusions.

The first was that he was not done trying.

(Being not-done wasn’t a plan—wasn’t even really a direction. He still didn’t know whether the way forward was to fight the chaos or feed it or some secret third thing. He didn’t have ideas. But still, it helped, knowing that he wasn’t willing to give up yet. That he still cared about things getting better, and still wanted to be a part of how they got better.)

The second was that there was indeed a thing here for Ashleigh to learn—a thing the other boy needed to learn, as Conor himself had needed to learn it.

Third: that it wasn’t something Conor could teach him.

Conor had learned it by picking Guyler’s dried blood out from under his fingernails, and feeling the snap of bones beneath his shoes, and from all the painful mistakes he’d made in half a year of fighting gravity, and yes, even from having his face shoved into the bathroom stall—it had been terrifying, it had been traumatizing, but he wouldn’t undo it, given the option, because it was a lesson he still hadn’t gotten all the way down into his core, where it needed to be, where it belonged—that there was danger out there, real danger, there were things that were bigger than him, that he could not reason with, could not win against, could only do his best to predict and avoid.

When trouble comes, Ms. Palmano had quoted, do not be there.

(And he wondered, now, as he would not have, before—wondered why she had said it, what she had been trying to do, what change his teacher had been working in him, with her words.)

And thinking about Ashleigh led him to his fourth conclusion: that the situation at East Binder was indeed well and truly out of his hands, at this point. They had made twenty-eight masks, and Timothy had given away seven already—had no doubt left their conversation and gone to grab the rest, to pick up more of the ones they had squirreled away and put them somewhere Conor could not reach. It was too late, the fuse was lit, the snowball rolled…

…and it was not, in fact, Conor’s fault.

Not in the sense that he should have done things differently, that he really should have known better. There were places, maybe, where he had been stupid, and could try to be less stupid next time, but it was like leaving Ashleigh alone for five minutes and coming back to find Guyler unconscious and bleeding. Sometimes things just…went wrong, and there wasn’t really anything you could have done to stop it—wasn’t any way for you to have known, in advance, that it was coming, and done something else instead.

The best you could do—

(Conor thought, as he packed up his bookbag on Monday morning, went outside to wait for the bus, his breath misting in the cold January air.)

—was try to be ready. As ready as you could be, in as many ways as you could manage, for whatever might ultimately come.

He had already been doing that, as well as he knew how. But—

(He was embarrassed to admit.)

—it was only just now occurring to him, for the very first time, that there was a huge difference between being ready himself—

(Being competent, himself; being capable, himself; being able to handle himself, carry his own weight without becoming a burden on anyone else.)

—and being ready for others. Ready on behalf of others, ready to take the weight of others, to carry them out of danger. Being sufficiently strong, sufficiently skilled, to make up for others’ unreadiness, their recklessness.

For Ashleigh’s recklessness.

And Timothy’s.

And fucking Eddie’s, probably.

And everyone else’s, too, there were dozens of people involved, now, they were all playing games on the edge of a cliff and seemed not to care, seemed not to know.

Some of them were sixth graders.

Children.

Children, like Timothy was a child—Timothy had gotten in over his head with Eichardt through childishness, it hadn’t had to happen, he had done it to himself and it had taken an even bigger monster to get him out and now he was smashing up school cameras in his spare time…

(Which, come to think of it, Devon was almost certainly mixed up in all of this, and at the very least would know things about what was going on.)

…and sure, maybe it would all turn out fine. Maybe Conor was just worrying, psyching himself out, letting his anxiety run wild. Maybe nothing truly terrible would happen.

But.

If it tried to.

If things started to go wrong for real, started snowballing in a bad direction—

(Started? scoffed an imaginary Holt.)

—from where Conor stood, it didn’t look like there was anything out there that would stop them. No walls, no nets, no guardrails.

There was nothing.

They were on their own.

“Can I, um.”

His teacher waited—polite, patient—as the other students streamed out of the classroom, Ashleigh and Jennifer both hovering by the door.

Stop stuttering. She literally told you to ask for things.

“Can I come here for lunch?” Conor forced himself to say. “And—maybe math? Like last time?”

“Ah,” she replied. “I’ve got meetings all through planning today and tomorrow both, unfortunately. You’re welcome to use the room—I’ll leave it unlocked. But if you wanted to chat it’ll have to wait until Wednesday.”

Conor shifted his weight from one foot to the other. Ms. Palmano frowned slightly, giving him a close, searching look—

“Unless I should reschedule?” she asked. “If it’s something imp—”

“Wednesday’s fine,” Conor said hastily, shaking his head. “It’s not—I mean, Wednesday’s soon enough.”

(Probably.)

She held his gaze for a long moment.

“Are you sure?” she asked, her voice now soft—now her other voice.

Conor felt his cheeks flush.

“I’m sure,” he said, trying to sound it. “It—I’ll see you tomorrow.”

“Wait,” she said, as he turned to leave. “Do you want me to write you a pass for Mrs. Atkins? So you can use the room during fifth?”

“No, I’ll just—I’ll wait.”

“Okay,” she said, sounding skeptical. “See you tomorrow, Conor.”

•••

“Can you teach me how to fight?”

Guyler’s eyes narrowed.

“I mean, not like—I mean like self-defense, like can you teach me how to, I dunno, not get beat up. Break out of joint locks, or—whatever.”

Guyler’s eyes stayed narrow. Around them, the cafeteria was a kaleidoscope of blue and gold, the roar of five hundred voices like a never-ending explosion.

“I’m a green belt,” the other boy said levelly. “And I lost the last fight I was in.”

“Is that a no?” Conor pressed.

“It’s not a yes,” Guyler said. “More of a what the fuck.” His head tilted slightly. “Did something else happen?”

There was something in the other boy’s gaze that Conor did not want to meet, and he dropped his eyes to the table, where he was surprised to see that his hands were currently curled into fists. He forced them to relax, spreading his palms across the cool laminate. “No,” he said.

The opposite, in fact.

But he didn’t know how to fit it all into words in real time—was used to having hours and hours to compose his thoughts, handing them over only once they were ready, complete.

Hey, quick question. Why didn’t you just ask your mom for another journ—

“Conor.”

Conor’s eyes snapped up.

(He wasn’t sure, but he thought that might have been the first time the other boy had ever said his name out loud.)

“Did something else happen?” Guyler repeated.

“No,” Conor said again. “I just…”

Want to be ready.

(Except that that wasn’t right, not quite, ready wasn’t the standard, was not a thing it was possible to be, it was more that he wanted to be less unready but those words, on their own, would not be enough to get the thing across—)

This was a mistake.

Yeah, no sh—

“Where’s your house?” Guyler asked abruptly.

“What?”

“Where’s your house?”

Conor gave him the address.

“Over by Hunter Glen?” Guyler asked. “Near the highway bridge?”

Conor nodded.

“I can come over Wednesday,” he said. “After school. To talk about it.”

Gratitude.

“I didn’t say yes,” Guyler cautioned. “I said I’d come talk about it.”

Still.

•••

Conor stopped cold on his way to the gym, a couple of other students bumping into him before the flow split around him like a rock in a stream.

The plexiglass in front of the camera over the double doors of the seventh grade wing was painted black.

It hadn’t been painted black at 1:05, when he’d left Math for lunch.

…well, yeah? That’s how, like, things happen.

But this was different. This wasn’t like showing up to see that something had happened—this was (almost) seeing it happen in real time. Conor had looked at that camera, had looked up and into its lens barely half an hour ago, when it was unruined and working.

Didn’t you see the cafeteria one get fucked up in real time too?

She didn’t seem mad, was half-smiling, her voice light and syrupy. She stuck out her tongue at him, and even in his twice-drained state he had to stop himself from lunging at her, tackling her, covering her like a blanket.

(Some part of him wanted to—to eat her, or something, there was something about the look on her face that lit his veins on fire, she had to be doing it on purpose—)

“Look,” Jennifer said, as she reached for a soft cloth on her desk and tossed it to him, as she pulled a tissue from a box and wiped at the corner of her mouth. “Don’t get me wrong. You definitely owe me one—”

“Two.”

She smiled. He smiled back.

“I bet right, then?” she asked.

It took Conor a second to remember—

I’m betting you’ll like it.

He felt his cheeks go hot.

“Two,” she agreed. “And I’m looking forward to cashing in. But—”

She paused, her eyes tracing over his body in a way that made him shiver.

“—not from Sad, Distracted Conor. You know?”

He blinked.

“Like, that was fun. I had fun, even though you’re kind of a lump. I never got to try anything like that before. But when it’s my turn…”

She sat back down on the bed next to him, put a hand on his bare chest, leaned in and gave him a kiss, the softest, lightest brush of the lips.

“I think I deserve your best, you know?” she whispered. “Your full attention?”

God DAMN.

She leaned back, stood, and he felt himself drawn into the space where she had just been, pulled, his body trying to fall toward hers even as she moved out of reach.

“I want you, I don’t know. On top of your game.”

The words were cocky, and the look in her eyes mostly playful. Challenging, teasing. Jennifer.

But there was something else, too—a crack in the façade, a tiny flash of something that Conor almost missed, would have missed, had probably seen before and missed before but in that moment he was really, actually looking—

“Yeah,” he said, hoping she could hear how hard he meant it. “For sure.”
