She drew her hand back, and her own eyes were excruciatingly gentle.

She drew back further still, straightening, her shoulders taking on something of the bearing that she had in her classroom, as though there were an invisible whiteboard behind her. “Doesn’t mean you don’t try anyway,” she said. “Do your best, Conor. Do your best, and let that be enough. I don’t know if Ashleigh’s going to come out of this okay. I do think you should try to help. But—”

She broke off, shrugged. “If you can’t help. If it doesn’t work. If you can’t actually think of anything that actually seems good to do, I hope you’ll be okay anyway, I hope you’ll say, I tried my hardest, and I didn’t do anything fake, and the rest was out of my hands.”

Conor swallowed. “But how do I know if it’s my hardest?” he asked, his voice barely above a whisper.

What if I could have done more?

His teacher burst out laughing.

“You listen to me,” she said bluntly. “When I tell you, which I am.”

Jennifer had a dentist’s appointment, would be gone until after break, and none of the rest of Conor’s people were there yet. So he wandered, aimless, drifting from one part of the school to the next.

It was risky, maybe, traveling alone. His injury meant that he was no longer nimble, no longer quick, could not outmaneuver a hostile bully or a suspicious teacher.

He swallowed. “Bummer,” he admitted. “But. Friends listen to music, together, too.”

“Am I your friend?” she asked.

“Yes.”

The word came out without thought, without resistance.

What is true is already so.

Jennifer squinted. “Where’ve you been all year?” she murmured.

“Sorry,” he said. “I, um. Sometimes it takes me a while to, uh.”

“Get unstupid?”

He winced. “Something like that.”

“I guess you have been preoccupied,” she teased, poking him in the ribs.

He jerked, grinned, caught her hand with his as she poked again and then—of course—jerked again as she got him with her other hand.

“Oh, sorry,” she whispered, as he gritted his teeth, lightning once again dancing through his broken arm.

“It’s fine,” he said. “Really.”

He was still holding her hand, and as he felt the play-fight go out of her fingers he loosened his grip, letting her twist until they were holding hands—holding hands together.

“About that,” she said.

“Hmm?”

“The preoccupied stuff.”

“Oh. Yeah?”

“Do you want to talk about it?”

He looked over at her, at the curves of her face lit up by The Magic School Bus. Her eyes were level, and warm, and steady, and searching.

“Not right now,” he said softly, leaning in to rest his head on her shoulder again. “Tell me about mushrooms instead.”
