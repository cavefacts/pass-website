“I’m sorry,” Conor said. “I didn’t have much notice, so I didn’t really have time to—”

“Holy shit,” Guyler breathed, lifting the box up from the torn wrapping paper with an air of something like reverence. “Is this really—”

“I had my mom check with your mom,” Conor said. “She said it was okay. She said you’ve actually gone and looked at it, like. A bunch of times?”

Guyler opened the box, reaching inside and drawing out the knife within, his hand wrapped firmly around the silvery hilt. It was huge, curved, and wicked, with multiple blades, looking as though it had been designed by aliens, or brought to Earth through a magical portal.

(Conor thought it looked almost like a dragon’s skull, if dragon’s bones were made of steel and silver.)

It was the first thing he’d thought of, when his mother had said Guyler’s birthday. He’d spent an hour that morning trying to think of something better and had come up blank, had scraped his piggybank clean for the requisite ninety-seven dollars.

“Anyway,” he said, still feeling inexplicably nervous. “I, uh. Saw it, a while back, and thought of you.”

•          •          •

Conor wavered in the doorway, feeling torn.

“Jared,” Guyler said. “Go upstairs, okay? I’ll meet you there in a minute.”

“I’m an ally; I don’t care if you guys smooch in front of me.”

“Yeah, but we do.”

The other boy laughed and vanished back into the interior of the house.

“What’s up?” Guyler asked softly.

They were standing in the hallway, the front door wide open behind Conor, his father waiting in the car in the driveway. It was 8:58 PM. They had left the mall, come home for cake and presents, spent the past two hours watching The Boondock Saints.

It was hard to leave. There was a part of Conor that was imagining itself on into the evening, video games and pillow fights and a long walk at midnight as the day turned over to Guyler’s actual birthday.

He remembered this.

He had missed this.

“I just,” Conor began, and then faltered.

“Take your time.”

Conor shook his head. It wasn’t time that he needed, it was—

“I’m sorry,” he said, forcing himself through the hole, pushing past the point of no return. “About—about standing you up. Before. I mean, I know I did the pushups, but like. I don’t know if I really told you how sorry I was.”

Guyler said nothing, his eyes taking on a strange and unfamiliar shape.

“And—I don’t know, I don’t know how to make it up to you for real, like pushups are sort of whatever, like you showed up on the—you know, to the rope thing, and I just—”

“Conor,” Guyler said. “Breathe.”

Conor breathed.

“I get mad sometimes,” Guyler said. Quietly, but firmly, burying Conor’s train of thought like eight inches of snow sliding off a roof. “Sometimes it’s stupid.”

(An ancient memory floated up, from over a year and a half ago, the other boy standing in the aisle of bus 134, his arms crossed. That’s my seat. Get out.)

“I don’t think it was stupid,” Conor said earnestly. “I—”

I respect you.

“I respect you,” he said, and the words felt strange coming out of his mouth, but somehow less strange than he would have guessed—less strange than they would have been before the afternoon of getting to know this other Guyler, the hidden Guyler, the only Guyler that Jared had (apparently) ever known. “I respect you more than—that.”

The other boy looked at him for a long, long moment, and then stepped forward, wrapping his arms around Conor in a hug.

(They were like steel, his arms—like braided bridge cables, not especially thick but shockingly firm, sculpted by ten thousand pushups. Conor hadn’t known, hadn’t noticed, had only ever seen the other boy in his school sweatshirt or his long-sleeved martial arts uniform, had never touched the other boy before.)

“Thanks,” Guyler whispered, his voice soft and close in Conor’s ear. “For coming to my birthday party.”

“Happy birthday, Guyler.”

And then Guyler let go, and stepped back, and pushed Conor gently out the door.

•          •          •

They were halfway home when Conor’s phone buzzed.

Holt says he ran into you and Guyler at the arcade. Said you can have visitors now? Can I like jerk off a bunch and then hang out with you tomorrow?

“You okay?” his father asked softly, as he turned the phone over in his lap, leaned his head back against the headrest, squeezed his eyes shut against the sudden threat of tears.

“Yeah,” Conor answered, the words coming out as a whisper. “I think I am.”
