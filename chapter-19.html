Conor processed this. “Nom means…bastard?” he ventured.

“You know, we never asked,” Jared said, shrugging. “Seems like a good guess, though.”

Conor looked back and forth between them again, hesitating.

“Go ahead,” Guyler said, grinning. “Spit it out.”

“You’re…different.”

“It’s my birthday.”

“That’s not what I mean.”

“What is he like at school?” Jared asked. “He won’t tell me.”

Conor looked at Guyler, who shrugged, still grinning.

“Quiet,” he said truthfully. And then: “Intense.”

And then, after a slightly longer pause:

“I’m, uh. Kind of…best friends with his worst enemy?”

“So I heard,” said Jared. “I also hear you’re shit at spelling.”

Conor blinked.

Guyler shrugged again. “What?” he said, in mock protest. “I was going down the list. Those were the top two things.”

What’s number three?

Conor couldn’t quite bring himself to ask.

“What’d he tell you about me?” Jared asked.

“Uh. Nothing.”

“Nothing?”

“…sorry?”

“He’s the one who should be sorry,” Jared said, reaching over and trying to pull the other boy into a headlock. Guyler ducked, dodged, slapped Jared’s hands away and retaliated with a kick under the table, both boys giggling.

(Giggling.)

“Bet he just didn’t want to have to admit that his friend Jared who goes to another school is now way better than him at tornado kicks after he went and took himself a three-month vacation.”

“Yeah, well, sabumnim says I can probably break a cinderblock with a punch, now.”

“Silver linings.”

“Are you,” Conor began, and then broke off.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

—friends outside of Tae Kwon Do, he had been going to ask. But obviously the answer to that was yes, since here they were, and apparently Jared would be spending the night—

“You’re kind of a weirdo, huh?” Jared said, interrupting the thought.

“What?”

“Not an insult,” he clarified. “Just—I’m noticing, is all.”

Conor stared.

“Wait, this isn’t, like. News, to you, is it? I’m not the first guy to tell you?”

Conor’s jaw worked soundlessly.

“Guyler, what the fuck, man, you gotta tell people these things.”

“Jared,” Guyler said, forming the words slowly and precisely. “You are a fucking weirdo.”

“Yeah, see? Like that.” The other boy turned back toward Conor. “Anyway, it’s cool to finally meet you. Sorry you can’t sleep over. For future reference, are you, like, scary movie type, or video games all night type, or like big deep philosophical conversations type, or what?”

“He’s sneak out and fuck around on rooftops type,” Guyler said.

“Oooooo, spicy. What’s your favorite roof?”

“Huh?”

“Your favorite. Do you not have a favorite? Are they like all equally good or something? I dunno, I’ve never fucked around on rooftops.”

“Uh,” Conor said, feeling slightly panicked.

“Breathe,” Guyler reminded him.

Conor breathed. And then—

When in Rome, I guess.

“You talk a lot,” he blurted.

“It’s true,” Jared said, seeming not the least bit bothered. “Let me know if it’s too much. I can’t really tone it down but I can shut up for, like, I want to say, three minutes at a time?”

Conor turned to look at Guyler, who smiled, and shrugged, looking almost shy.

•••

“I’m sorry,” Conor said. “I didn’t have much notice, so I didn’t really have time to—”

“Holy shit,” Guyler breathed, lifting the box up from the torn wrapping paper with an air of something like reverence. “Is this really—”

“I had my mom check with your mom,” Conor said. “She said it was okay. She said you’ve actually gone and looked at it, like. A bunch of times?”

Guyler opened the box, reaching inside and drawing out the knife within, his hand wrapped firmly around the silvery hilt. It was huge, curved, and wicked, with multiple blades, looking as though it had been designed by aliens, or brought to Earth through a magical portal.

(Conor thought it looked almost like a dragon’s skull, if dragons’ bones were made of steel and silver.)

It was the first thing he’d thought of, when his mother had said Guyler’s birthday. He’d spent an hour that morning trying to come up with something better and had drawn a blank, had scraped his piggybank clean for the requisite ninety-seven dollars.

“Anyway,” he said, still feeling inexplicably nervous. “I, uh. Saw it, a while back, and thought of you.”

•••

Conor wavered in the doorway, feeling torn.

“Jared,” Guyler said. “Go upstairs, okay? I’ll meet you there in a minute.”

“I’m an ally; I don’t care if you guys smooch in front of me.”

“Yeah, but we do.”

The other boy laughed and vanished back into the interior of the house.

“What’s up?” Guyler asked softly.

They were standing in the hallway, the front door wide open behind Conor, his father waiting in the car in the driveway. It was 9:06 PM. They had left the mall, come home for cake and presents, spent the past two hours watching The Boondock Saints.

It was hard to leave. There was a part of Conor that was imagining itself on into the evening, video games and pillow fights and a long walk at midnight as the day turned over to Guyler’s actual birthday.

He remembered this.

(He had missed this.)

“I just,” Conor began, and then faltered.

“Take your time.”

Conor shook his head. It wasn’t time that he needed, it was—

“I’m sorry,” he said, forcing himself through the hole, past the point of no return. “About—about standing you up. Before. I mean, I know I did the pushups, but like. I don’t know if I really told you how sorry I was.”

Guyler said nothing, his eyes taking on a strange and unfamiliar shape.

“And—I don’t know, I don’t know how to make it up to you for real, like pushups are sort of whatever, like you showed up on the—you know, to the rope thing, and I just—”

“Conor,” Guyler said. “Breathe.”

Conor breathed.

“I get mad sometimes,” Guyler said. Quietly, but firmly, burying Conor’s train of thought like eight inches of snow sliding off a roof. “Sometimes it’s stupid.”

(An ancient memory floated up, the first day of sixth grade, over a year and a half ago, Guyler standing in the aisle of bus 134, his arms crossed. That’s my seat. Get out.)

“I don’t think it was stupid,” Conor said earnestly. “I—”

I respect you.

“I respect you,” he said, and the words felt strange coming out of his mouth, but somehow less strange than he would have guessed—less strange than they would have been before the afternoon of getting to know this other Guyler, the hidden Guyler, the only Guyler that Jared had (apparently) ever known. “I respect you more than—that.”

The other boy looked at him for a long, long moment, and then stepped forward, wrapping his arms around Conor in a hug.

(They were like steel, those arms—like braided bridge cables, not especially thick but shockingly firm, sculpted by ten thousand pushups. Conor hadn’t known, hadn’t noticed, had only ever seen the other boy in his school sweatshirt or his long-sleeved martial arts uniform, had never touched him before.)

“Thanks,” Guyler whispered, his voice soft and close in Conor’s ear. “For coming to my birthday party.”

“Happy birthday, Guyler.”

And then Guyler let go, and stepped back, and pushed Conor gently out the door.

•••

They were halfway home when Conor’s phone buzzed.

Holt says he ran into you and Guyler at the mall. Said you can have visitors now? Can I like jerk off a bunch and then hang out with you tomorrow?

“You okay?” his father asked softly, as he turned the phone over in his lap, leaned his head back against the headrest, squeezed his eyes shut against the sudden threat of tears.

“Yeah,” Conor answered, the words coming out as a whisper. “I think I am.”
