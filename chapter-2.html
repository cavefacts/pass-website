August 31st, 11:34 AM

“I never realized you could see our houses from up here.”

“Yeah, this is where I go to watch your mom in the shower.”

“Funny. Your mom just invites me in.”

“No wonder you smell like a girl.”

“…think we could get on top of the chapel?”

“What, like the steeple?”

“No. I mean, yeah, that would be sweet, but I meant the castley part. Those cracks between the stones—they’re big enough to get your fingers into them.”

“Maybe. Pretty long climb, if we’re going straight up the wall. Why?”

“Well, if we were up there, we could probably see every roof at the community college. Maybe there’s some cool stuff up top that we never spotted from the ground.”

“Like more roof jumps?”

“Maybe. I want to practice someplace smaller, first, and we still haven’t found anything good around here. Would be good to do one that’s a little less—”

“Insane?”

“It’s not insane. We both did it at the beach, didn’t we?”

“Into sand. And it took, like, six tries before we made it far enough.”

“Yeah, but we also didn’t have as much height.”

“You think reminding me about how big a drop it is will make me feel better?”

“That’s why I want to practice. The sand thing was good, but we’ve got to get better at rolling on hard stuff.”

“I still think there’s, like, a limit on how much the rolling’s going to help.”

“Come on. It worked when we jumped off your garage. And it works for paratroopers. They hit the ground a lot harder than we do.”

“Not if we’re dropping four stories.”

“It’s one story! Not even, really. Twelve feet.”

“Onto gravel. At a full sprint. And that’s assuming we make it.”

“This was your idea! You saw it first! What gives?”

“I was joking. I didn’t think you’d actually drag us up here. And it’s scary. You’ve been obsessing over it for months, and it’s still scary. I don’t think more practice is going to make a difference. I think it’s time to admit that you’re never going to feel more ready than you do right now.”

“I don’t feel ready right now.”

“Yeah, that’s my point.”

For everyone else, the real trouble started on Tuesday.

It began with the morning bus ride, when Eichardt, making his way down the aisle, timed things so that the bus jerked into motion just as he was passing Timothy’s seat. He fell deliberately, driving an elbow squarely into the sixth grader’s gut. Conor and several other boys leaped to their feet at once, but the damage was already done—smirking, Eichardt sauntered on toward the back while Timothy doubled up, wheezing, tears streaming openly down his face. It wasn’t until they pulled into the parking lot that he was able to uncurl and take a full breath. Conor made sure to stay between him and the eighth grader as the bus emptied, but neither made a move toward the other. As they passed through the cars of the front circle, each went his separate way—Timothy to the library, Eichardt to the flagpole, and Conor to the open courtyard in between.

Of course, they first had to pass through the police cordon, which was in full effect once more—only this time, in addition to a quick check for drugs and weapons, each student’s hands and fingertips were being given a thorough examination. A blood-red eagle—the mascot of Central High—had been spray-painted across the front doors of the school during the night, and the cops were searching for traces of red paint. The janitor was hard at work, scrubbing away at the glass with chemicals, but much of the eagle was still visible.

The brazen act of vandalism triggered widespread resentment against the new Central students, many of whom had apparently been rather unpleasant the day before. Every time Conor stepped out into the hallways for class change, there was a new story of someone being cornered in a bathroom or hurled into a locker door. It didn’t help that the newcomers were the only kids in the whole school without uniforms—each time one of them tried to disappear into a crowd, that crowd solidified, bouncing the unfortunate student back into the arms of their pursuers. By the middle of the day, they were moving around the campus in packs, bristling at anyone who looked their way.

Meanwhile, the classes themselves were almost surrealistically normal, as if the teachers were trying not to think about it—or trying not to let them think about it. Conor’s homeroom turned out to be math; Mrs. Atkins introduced him to the rest of the group, most of whom he’d known since kindergarten, and assigned him a seat at a table with Holt. They left for English together, and on the way were told by a breathless Eddie that someone had somehow filled a locker in the civics wing so full of shaving cream that it had poured out of the vents and covered ten square feet of floor.

Conor had been looking forward to English, but it turned out to be far less interesting than…whatever the word was, for what had happened the day before. When they entered the room, the words KEEP GOING were written in foot-high letters on the board, and those students already seated were bent over their papers, working diligently. Apart from a whispered bless you when Holt sneezed and lending an eraser to Jennifer, Conor said nothing the whole period.

Then it was off to Spanish, his first elective, and after that Science, Social Studies, and Math, all in a row. They were getting into actual work, now, and Conor went up to the teacher at the end of each class with a folder to collect papers for Ashleigh. Back out in the halls, he overheard a rumor that Mr. Sykes had already suspended fifteen of the new Central students, for their own protection—no, it was fifteen Bulldogs, as the East Binder students were suddenly referring to themselves, and the Central kids were all in the nurse’s office. No, it wasn’t fifteen, it was only three—two Bulldogs and an Eagle—but the police had taken all three of them away in handcuffs.

“Ándale,” said Holt. “That’s right—one of them had a grenade, and the other two were fighting with knives dipped in the juice of the bullshit berry.” He winked, and Conor laughed. They listened to fewer rumors after that, but there was no denying that at least a few of the stories had a kernel of truth in them. Conor had spotted Mr. Sykes when he passed the office on his way back from the electives building, and the grey-haired AP was talking in low, serious tones with Officer Grimsley, the man who’d taken Conor’s statement about Guyler two days before.

Now, it was lunchtime. Conor, unwilling to tolerate Eddie’s idiocy, split off from the others and sat down at a table with a group of eighth graders he didn’t know. Instantly, it was as if he’d built a wall around himself—the older boys ignored him completely, and when one of them spilled his drink next to Conor’s tray, he wiped it up without speaking, as if Conor and his food were simply part of the furniture.

He had slipped the leatherbound journal into his bookbag, that morning, and he opened it now, setting it on the table beside him as he ate. He had thought he might use the book to keep in touch with Ashleigh, during his suspension—with no phone or computer for the next two weeks, and only a couple of minutes each afternoon to drop off the other boy’s homework, there was no time for a real conversation. He’d figured he could write in it during the day, and leave it with Ashleigh overnight, collecting it from Timothy on the morning bus ride along with Ashleigh’s completed assignments.

It had seemed like an excellent plan, at breakfast. But now, he hesitated, the blank page stark and vast and intimidating. He’d opened it up half a dozen times that morning, but each time put it away without writing anything. There was at once too much and too little to say, and he wasn’t sure he knew how to write down the things that mattered.

Maybe start with “Dear Ashleigh.”

But even that turned out not to be simple. As he had the night before, he wrote Dear, then Hey, then Hi, and finally What’s up? before giving up and settling on Ash. Then he moved his pencil to the next line, where it hovered, waiting.

A minute passed. Conor squeezed his eyes shut.

Maybe trying to write during lunch was a mistake.

The sheer volume of noise made it almost impossible to think—even from thirty feet away, the sound of Holt singing ostentatiously into an imaginary microphone was completely swallowed up. Setting the pencil down, Conor took a sip of chocolate milk.

The problem was, there really weren’t any other good times left. There was Activity (where he wouldn’t be able to bring the little book), and then Art, homeroom, and the bus ride home. He could try during Social Studies tomorrow, maybe—

No. It has to be today.

He paused, taken aback by the forcefulness of the thought.

It…doesn’t?

It didn’t have to be any time at all. He would see Ashleigh later that afternoon, in person. The book was just in case…

He felt another twinge.

In case?

In case what?

Slowly, as if approaching a wild animal, Conor felt his way around the edges of the thought, asked himself—gently—what might be inside of it.

It’s okay, he whispered, feeling simultaneously silly and deadly serious. It’s okay.

Bit by bit, his mind began to offer up fragments to itself, pieces of the puzzle that had been plaguing him since Sunday. The other boy’s refusal to talk outside the bus, and his brief but undeniable cold shoulder outside Mr. Sykes’s office. The way he still hadn’t responded to any of Conor’s calls or messages. The fact that he’d slept outside on Sunday night, and not at Conor’s house, the way Conor would have expected him to, if he ever needed to not-go-home for some reason…

The way he’d turned and run, when he saw Conor coming around the corner of the gym.

It’s in case he’s decided he’s just not going to talk to me at all.

A part of him scoffed that he was being paranoid, dramatic. But he recognized in that thought a hint of performance, audience-awareness—

But there’s no one watching.

No one to look cool in front of, no need to pretend, inside his own head, that he wasn’t worried…

Which I am.

The very idea that Ashleigh might have cut him off, might be refusing to talk to him—

(And refusing to acknowledge it, either, pretending like it wasn’t happening, leaning into plausible deniability, Conor knew exactly how the conversation would go, could hear the words in his mind—what are you talking about, stop overreacting—)

Hey, um. What? Can we, like, slow the fuck down?

Conor took a deep breath.

Then another.

Then a third.

Okay, yes, maybe he was being paranoid, some part of him jumping to conclusions. Maybe he would knock on Ashleigh’s door in a couple of hours and everything would be—

Well. Not normal, surely. But fine. Maybe everything was fine.

But—

But something had spooked him. Spooked the animal parts of him, the parts that knew things without knowing why, without being able to justify them. Some part of him was scared, and it hadn’t been scared on Sunday—had become scared, in the subsequent day and a half, as Ashleigh’s silence stretched.

Conor looked down at the empty page.

What if…

(He swallowed.)

What if Ashleigh really didn’t want to talk?

A sudden hush made him look up, and a flash of movement caught his eye. A few tables away, over on the high school side, three boys in the blue-and-gold sweatshirts of the East Binder uniform were looming menacingly over a fourth wearing jeans and a t-shirt. The fourth boy was seated, but he tried now to rise, and as he did so, one of the Bulldogs put a hand on his chest and shoved, sending him crashing backwards to the floor, his tray and food flying.

Fast.

The teachers were already on their feet, had already been closing in, and in another heartbeat they were there, hustling the three antagonists away and helping the fallen boy up. But the damage was already done, had been done in seconds, rescue or no.

(Conor was surprised to see that none of the other Central students were nearby, or had rallied to the boy’s defense, but a moment later he understood: the victim had the scrawny, underfed look of an outcast, and had been eating lunch alone, without protectors or allies.)

(Like me, Conor realized.)

He tugged anxiously at the neck of his sweatshirt.

Rule number one: protect yourself.

Incidents like the one he’d just witnessed happened every week, if not every day, and the standard advice was simple: never be alone.

Picking up his pencil, he began to write, thinking as little as possible, putting the words down as soon as they came to mind. When he reached the bottom of the first page, he stopped, scribbled his name, and read back over it.

Ash,

I hope your doing ok at home. I cant call or message you bc I’m grounded bc I didn’t leave a note when I went to try to meet you yesterday before school. I did try to meet you just like I tryed to call you. We got to talk about Gyler and stuff. I dont know what your mom knows my parents know most of it, there not too mad tho. I’m not mad ether so if you thot I was dont worry. I’m just worreid about you (not to be gay) and I dont know what happend.

School is ok. I found out were your locker is and its pretty close to mine. We have English together and I’m pretty sure your in pe with me to. Tim got hurt on the bus but I guess you already know that by now but it wasnt a big fight. Sorry. Ill try to watch out for him better. PS I cleaned up the mess we left and put it all in a BAG. Let me know if you wont any of it back or Ill just throw it away. Write me back if you wont. Ill come get this in the morning b4 school

Conor

He sighed. It was not at all what he wanted. He had hoped to convey concern—respect—understanding—forgiveness—all of the things his father had given him, in their conversation the night before. Most of all, he had wanted to show the other boy that things were the same—that he was fine, they were fine—that there was no need for this to be different than any of the other misadventures they’d survived together.

But the right words hadn’t come, and in their place was this stumbling, disjointed mess. It made him uneasy, because he couldn’t work out why all of this was suddenly so hard.

The bell rang.

Might as well give it to him.

It wasn’t like doing it over was going to produce anything better. At the very least, it was an honest attempt—a gesture that Ashleigh would hopefully recognize, and respond to. An uncomfortable distance had opened up between them, when they found themselves on opposite sides of the Gap, and Conor was determined to throw a line across before either of them got used to it.

Closing the book, he stood, joining the crush as he threw away his trash and replaced his tray. Two more hours, he thought. Just two more hours, and he would be on his way home.

Of course, he’d have to share a bus with Eichardt to get there, and once he did, he had little to look forward to except yardwork and homework until he fell asleep and everything started all over again. With any luck, he’d see Ashleigh, but with a half-mile walk each way, he would have at most something like three minutes to actually talk. Not so much two hours as two weeks, really—then, hopefully, things would start to return to normal, become bearable again.

But two hours first.

There was no circle of onlookers outside of bus 134, and Conor permitted himself a small sigh of relief. He spotted Timothy and Eichardt in the crowd—they were far apart and seemed to be ignoring each other, but he maneuvered quietly between them anyway.

Most of East Binder was split between high school and middle, the quad a vast buffer zone between them. Some places, like the cafeteria and the electives building, brought the two groups close, but there was still segregation, supervision—structure.

Not so, with the buses. Here, and here alone, sixth graders rubbed shoulders with seniors without guards or chaperones. There was structure, but it was emergent rather than engineered—the law of the jungle, the fragile equilibrium of the frontier.

Among the middle schoolers, perhaps half rode the buses, the other half dropped off by one parent or another. East Binder was not in a particularly wealthy part of the county, but maybe half of the high schoolers drove their own cars, and most of the rest caught rides with them.

Thus, the only older students still on the bus were those who were too poor, too reckless, and too antisocial to get to school any other way. They were massively outnumbered by the middle schoolers and the underclassmen, but they were the ones who set the grid—everyone else just fell into place.

This year, there were only four seniors, and they claimed the wide back bench as their own. Immediately in front of them sat the small handful of juniors (with the druggies and the dealers on the left side, where they couldn’t be seen as well by the driver). Conor supposed that outside of East Binder, those would be the most dangerous—two of them at least were rumored to be actual gang members, and he had watched with his own eyes as one of the seniors had quietly dropped a knife out of the window last year, when the old AP had boarded the bus.

But in practical terms, the oldest kids were nothing to worry about. None of them had the slightest interest in terrorizing little kids—even for the bullies among them, the novelty had worn off, and they largely kept to themselves, aloof and disinterested.

Of more concern was the next tier, the posers and wannabes who clustered on the border of the older kids’ territory. They were the ones most likely to cause trouble—picking fights, floating from seat to seat in search of prey. They tended to work a year down, trying to impress those above by intimidating those below—tenth graders picking on ninth graders, who in turn picked on eighth.

Eichardt was among them—was one of the smallest and youngest, and thus keenly motivated to show off, to appear tough. Conor watched as the eighth grader carefully chose a seat in the middle of a near-empty section in the rear third of the bus. The choice was deliberate—they always were. The bullies spread themselves out, leaving open spaces beside themselves to draw their victims in, waiting for the overflow from the crowded front to provide them with entertainment. The sixth graders were in particular peril—usually the last to board the bus and (this early in the year) the least aware of the danger, and almost never able to force someone else to scoot over and give them room.

A few found shelter at the very front, in the seats closest to the driver, where no bully bothered to operate. It was the loser’s haven, the province of those who valued safety and comfort over social status, and they sat three to a seat in awkward quiet. The ones more sensitive to the stigma ventured slightly further, joining the remaining middle schoolers and underclassmen in the center of the bus—the normal kids, who had no beef with anyone and were just trying to get home.

Conor had settled himself in the sixth row, his usual spot—close enough to the front for peace, but not so close as to invite mockery. Ashleigh’s place beside him, against the window, was empty. He could hear Eichardt’s voice behind him, overloud, telling outrageously filthy jokes, and he listened with half an ear as he peered out the window, looking for Timothy.

The younger boy still hadn’t boarded, and as Conor counted the remaining seats, he began to feel a rising sense of urgency. Yesterday’s ride had been quiet, Ashleigh’s bizarre behavior serving as a kind of shield, keeping the eighth grader at bay. But his stunt that morning was proof that the effect had worn off—given the chance, he would make trouble for the younger boy.

And he was likely to have that chance. As the line outside the bus dwindled, more and more of the seats in the safe zone disappeared. Conor held the spot beside him as long as he could, but eventually had to yield it to another seventh grader, who grumbled about not getting the aisle before sliding sullenly in. By the time Timothy came through the door and started down the row, there were only a few obvious places left, and all of them near the back.

C’mon, Timothy, make somebody scoot over.

(He didn’t say it aloud, of course.)

But the younger boy showed no sign of slowing. Glancing backward, Conor saw a feral grin on Eichardt’s face as he watched his victim’s approach. Making a swift decision, Conor stood up, blocking Timothy’s progress down the aisle. “Here,” he said, gesturing. “Take my seat.”

Timothy stopped short, frowning. His eyes flickered down at the seat, then back to where Eichardt was waiting, then back up to meet Conor’s. “Why?” he asked suspiciously.

Conor dropped his voice to a murmur. “Are you kidding? Just take it, thank me later. I’ll sit back there with the sharks.”

Timothy’s frown deepened. “No,” he said flatly.

“What? Why?”

“Move.”

“But—”

“Move.”

“Timothy—”

“Move.”

Conor hesitated, and the pair of students waiting in the aisle behind Timothy joined in, complaining. In a moment, Conor was shouted down, and he sank back into his seat, turning his head to watch as the sixth grader carried on down the aisle.

“I’ll sit there, if you want to move so bad,” the boy next to him said.

Conor ignored him.

A prickle of foreboding worked its way up his spine as Timothy headed straight for Eichardt. The eighth grader was sitting longways, by himself, his back against the window, arm draped casually over the seat back. Timothy stopped directly in front of his outstretched feet and turned toward him, unshouldering his bookbag. Conor had to crane his neck to see, since the two other kids behind him had kept going, too, and were now blocking the view.

Timothy said nothing—just stared, until Eichardt opened his mouth. Then Timothy spoke too, quickly, his words cutting across the older boy’s.

“What do you—”

“So, you gonna move your legs, or not?”

Eichardt’s grin slipped, but he recovered quickly. “Sorry, kiddo. Afraid this seat’s taken.” He smiled again, crocodilian, and crossed his legs, his shoes brushing against Timothy’s knees.

“Bull,” Timothy said, and around him, conversations fell quiet. The two kids in the aisle each took a step backward, distancing themselves from the situation.

“What’d you say, shithead?”

“You heard me, IEP. I said bull.” Timothy’s voice was clear and unafraid, and he glanced at the faces around him, all of which were watching, rapt. “I got an older brother—you think I don’t know how this dumb shit works? You find out what I want, hold it over my head, make me beg and cry—that how you make yourself look big and bad? Playing keep-away like a kindergartener?”

Eichardt’s face darkened. That was how it worked—usually. But now Eichardt was the one who looked small, and pathetic. It was a cheat, but a good one. The other bullies were laughing now, and not at Timothy.

“Anyway,” the sixth grader continued. “I was just asking. There’s a seat here, and there’s three of us waiting to sit. But if your ass is so fat you need the whole thing all to your—”

Too far. Eichardt surged to his feet, lunging at the younger boy with hands curled into claws, snarling with rage.

But Timothy was ready. At exactly the right moment, when Eichardt’s body left the seat, he swung the bookbag, slamming it into the bully’s face. With so little room to work with, he couldn’t muster enough force to do any real damage, but it completely broke the older boy’s momentum, throwing him off balance, knocking him back the way he’d come—against the window, leaving a space by the aisle.

Quick as an adder, Timothy slipped into the seat, dropping his bookbag onto the floor. “Hey, thanks!” he chirped, just before Eichardt lunged again, grabbing him by the throat and sending both of them tumbling out between the seats.

Conor was helpless, cut off by the two students still standing in the way. But they weren’t helpless, and neither was the tall black boy who had been sitting by himself in the next row back. In a second, the two combatants were separated, and suddenly the tables were turned as the black boy pinned Eichardt up against the window by his throat, leaning across the seat back between them.

Everyone froze. The black boy wasn’t wearing a uniform—he was from Central, and an unknown quantity. A couple of the older East Binder boys began to rise, but he stopped them with a glare and a raised finger. “Hold on, there, folks,” he said, his voice an exaggerated drawl. “Let’s just everybody calm the fuck down, shall we?”

He looked to be sixteen at least, thin but tightly muscled, with hair in a short afro and fingers that spread easily across Eichardt’s neck, touching the glass behind on either side. Conor’s eyes flickered up toward the driver, but the whole fight had taken place under the radar, and even now the standoff wasn’t clearly visible in the grownup’s mirrors.

Slowly, the boy released his hold on Eichardt’s throat, putting a hand on the eighth grader’s chest instead, pressing him back against the window. “It seems to me our little man here just demonstrated that there’s plenty of room in your seat, don’t you think?” He glanced down at Timothy, who was frozen along with everyone else, still sitting on the floor of the aisle. “Now, I’m kind of new around here—”

The boy broke off, looking over his shoulder. Following his gaze, Conor saw that he had locked eyes with the largest senior in the back.

(The one who had ditched the knife the year before, and was probably carrying one right now.)

Something passed between them, and the senior lifted a finger dismissively, as if to say go ahead. Raising his chin respectfully in acknowledgement, the boy turned back to Eichardt.

“—but where I’m from, it’s considered rude to jump a guy who’s just trying to make friends, know what I’m saying?”

Eichardt had no response. He sat rigid, as though cast from iron, only his eyes moving as they darted wildly from side to side. Conor was struck by how small the eighth grader looked, stripped of his usual bluster—much smaller than the Central student, and no different from any other scared little kid.

Timothy, on the other hand, spoke up in a ringing voice. “Hey, little man, did I ask for your fucking help?”

The bus grew suddenly airless as the spectators sucked in a collective breath. Slowly, the black boy’s head turned toward the sixth grader on the floor, his expression blank and unreadable.

Every fucking time with you—

But there was no explosion. Instead, the black boy burst into a laugh of deep, rich amusement. “Well, well,” he said, looking around as though inviting everyone else to join in on the joke. “Seems our little man don’t want a hand. All right, all right—no hand it is.”

He pulled his own away from Eichardt’s chest, raising it in mock surrender, and settled back into his seat. “You two can work this shit out on your own, then.”

All eyes turned to Eichardt, who seemed to search among them, looking for sympathy or support. Finding neither, he cut his losses.

“Fucking psychos,” he spat. “You and your retard brother both. It’s just a seat—who gives a shit?”

But the words were hollow, the retreat obvious, and the bully’s face burned as he turned to stare out the window, muttering balefully under his breath.

Timothy stood and took his seat, carefully keeping his distance. The other two kids in the aisle quickly found places—nobody making any pretense at refusing to make room—and as the bus shuddered into motion, the tension in the air slowly, slowly, slowly drained away.

Conor continued to watch, sitting backwards in his seat, trying to decipher Timothy’s intentions. If the younger boy had been attempting to get Eichardt off his back, he had failed spectacularly—by the look on the bully’s face, if there had been fewer witnesses, Timothy might have found himself following Guyler into the hospital. The eighth grader hadn’t been beaten, merely outmaneuvered, and if anything, his anger was becoming more personal with each postponement of revenge.

…was that the point? To provoke him, goad him into some rash attack? Conor thought back to the words that Timothy had thrown at the older boy, how they had been perfectly chosen to work their way under his skin.

Younger brother.

More than once, Timothy’s best defense against Ashleigh had been to push him over the edge just before their mother walked into the room.

But a strategy that worked with family was risky when applied to outsiders. Ashleigh loved Timothy, and so even in his blindest fury there was a check on his capacity for violence. Eichardt had no such built-in safety, and there was no mother to intervene. And Timothy had now been the instrument of the eighth grader’s humiliation three times running.

A memory of flashing lights rose in Conor’s mind, and he swallowed.

“Hey—little man. My name’s Devon. What’s yours?”

Conor’s ears perked up.

“Listen, little man, you want me to stop calling you little man, I’m down, but you gotta give me something to work with.”

There was no arguing with that.

“Timothy.”

“And your brother, the dude with the chip on his shoulder. Where’s he?”

So Devon saw that.

(Or heard about it later, maybe.)

“Suspended,” Timothy growled.

“Ain’t that the shit. How long?”

Timothy said nothing.

“C’mon, don’t be all puffed up and grouchy. I’m trying to have a conversation. Was it two weeks?”

No answer.

“Bet it was two weeks. Old man Sykes, he loves that two-week suspension. ‘Discretion,’ and all that.”

Devon peered at Timothy, who continued to stare straight ahead.

“Maybe you don’t know him yet, and that’s why you’re not laughing,” the older boy said. “Sorry. Just thought I’d share some sympathy with another victim of the system. Me, I only spent twenty-seven weeks in school last year.”

“My brother’s not a victim,” Timothy spat. “He deserved it.”

“Hey, look—it talks!” Devon grinned. “Big brother done pissed you off, huh? What’d he do—talk too much?”

Timothy bristled. “What do you want?”

“Like I said. Just making conversation.”

“Well, get to the point.”

“Ain’t no point, friend. Small talk. I say shit you don’t care about, then you say shit I don’t care about. You know it’s supposed to get up to eighty degrees on Friday? All y’all motherfuckers in them damn sweatshirts are fucked.”

“I don’t like small talk.”

“Oh? Well then, how about big talk? Like how this motherfucker here is sitting there scheming about how he’s going to whoop your ass first chance he gets?”

Eichardt, who had been staring out the window the whole time, stiffened noticeably.

“Yeah, that’s right, you—what’s your name, kid?”

Reluctantly, Eichardt turned so that Devon could see his face. “Craig,” he said sullenly.

“Craig, you gonna tell me I’m lying?”

Eichardt said nothing.

“Hey, Craig, let me ask you something. If I told you not to lay a finger on little man’s head, would you do it anyway?” Devon leaned forward as he spoke, his eyes glittering.

Eichardt said nothing.

“Yeah, that’s what I thought. But I haven’t told you that—have I, Craig? Funny, huh?” He turned back to Timothy. “Want me to tell him that, Tim?”

Timothy glowered.

“Boy, you two don’t ever shut up, do you?” There was no answer, and Devon laughed. “Well, shit. Looks like I got nobody to talk to today. Let me know, you change your mind—I saw the weather report for the whole week this morning, and I could go on for fucking hours.”

Still laughing, he sat back again, his eyes drifting.

Timothy said nothing. Eichardt said nothing. Conor watched as they rode in silence, surrounded by the chatter of the other riders. Bit by bit, the bus emptied out, and soon it came to a stop at the corner by Timothy’s house. Without so much as a glance at either of the two older boys, the sixth grader stood, shouldering his bag and pushing his way down the aisle. Conor watched him go, wrestling with indecision—

One last drag of the rake, a final flutter of ochre and amber, and the backyard was clean. Conor leaned on the slim shaft of the rake handle, feeling it flex as he looked out over the ragged, abused lawn. It was six thirty, and the sun had already slipped below the trees; he had worked for nearly two and a half hours to get the whole thing done.

He heaved a sigh, and his breath caught in his throat, turning into a cough. After leaving Ashleigh’s house, he had run the half-mile back, hoping to make it home within the time limit of twenty minutes. His parents were at work, and would never have known either way, but with so much else off-balance in his life, he wanted to avoid the lie if he could.

The run had taken him barely five minutes, but it had left him completely winded; upon tagging his mailbox, he had needed to walk around the yard three times before his breathing slowed enough for him to drink a glass of water. Another half hour had passed before his heart rate fell all the way back to normal, and even now his chest felt dry and tight.

It bothered him, to discover this weakness in himself. He had never been athletic, not even a little—on the soccer team, they’d dropped him at fullback in second grade and left him there ever since. His dad had tried to interest him in running and swimming and even a little light weightlifting, but none of it had stuck. His body worked just fine, as far as he was concerned, and he had never seen the point in fitness for its own sake—

Until the warehouse.

The warehouse, which had been his first climb, and his first failure, Ashleigh’s laughter ringing in his ears, good-natured but still somehow cutting.

He had come back alone the next day, and again the day after that, pushing the limits of his meager strength, working each impossible step until it became possible—

—and then working each one further, until he almost couldn’t get them wrong. It took three weeks, but the look on Ashleigh’s face the next time they happened to pass by had made it all worth it.

And then Ashleigh insisted that Conor do it again, show him how it was done, and the course of their summer was set. Their next triumph had been a night mission, at the monstrous, Victorian mansion that had been converted into an old folks’ home—Ashleigh had almost gotten them caught when he slipped and came crashing down onto a balcony just a few feet away from an old man sleeping with his window open.

After that had come the Raymond tower downtown, and then they’d begun exploring the school—which had led to the discovery of the gap—and in the intervening months, Conor had come to think of himself as tough, capable, ready for anything.

Yet now, a simple flat-ground run had brought him almost to his knees, leaving him weak and shaking so that hours later the chill autumn air still burned in the back of his throat. It was embarrassing, shameful—like being back at the very beginning. The half-mile had beaten him just as thoroughly as any too-high wall or too-large jump, and he felt an almost primal need to do something about it.

Tomorrow, though.

He had learned something about weakness, over the long, arduous summer—knew that there was nothing to be gained from punishing his heart and lungs when they’d already been pushed to the limit. Plenty of time for that later, after he had recovered—when he could test himself slowly and deliberately, map the boundaries of this newfound frailty.

In the meantime, though, he itched with unspent energy, his limbs thrumming with nervous tension. The last two days had left him feeling caged and useless, unable to do anything but pace. He needed exhaustion—the weariness of overworked muscles, arms like lead and legs like butter, the ache that worked its way deep into his body, drowning out his thoughts, turning him into a lower sort of animal.

Leaning the rake against the deck, he strode to the edge of the backyard, where the roots of the old oak tree spread out like the tentacles of a sea monster, rising up from the hard, packed earth. Squinting at them critically in the fading light, he chose one that was roughly two inches thick and maybe twice that off the ground. He stepped lithely onto it, bringing his legs together so that he perched like a bird, balanced on the balls of his feet with the root stretching out to either side.

Balanced, but not steady—for several tense seconds he swayed back and forth, bowing and straightening, his knees bending and unbending as he fought to find a stable equilibrium. Eventually, he managed to coax his body into the ideal position—hips slightly back, knees slightly bent, chest and arms forward as if he were scratching behind the ears of an invisible dog. Only then did his movements slow, all but the tiniest adjustments shrinking away as he focused on his target, another thin root emerging from the soil three paces away.

For a single heartbeat, he stood completely still, everything in perfect balance as he took a deep breath, an archer on the draw.

Then he jumped.

It all happened in the blink of an eye, but to Conor it was like a dance, like a poem, each piece of it distinct and necessary, combining into a whole that was greater than the sum of its parts.

It began with his arms, which swung backwards like the hammer of a gun being cocked as he sank deeper into his half-crouch, tipping forward until it seemed he would fall on his face.

Then—as he passed the point of no return—everything uncoiled, his arms reversing direction as his legs fired explosively, driving him up into the air, his whole body stringing out along a line drawn at forty-five degrees. He rose as he flew forward, and at the peak of his jump he drew his knees up and in, flashing into a cannonball before once again reversing his movement, this time extending his feet down and out toward his target. His legs were at maximum extension at the exact moment when he made contact with the root, touching down just behind his toes, and he immediately began to compress, absorbing the impact with the muscles of his legs, his ankles, knees, and hips all hinging as he shrank back into a ball, ending in a deep crouch atop the second root.

And that was the end, and it would have been perfect, except that he came in slightly too low, had just a hair too much forward momentum and didn’t adequately compensate. Windmilling his arms, he lost his balance and had to step down onto the ground in front of the root.

Turning, he stepped back up, facing the way he’d come, and in a moment he found his balance, jumped, and fell again, this time because he’d come in too short and ended up staggering backward.

Again he stood, again he jumped, and again he fell.

And again.

And again.

And again.

He was seven jumps in before he made his first good landing, but he didn’t pause to celebrate—only spun around without stepping down from the root, wobbled, and jumped again, this time arriving with one leg too far forward, so that a jolt of pain stabbed through his heel.

Four jumps later, he stuck his second landing, and this time he found the rhythm, making two more before he lost concentration and fell again.

And again.

And again.

Slowly, Conor felt his mind recede, his body’s reflexes gradually assuming control as he sank into the repetition—jump, turn, balance, jump, turn, balance. A dull burn kindled in his thighs and in the arches of his feet as he passed his fifth success in twenty attempts.

I’ll go until I’ve stuck thirty, he decided. Either that, or until it was too dark to see, which wouldn’t be too much longer. Already the crickets were chirping, and the cars driving past on the street had their headlights on.

He landed another, but badly—heavily—wobbling dangerously on the root, his arms swinging wildly. Grimacing, he added it to the count of failures and turned around again.

Jump.

Thursday I’ll work on rolls, after I finish stacking the firewood.

Jump.

Friday I can do switchovers and slipthroughs.

Jump.

And I’ll work on running every day. I’ll get off at home, run to Ashleigh’s, and run back.

Jump.

It had quickly become apparent—once they started fucking around together every day—that Conor and Ashleigh approached their little adventures from very different directions. For Ashleigh, it seemed to be all about going places—places that no one else had been, places no one else could reach. The other boy climbed for the thrill of the challenge and the joy of exploration; once a particular route had been broken, he saw no point in going back and doing it again.

But Conor had never quite gotten past the sting of that very first failure. He remembered all too well the bitter frustration of seeing the way up, knowing it, understanding exactly how it could be done, and coming up short. On that very first attempt, he’d reached a point where his fingers simply could not hang on anymore, no matter how hard he’d willed them to.

It was something that Ashleigh, who was a natural athlete, would never really understand. In those three long weeks when Conor had gone back to the warehouse over and over again, he hadn’t just made it to the top. He had become the kind of person who could, consistently and reliably. The climb had changed him, physically and mentally, and the process of transformation had awakened in him a hunger for more.

It had taken him three weeks to reach the warehouse roof. Ashleigh did it in ten minutes flat.

Unacceptable.

And so, Conor began to push himself. On their second climb, he started taking deliberate detours whenever the route became too easy, using his fear as a gauge to make sure he stayed close to the edge of his ability.

(The little copy of Ashleigh he carried around in his head had been confused, had asked him wait, isn’t the whole point of practicing to stop being scared? But Conor had answered no—the point was to push the limits of his fear, to make it so that the things he was almost too scared to do were better things. Fear was not the enemy. Fear was part of it.)

As the summer wore on, he sought out ever-trickier challenges, following daunting paths to hidden places, striving always to recapture the strain and uncertainty of that very first climb. When the natural obstacles weren’t difficult enough, he handicapped himself, skipping handholds or carrying rocks, limiting himself to only three limbs.

(What if your arm was broken? What if your ankle was sprained? What if you had to carry something, or you only had one eye, or one shoe? What if everything was wet?)

It was some time after their third climb—while they were planning for the fourth—that a light had gone on in Conor’s brain, and he had made the obvious connection. Kicking off of walls, vaulting over planters, hauling himself up and over ledges—each of these skills was cropping up over and over again, as the most basic and straightforward solution to a certain category of problem. And each of these skills could be strengthened independently of any particular route or climb—could be practiced separately, at ground level, where he would have the freedom to experiment, working out the kinks without the threat of falling.

It was a revolutionary shift in his thinking, no less powerful for being—in retrospect—kind of obvious. Like rock climbing, or skateboarding, or even soccer and basketball—there were basic ingredients to this thing he was discovering, building-block movements that made up the bulk of every route. If he could master them, it would dramatically reduce the time and energy he was spending solving the same problems over and over again, would let him focus his attention on the unique and interesting aspects of each new challenge. It would mean stepping back from the edge, where he’d done all of his growing so far, but it would allow him to return to that edge with a much more mature vocabulary, ready to tackle significantly harder problems.

It was then that he had first imagined being Batman.

Oh, he’d been thinking about Batman all along. That’s how the whole thing had gotten started—they were walking down the street, each arguing his favorite hero’s merits, and Ashleigh had pointed to the warehouse roof. “Look,” he’d said. “Spiderman could climb that thing in ten seconds flat.”

“So could Batman!”

“Not if he couldn’t use his grappling hook.”

“If Batman can’t use his grappling hook, then Spiderman can’t use his web slingers.”

“And Spiderman wins. Climbing powers. No way Batman could climb that with nothing but his hands.”

Conor had disagreed, and they’d gone back and forth, their voices growing more and more heated until he found himself standing on a dumpster on his tiptoes, straining to reach a rusted metal windowsill. He’d made it to the sill, but not to the ledge above that, and Ashleigh had claimed victory—until Conor brought him back three weeks later.

He had pretended to be the caped crusader ever since, romping across the rooftops as Ashleigh fired imaginary webs after him. But it wasn’t until his epiphany that he really felt it, could imagine himself actually standing in the Batcave, plotting out a training regimen, trying to anticipate every physical skill that he would need to win the fight against the Gotham underground.

Combat training, sure—Batman needed that, just like any other superhero. But what fascinated Conor most—what drew him to Batman particularly—was the way he used the city as his ally, flitting from rooftop to rooftop, lurking in the shadows, laying traps and snares in blind corners, drawing the enemy in. Making the environment work for him, so that every brick and pipe became a tool, a natural extension of his utility belt. Batman was everywhere at once, through a combination of gadgets, brains, and—most importantly—the strength and agility of his body.

And—crucially—he got there by practicing. Not by being an alien, or getting bitten by a spider, or being granted three wishes. In most of the stories Conor knew, Batman’s training was centered on punching bags and sparring drills, on endless sets of pushups—but with a thrill of excitement, he realized that there had to be another side to it, a hidden one that the movies and comics never mentioned. Conor imagined Bruce Wayne standing before a ten foot wall, running at it, throwing himself at the top, and scrambling over—and then doing it again, nine hundred and ninety-nine more times.

(Sure, it was all make-believe—the stunts Batman pulled weren’t realistic to begin with, which was probably why they didn’t bother to show him training for them in the first place. But hadn’t Conor done something similar, without even meaning to? Haphazardly, unmethodically, without any goal beyond reaching the top of a particular building—and yet still, he’d grown. Improved. It had made a difference.)

And if that was all it took to get better at climbing, what about the rest of the things Batman did—popping over fences, jumping off rooftops, leaping over cars and diving through windows? In that moment, Conor’s eyes had been opened, and the world around him had transformed. Where before there had been a static and empty environment, there was now a vast mosaic of possibilities, a never-ending cascade of what-ifs stretching far beyond the streets and sidewalks he had previously thought of as the world.

What if he needed to hide on top of that sculpture?

What if he had to break into that fifth-story window?

What if the bad guy was on the other side of that fence, that ledge, that truck, that gap? What if it was a chase, a race, and every second mattered?

It was his Bat vision, and once it clicked on, he couldn’t turn it off. From then on, Conor found himself constantly evaluating the buildings and structures around him, eyes drawn to odd quirks in architecture and landscaping, always asking himself: what would Batman do?

(And could I do it, too?)

He began practicing methodically, drilling the core movements over and over, building a repertoire of techniques that he could call upon at any time. He sought out unusual obstacles and lingered with them for days, crisscrossing them back and forth, boiling down the various possible approaches until he found the most efficient methods—the ones that maximized speed, or minimized effort, or were the most adaptable. He spent hours studying the movements of the squirrels in his backyard, the cats that ran through the neighborhood, the monkeys on Discovery Channel. Slowly, through trial and error, he developed his mantra—safety before simplicity, simplicity before speed, and silence wherever possible.

(And then the other rules, as he made mistakes, wisdom gained through blood and bruises.)

And all the while, he kept it secret, embarrassed. Because it was silly—wasn’t it? What would he say, if anyone stumbled upon him—dangling by one arm from a low-hanging branch, trying desperately to reach thirty Mississippi before his screaming muscles refused to hold any longer—oh, I’m training to be a crime fighter?

It was ridiculous. Exhilarating, intoxicating, the most alive he’d ever felt—but ridiculous. Only Ashleigh would understand, but Conor kept it secret even from him, waiting for the perfect moment to blow his friend away.

That moment came on a hot day in late June, when they’d gone to the park to play on the playground, he and Ashleigh and all of their other friends. Someone had started a game of freeze tag, and after three rounds in which Conor had evaded capture entirely, they made him it, and he’d started running, and forty-five seconds later the entire playground was still, every kid frozen.

He hadn’t been particularly fast—he had won the game by inches, in the closed jumble of the playground architecture, catching each victim on the scrambles as he floated over obstacles that made everyone else falter.

But inches made the difference. For a moment they’d all stared at him, unnerved, until Holt broke the silence, shouting “Es un monito—who are you, monkey boy, and what have you done with Conor?”

Then everyone had laughed, and as the last boy tagged started up the next round, Conor had walked quietly over to the benches and sat down to catch his breath, waiting for Ashleigh. Ten minutes later, they were gone, on their way to the bank where Conor’s favorite practice wall stood. They climbed it fifty times that afternoon before the manager came out and ran them off.

In the weeks that followed, it soon became clear that being Batman wasn’t really Ashleigh’s style. The other boy easily grew restless, was reluctant to drill, itched to move on to the next new thing.

But that was okay, because Conor didn’t have to do his serious work while they were together. It wasn’t long before he settled into a routine—skill sessions in the morning, then edgework with Ashleigh in the afternoon, the pair of them conquering the unknown. He quickly became the stronger of the two, his new discipline adding whipcord muscles to his slender frame, but that was okay, too—Ashleigh still had no fear, and so they remained matched, the other boy’s daring a perfect counterpart to Conor’s steady competence.

That was how they’d made it to the auditorium roof, their fourth major climb—Conor’s careful preparation, dangling off pipes for weeks as he built up his endurance, and Ashleigh’s refusal to quit, bombing up after him with every scrap of energy he had.

(It frustrated Conor sometimes—the way that Ashleigh, without putting in any work at all, was able to follow almost every trail that Conor blazed. But that was just Ashleigh. It wasn’t malicious—he never went anywhere Conor couldn’t follow, or mocked him for taking things slowly.)

Well, except for all that bullshit about how Spiderman doesn’t need to practice.

Conor paused in his jumping, teetering on the root as he wiped his brow. It was almost time to quit—the stars were coming out, and the shadows beneath the oak tree were deep and dark. Turning, he jumped again. Another miss.

Apparently Spiderman doesn’t need to do Binder’s Gap, either.

Conor frowned, shook his head, unsettled by the churlish thought. That’s not right, he told himself firmly. It was not a contest—seeing it as one was a good way to get himself hurt, to push himself into something he wasn’t ready for just because Ash could already do it. They weren’t rivals—they were partners.

Besides, the Gap wasn’t about practice. It was about fear, plain and simple. So what if Ashleigh had finally found something he was afraid of? Conor was afraid of plenty. There was no shame in that. Not when you were forty-eight feet above the ground. Rule number one was protect yourself—fear was what kept you alive.

He made one last attempt, barely able to see his landing in the thickening gloom, and smiled as his feet connected cleanly, his balance holding without a wobble. Eighteen, out of seventy-six. Not bad.

But not great, either. Fifty…four? No. Fifty-eight.

Fifty-eight misses—that was what mattered. Not how he stacked up to his friend, but that he’d fallen three times for every good landing he’d made. That was why he practiced. It wasn’t about ego, or adrenaline, or a sense of accomplishment—it was about perfection. That split second of certainty, before his feet even hit the target, that yes, this jump was good, was going to end exactly as it was supposed to.

It was a small thing, a silly thing to care about. But with everything else spiraling ever further out of his control, there was something nice about the promise of it—the idea that all it took was work.

Crossing out of the shadow of the trees and into the next yard, he stepped up to the low brick wall Mr. Baker had built around his air conditioning unit. Grabbing the top with his hands, he squatted down, lifted his feet one at a time, pressed them against the vertical surface so that he was dangling from his fingers in a tucked position, his butt mere inches above the grass. With a titanic heave, he pulled himself upward, hauling his head and chest over the top, his sneakers scraping as he scrambled to his feet and stood atop the narrow row of bricks. Then he reversed the movement, squatting to press his hands down on either side of his feet, kicking his legs out behind him, and lowering himself back into the tuck, careful not to dig his chest into the edge or let his feet touch the ground.

One.

Heave. Scramble. Squat. Drop.

Two.

Conor’s father was working in the shop when he came in with the rake, poring over a set of plans on the workbench. “Oh, hey,” he said, without looking up. “Finished with the leaves already?”

“All done,” Conor replied, setting the rake on its hook against the wall. He walked back to his father, peering over his shoulder. “New props?”

“Miniatures.”

The plans were for a scale model of a city, with buildings, streets, and cars all carefully marked out. Next to them was a large plywood board, covered in pencil marks. Conor squinted. It looked a little like a map.

His father was a corporate artist, part of a small production company. He worked in the physical design department, making sets and props for filming. Two years earlier, he had turned their garage into a workshop, filling it with wood and plaster, fabric and paint, so that he could spend more time at home. There was even a machine for injection-molding rubber and plastic, which Conor and Ashleigh had used over the summer to make the masks they were planning to wear for Halloween.

They had found the bag on the auditorium roof just a week earlier, and his mind was filled with the thrill of possibility, the idea that they might actually catch a real-life bad guy. They’d spent the night sketching and arguing and sketching some more, and had taken the picture to Conor’s father the next morning. A week later, the masks were cooling on a rack out in the workshop. Black for the Tracer, and red for Siphon—Ashleigh had pointed out that Tracer was actually already the name of an obscure villain in one of the Spiderman comics, but Conor kept it anyway.

They’d been planning to start work on the actual costumes once school started up again. There was a Halloween party at East Binder every year, and Ashleigh figured they had a shot at winning a prize in the competition.

Probably not going to happen now.

Conor set the masks aside. He was tempted, for a moment, to sink back into despair—into the grey fugue that had gripped him under the equipment shed and never really let go.

But the ache in his arms and legs kept him afloat, kept him from going under. It was the same ache he had felt a hundred times, over the summer—a familiar, friendly pain that spoke of better days to come. He had made progress today, if only in one insignificant corner of his life, and the next time he went out to do target jumps or topovers, they would come a little bit easier.

Picking up the masks again, he opened up his closet and set them on a high shelf.

No more.

Ever since Sunday, he’d been acting like a passenger, moving only in reaction, letting everyone else’s choices dictate his own. He’d been in limbo, waiting for things to go back to normal, and it was time to face the fact that this was normal—the new normal, whether he liked it or not. There wasn’t any point in dwelling on the year he wished he was having, when the real one was trying to knock his feet out from under him.

He closed the closet door, firmly.

They’re still there.

Still there, if Ashleigh asked about them. Still there, if the other boy came around. And if he didn’t…

What would Batman do?

Conor turned back to his desk, and sighed. Probably not a lab report, a current events questionnaire, and a worksheet on negative numbers. But at least those were things he understood, and knew how to deal with.

Settling into his chair, Conor opened up his folder, took out the first assignment, and began to write.
