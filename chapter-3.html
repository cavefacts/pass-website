The journal came back empty.

Timothy handed it to him on the bus on Wednesday morning, along with Ashleigh’s completed homework and a not-so-subtle hint about picking it up from Conor that afternoon. For the rest of the week, though, Conor continued to run the half-mile between their houses, depositing the little book and his friend’s assignments in a neat stack outside the locked bedroom door.

Each day, he wrote on the left-hand pages of the little leatherbound book, leaving the right-hand ones blank, and each morning he picked it up from Timothy and flipped through them, looking for an answer.

None came.

It was disheartening, but Conor refused to quit. Soon, he was bringing the little book with him to every single class, jotting down a constant stream of thoughts and notes as he dragged himself through the day.

Just until I think of something better.

Much of what he wrote was trivial—his classes were proving to be every bit as tedious as he’d feared, and he passed the time by writing jokes, transcribing gossip, sketching doodles of Jennifer and Emily and Caitlin. With a little effort, he found that he could imagine Ashleigh’s responses in detail, picture the other boy’s expression in his mind’s eye. He earned a silent lunch from Mr. Vernon for laughing out loud in the middle of his first science quiz, at a witticism that only he could hear.

It was a poor substitute for company, but it preserved his sense of connection, made the other boy’s absence slightly less unbearable. At times, the little book made him feel even closer to Ashleigh than he might have, normally—his real friend would have been with him in only two of his seven classes, but the imaginary one could go with him everywhere.

That was small comfort outside of the classroom, though, where things were settling into a tense and unpleasant routine. It didn’t take a vendetta like Eichardt’s to make you a target when the sixth, seventh, and eighth grade bathrooms were just a few dozen paces away from one another and all three grades changed classes at the same time. The teachers poked in their heads often enough to prevent serious trouble, but Conor still had to endure the typical taunts and shoves of the bigger kids, and he only narrowly avoided getting drawn into an actual fight when he stumbled across a trio of bullies during an ill-advised mid-class emergency. The three of them had a sixth grader cornered in one of the stalls, and once again Conor found himself torn between the moral imperative to do something and the sad reality that there were no viable options.

(He raised his hands, backed away slowly, and went back to class. He didn’t bother saying anything to the teacher—by the time any grownup could get there, it would all already be over, and he’d be branded a snitch for no one’s benefit.)

Another time, he lingered too long during a class change, and as he came out of the stall he saw two eighth graders making a surreptitious swap by the sinks, each quickly tucking away whatever the other had handed him. Conor kept his head down, and said nothing, but he could feel their eyes on the back of his neck, could not help his shoulders tensing as he washed his hands and left.

There were some bright spots to the chaos—Josh Miller, an eighth grader from Conor’s neighborhood, stole an eight-pack of crayons from the art room and took to holding one casually in his hand each time he walked down the hallway of the electives building, so that over the course of a week a crooked rainbow slowly appeared at waist height on the whitewashed cinderblock. Mr. Sykes was furious—Conor overheard him berating the teacher on hall duty, demanding that she explain how another two lines had materialized during the hour he’d been away patrolling the high school.

(It wasn’t as though Josh was hiding. He had greeted Conor with a grin and a wave, had gestured toward his outstretched arm and said out loud that he was “just doin’ a little vandalism.” Everybody knew. Everybody saw.)

Overall, though, things were not pleasant. One day the bathroom was covered in what looked like half a dozen entire rolls of toilet paper, unwound and soaked, a sagging spiderweb of pulp. Another, he heard soft, wet noises coming from a locked stall and bent over to see two pairs of legs, one kneeling. Once, his foot sent something tumbling on the sidewalk just outside the cafeteria, and on closer inspection, it was a metal spoon, folded back onto itself and scorched black on one side.

The hazing, too, was harsher than he remembered, perhaps a byproduct of the tension in the high school, where the covert war against the Central students had splintered into a murky free-for-all as Bulldog solidarity waned. Conor went back to eating lunch with Ashleigh’s fan club after he had his chocolate milk poured into his mashed potatoes, and he had to lend his Spanish homework to Sam Marlin when the other boy’s locker was broken into and trashed.

(“Why you?” he had asked. Sam had shrugged.)

Things were going especially poorly for Timothy, who was clearly suicidal and was continuing to provoke Eichardt at every turn. The older boy hadn’t tried anything on the bus since the second day, but he seemed to have memorized Timothy’s schedule, Nicholas quietly confirming that the bully was popping up outside of the other boy’s classes every time the bell rang.

At first, Conor tried doing the same, but Timothy gave him such looks of withering fury that he quickly stopped. Instead, he watched from a distance as Timothy’s clothes and possessions began to show signs of abuse, and his face hardened into a permanent scowl. From time to time, Conor overheard him spitting contemptuous poison at the eighth grader in the courtyard, in the hallways, outside of the cafeteria—any time when there were too many witnesses for Eichardt to respond with violence. But each of those fleeting victories was followed by a savage round of private retribution—by the middle of the second week, the younger boy had had to buy a new uniform sweatshirt, and had quit carrying a lunchbox altogether.

It was a jarring rhythm, in and out of danger, the high alert of the hallways and the stultifying safety of the classrooms. Conor could see it in the bearing of his peers—he wasn’t the only one whose shoulders visibly relaxed each time he passed through a doorway and out of the jungle, only to slump into exhausted semiconsciousness five minutes later when the adrenaline wore off.

Math was by far the worst—Mrs. Atkins was by no means cruel, but she could only circle the room so many times before fairness required her to call on Conor, and it seemed distinctly unpromising that his answers confused her every bit as much as her questions confused him. Their first major test was coming up soon, and Conor couldn’t decide whether to bury himself in the textbook or write it off as a lost cause. He had briefly considered copying Ashleigh’s homework, then decided against when he realized that even the other boy’s C-level work would be implausible, coming from Conor himself.

The other classes were not quite so bad—in Science, Social Studies, and electives, he was meeting with his usual mixture of modest success and acceptable failure. But he still found it hard to look forward to any of them with anything like enthusiasm.

The one exception was first period English. Ms. Palmano’s class was turning out to be the oddest he’d ever been in. After their first discussion, she hadn’t spoken a word for three days straight, except to crack down on a pair of girls who’d come in chattering and been too slow to settle down.

(She had sent both of them straight to the office without so much as a warning, paying no heed to their protests, and the room had been deadly silent for the rest of the period.)

On Friday, she had taken up their papers, put tape over their names, and passed them back out, so that each student held someone else’s anonymous work. They spent that day and all of Monday reading them aloud, one at a time, ignoring by tacit agreement the sudden and involuntary twitch of a classmate recognizing their own words in the air. The papers had varied wildly in topic, length, and style; throughout the recitation, Ms. Palmano had sat unmoving at her desk, a look of polite attentiveness fixed on her face.

Once the last piece had been shared, she had taken the papers back, gathered them into a neat stack, and dropped them into the trash. “Terrible,” she had said into the shocked silence, wiping her hands theatrically. “Unpublishable, across the board.”

As on the first day, reactions had been mixed. But the outraged students had been truly outraged, some of them actually leaping up out of their seats as they shouted their objections. It had taken Ms. Palmano a full thirty seconds to make herself heard over the clamor.

When she finally did, though, her response was devious. “Any student who disagrees with my evaluation is welcome to have me take a second look,” she’d said. “Simply identify which paper belonged to you, and we’ll go over it publicly, for everyone’s benefit.” At that, the shouters had abruptly recanted, their complaints trailing off into seditious grumbles.

That had been the end of Monday’s class, and on Tuesday of the second week, they had come in to find laminated speeches and essays spread throughout the room, taped to the desks. The words READING REQUIRES SILENCE had been written on the board, and they had spent the period rotating from one seat to the next, poring over the words of Confucius and Alexander, Mandela and Churchill, Jesus and Socrates and Sun Tzu and Swami Vivekananda. Most of it was utterly incomprehensible to Conor, and his classmates didn’t seem to be doing any better—the indifferent ones were clearly daydreaming, and many of the nerdier ones were on strike. But he copied a handful of good quotes into the journal, and he managed to make it all the way through Lincoln’s Gettysburg Address, which was the shortest piece. The entire time, Ms. Palmano sat behind her desk, ignoring them completely as she thumbed through a tattered paperback novel.

Strangely, Conor felt encouraged rather than frustrated by her offhand discourtesy. Partly it was because he felt none of the fear and outrage his more scholarly classmates were feeling—he was, after all, making an inoffensive 85 in the class. Partly it was because he enjoyed watching those same classmates get cut down to size—Ms. Palmano had affirmed what he’d always secretly believed, that while his own writing was admittedly terrible, theirs wasn’t really much better.

But mostly, he liked it because it had the ring of pure truth. There were no euphemisms, no careful tiptoeing around people’s feelings. She talked to them as if she were a kid, unafraid to judge and even less afraid to pronounce those judgments.

For as long as Conor could remember, grownups had bent over backwards to hide their real opinions, blurring the boundary between good and bad with an almost frantic desperation—No, no, it’s great, I love it! Ms. Palmano, though, had reintroduced the sharp, dividing line, driving the two sides apart once more. That she had placed all of them on the bad side was almost trivial, by comparison—what mattered to Conor was that she was willing to acknowledge the difference, wasn’t cringing and terrified and unwilling to talk about it. It was a backhanded kind of respect, and it reminded him of Ashleigh, somehow.

Plus, she winked at me.

That hadn’t meant anything, of course, but it stuck in his mind. Out of everyone in the room, he had been the first to get it, that first day—to figure out the game that she was playing, and make the first move in response. He might not have any special aptitude for reading or writing, but he knew how to recover after being thrown off balance, and if the first week was anything to go by, that was going to matter, in this class.

And so it was that on Wednesday of the second week, he entered first period feeling almost eager, ready to participate. But there were no instructions on the board, and no papers on the desk, and the usual pre-class quiet had a nervous, apprehensive feel to it, their teacher’s expression dark and ominous.

She must have been counting them as they came in through the door, because with half the class still on its feet, she strode over and closed it with a sharp click. “I thought,” she said, filling the sudden silence, “that we might start today with a bit of candor. You see, some of you tried to get me fired last night, and I think it’s best we clear the air before we go any further.”

Her tone was light and casual, not matching her face at all, and so it took a heartbeat for the words to sink in, after which the air in the room turned to ice.

“Oh. Guess I’m not supposed to say that, am I? Unprofessional. I’m supposed to pretend like nothing happened, and if I know which students were behind it, I’m definitely not supposed to let it influence the way I treat them for the next—what is it, hundred and seventy-three class days. Right?”

There was a thick, uncomfortable silence as most of the students tried looking anywhere other than straight ahead. Conor exchanged a glance with Holt, who jerked his head subtly toward the boy who had complained during the very first lecture. He was sinking slowly in his seat, his cheeks scarlet.

“Maybe I should back up, since only four of you actually know what I’m talking about.” Ms. Palmano leaned against the empty dry erase board, arms crossed. “On Monday, I did something that took a lot of you by surprise. I took your papers—which you had worked on for four whole hours, my goodness—told you that they weren’t good enough, and put them in the trash.

“Of course, I didn’t actually throw them away. That was a bit of theater on my part.” Following her nod, they saw a neat stack of papers sitting on the corner of her desk. “But since I never intended for you to have them back, let’s say I don’t hold it against you if you bought the act. Very understandable.”

She smiled tightly. “Now, I expected a certain amount of blowback, which is why I cleared my plan with our dear principal three weeks ago. He’s fairly old school—still complains that we should bring back paddling—and so he got it right away. But being old school, he was surprised at the vehemence of the complaints he received. If you don’t know the word ‘vehemence,’ you should look it up later—it’s got an ‘h’ in it.”

She paused, as if expecting—what? But no one moved. No one spoke.

She continued. “Four sets of parents all called within the same hour, demanding I be fired on the spot. Well, seven in total—I did the same thing with all of my classes. But this class was the only one with so much…solidarity. Our dear principal asked me, what did I do differently with these kids, that their parents were so personally offended? And I said gee, I don’t know—which kids were they?”

There was a slight rustling as heads twisted back and forth. The boy at the front of the room was sinking even lower, and the girl in front of him—Jennifer—had her hands cupped around her eyes like blinders, shutting out her classmates.

“Exactly,” continued Ms. Palmano. “That’s where it got interesting. Because wouldn’t you know it, every single one of those parents had a kid who’d put themselves in the ‘A’ column.”

She gave another tight, humorless smile. “Then it all made sense. You see, you guys are what we call the ‘higher level’ class. Did you know there are more academically gifted students in here than in any of my other periods? We don’t make a big deal out of it, but it’s true. Which means that this class in particular is made up of a bunch of kids who aren’t used to hearing that their work just isn’t going to cut it.”

She stepped away from the board and began walking slowly up and down the rows of desks, the students leaning away from her as she passed as if she were giving off heat. “The parents, of course, insisted that I had misjudged their brilliant children, that I was a terrible teacher, and that maybe the reason I couldn’t recognize quality work is that I’m unable to produce it myself. Oh, yes,” she said, catching Holt’s strangled laughter. “That one especially made me smile. Because as it happened, I’d already done something that afternoon, in preparation for this week’s lessons: I’d taken every paper that you all had written and typed them out in the same font and format, fixing all the little grammatical errors along the way so that everyone’s words were presented with the same level of polish.

“And guess what? Those very same parents were completely unable to pick their children’s work out of the pile. Only one father managed to do it, and he, at least, was honest enough to admit that he’d already asked his daughter what she’d written about.”

Ms. Palmano stopped at the back of the room, a few feet away from Conor’s desk, turning away from him as she faced the rest of the class, the other students all twisted around in their seats.

“So that put an end to that little episode,” she said. “For the record: I’m not actually the monster some of you were making me out to be. I’m not a sadist and I have no interest in bruising your self-esteem. I’m simply not going out of my way not to.”

She stepped around Conor’s desk and reopened the door to the hallway, leaving it ajar as she strode back up the aisle to her desk. Picking up the top paper from the stack, she began to read aloud.

“’I am writing this paper, which is a dumb paper because you haven’t told us what we’re supposed to do, so I’m going to fail like you said because I can’t think of anything to write. This whole assignment is stupid. I don’t get how you can be a teacher if you don’t even have anything to teach. I thought you are supposed to help us instead of just sitting there but here I am not learning nothing. I wish I was fishing instead of wasting time writing down words until you say enough. If this is what this class is like then I’m going to get an F because it’s a waste of time and it don’t make no sense.’” She lowered the paper. “You all remember hearing this one on Friday, I’m sure. It goes on like that for about a page and a half.”

Setting it back on the stack, she leaned against the dry erase board again, fiddling idly with one of the markers as she swept her gaze across the room.

“In case you couldn’t tell, that was not one of the students who put themselves in the ‘A’ category. Our problem is more complicated than that. We’ve got one group that thinks they already know everything, and another that thinks they’re not learning anything. Add to that the kids who feel one way or another but are too polite to say anything, and the ones who just don’t care, and we’ve got maybe three-quarters of the class ready to walk out the door right now. Lucky for you, it’s open.” Raising her hand, she pointed with the marker. “Anybody wants out, now’s your chance.”

If the previous silences had been uncomfortable, this one was downright oppressive. She looked at them. They looked at her—at the door—at each other.

No one spoke.

“You see, I’m not even remotely interested in trying to teach people against their will. It’s barbaric, it’s not what I signed up for, and—incidentally—it doesn’t work. You aren’t babies. If you want out, get out, and let the rest of us get to work.”

Conor’s fingers twitched. The little leatherbound journal was down in the rack under his seat, along with his binder and pencil pouch and second period textbook—he didn’t think it was wise, all things considered, to draw attention to himself by bending down and fishing it out. But there was something there that didn’t feel quite right—some note of confusion, something that had triggered his new habit of writing down anything interesting—

It was Holt who summoned the courage to speak first.

“What happens if we leave?”

“Not my department,” the teacher said, shrugging. “Whatever usually happens to kids who walk out in the middle of class, I guess. Detention.”

“That’s not fair!” Jennifer blurted.

“What’s not fair about it?”

“You aren’t even giving us a choice!”

“I’m sorry—was I supposed to? Did any of your other teachers offer you a choice?”

“None of them told us we should leave and then said we’d get in trouble if we did.”

“I never said you should leave. I told you that you could, if you wanted to. And that was already true—me acknowledging it didn’t make it possible all of a sudden. You can do whatever you want. But all else being equal, your best bet is to stay right where you are.”

“Then what was that whole lecture about?” Holt demanded.

“I thought it was pretty clear—I was clarifying your options.”

“What options? You still didn’t give us a choice!”

“Aren’t you listening? You already have a choice—stay, or go.”

“But that isn’t a choice. We have to stay. Those are the rules.”

Ms. Palmano opened her mouth to reply, then hesitated, a frown working its way down from her eyebrows. “Maybe we need to back up,” she said. “There’s a fairly basic point that you’re not getting, here.” She shifted her stance, turning away from Holt and addressing the class as a whole. “How many of you are here at school because you want to be?”

Several glances were exchanged before a scattering of hands rose hesitantly into the air. Conor’s and Holt’s were not among them.

“And how many of you would say that you’d rather be somewhere else?”

The hands fell, replaced by a dozen more.

“Okay, those of you with your hands up—where would you be, if you didn’t have to be in this class right now?”

A sudden impulse seized Conor. “Fishing,” he called out.

There was a little laughter, and as Ms. Palmano smiled, the temperature in the room came up a degree or two from freezing. “All right, Conor, since you volunteered—”

Aw, crap.

“—what is it, exactly, that’s keeping you from going fishing right now?”

“Aren’t…you?” he answered, already regretting his decision to speak. He could feel the pressure of his classmates’ attention like a physical weight across his shoulders.

Rule two, rule fucking two, there’s only five rules and you forgot the second one—

“From all the way over here? You’re right next to the door—what am I going to do, use the Force?”

More laughter. Conor shifted self-consciously in his seat. “You could call the front office.”

“True. But by the time anybody got here, you’d be long gone, wouldn’t you? I mean, how long does it take to get off campus from here, two minutes? Half that, if you run.”

Tell her you could do it in thirty seconds if you go over the wall.

This time, Conor said nothing.

“The point is,” Ms. Palmano continued, “there isn’t actually anything stopping you. No guards, no chains, no barbed wire fences. If you wanted to leave, you could. But you haven’t. Modus tollens—logically, you must not want to.”

“Otis what?” Holt interjected, triggering another round of giggles.

Conor didn’t laugh. He was beginning to grow frustrated—she was toying with him, twisting things around so that his answers kept coming out wrong. “What are you trying to get me to say?” he asked, an edge of anger creeping into his voice. “That I don’t—that I’m wrong about what’s inside my own head?”

Ms. Palmano’s gaze sharpened. “Would you rather be fishing?” she asked quietly. “Because I can think of two good creeks in walking distance. Three, if you’re willing to skip second period, too. Or do you prefer to stay in class?”

“I prefer not to get grounded!”

“Bingo!” Throwing him a mock salute, Ms. Palmano turned back to the rest of the class. “I hate to sound like a self-help book, but the first step in taking control of your life is realizing that you already have it. Conor wants to go fishing, but he wants to stay out of trouble more—so he stays in his seat even though there aren’t any chains. He could get up and leave, but he knows I’d call the office; he could outrun the AP, but he knows we’d call his parents; he could run away from home, but it just isn’t worth it. So he chooses to follow the rules. He chooses—not his parents, not me, not the police or the president. Just him.”

She began to pace. “Unless you’ve been kidnapped, hypnotized, and put under direct control by the government or aliens or the Wicked Witch of the West, no one has ever made you do anything. Never. Not once. The most they’ve done—the most anybody can do—is limit your options. Make certain choices so attractive or unattractive that you’re overwhelmingly likely to pick the one they want. But even then, there’s nothing to stop you from going the other way. Somebody could put a gun to your head, and if you had a good enough reason, you’d still tell them no.”

She turned and began writing on the board. When she finished, she capped the marker and moved aside so they could read:

“There has never been a slave who did not choose to be a slave.

Their choice may be between bondage and death, but the choice is always there.”

“Free will, kids. Whether you admit it to yourself or not, you’re here at school because you want to be—or at least, you unwant it less than you unwant all the other options. That’s how human beings work—we always pick whichever option looks best. Coming to class every day nets you the lowest possible amount of pain and suffering—although given how hard some of you tried to get me fired, maybe you’re reconsidering.” She smiled humorlessly, and the sinking boy twitched.

Conor frowned, feeling oddly unbalanced, as if he was up on the roof and the wind was blowing. He wanted to object—but he didn’t want to speak—but what she was saying was wrong—

Wasn’t it?

But he couldn’t put his finger on how, exactly—

“Holt was only halfway right,” Ms. Palmano continued. “The rules do say you have to stay, but the rules by themselves can’t make you do it. Rules and laws don’t control people—they just convince people to control themselves, by setting the price for misbehavior. Want to go ninety on the highway? Fine—but it’s going to cost you five hundred bucks every time you get caught. For some people, that’s a fair trade, and they take the risk. Other people have to get by on a public school teacher’s salary, so their car doesn’t even go that fast. It's all a question of what you want, and how much you’re willing to pay.”

She turned back to the board and added a third line beneath the quote:

THERE IS ALWAYS A PRICE.

“That’s what this lecture is secretly about. Because, see, you don’t just pay for the bad stuff. You have to pay for the good stuff, too. Robbing a bank might cost you twenty years of your life, but so does becoming a neurosurgeon. And some of you have been trying to get a free ride—you’re not willing to pay the price for skipping class, but you haven’t paid the price of taking class, either. You’re dozing off in the back, or you’re writing passive-aggressive essays, or you’re going home and complaining to Mommy and Daddy that your mean old teacher hurt your feelings.”

Leaning back against the board, she crossed her arms again. “It’s not going to fly. I let it slide for the first week, but the petulant little kid act has to stop. For the record, there have been kings younger than you—I don’t think you need your parents to fight your battles for you. Get off the fence, stop dragging your heels, decide whether you’re in or out because those of us who are here to get things done don’t need the rest of you slowing us down.”

Silence reigned as she pointed to the door once more. For several long seconds, the class held its breath. Then—

“What about what we want?”

It was Jennifer, and her voice was laced with indignation. Conor couldn’t see her face, but the rest of the class could; she had stopped hiding behind her hands and now sat rigidly in her seat, arms crossed, a mirror image of the woman at the front of the room.

“Pardon?” Ms. Palmano replied, arching an eyebrow.

“You keep going on about prices and payment and stuff, but you haven’t said what we’re paying for. You’re saying we have to cooperate, but you haven’t given us any good reasons why, except that you’ll throw us out if we don’t. You sound like some kind of crummy stepmom trying to bully us into doing extra chores. Why should we say yes? What do we get out of it?”

“An education.”

“But you’re not teaching us anything! All you’ve done so far is throw out our papers and insult us!”

“And what did you learn from that?”

“That you’re a total jerk and you should be fired!”

Jennifer’s tone had risen with each exchange and was now not far from being a shout. A shiver moved outward from her desk like the shockwave from an explosion, leaving behind expressions of horror, trepidation, admiration, glee. Conor glanced across the aisle at Holt, whose face was surprisingly still, and thoughtful.

Ms. Palmano shook her head ruefully. “You say ‘total jerk’ like it’s a bad thing.”

“It is a bad thing! You’re supposed to help us, not treat us like dirt!”

“I am helping you. This is lesson number one, and I’m sorry, but I don’t know any non-harsh way to do it. I’m up against six-plus years of propaganda and we don’t have a lot of time.”

“You threw my paper in the garbage.”

“And you left the room without fishing it out.”

There was another collective rustle as half the class let out a breath.

Point, whispered Ashleigh.

“Because you would have—”

“Stop. Be honest, and by ‘be honest’ I mean actually check, inside your own head. Did you really, actually not fish it out because you expected the price to be too high? It took Conor nearly an hour to get to the school, but only five minutes to reach the roof. He paced back and forth across it as he caught his breath, kicking aside the stones, recreating the path of sound footing that led up to Binder’s Gap. When he finished, he paused at the edge, looking out at the gymnasium below.

The evidence of his previous landing was still there, the rough gouges casting long shadows in the slanting light of the setting moon. It looked somehow closer, as though the buildings were huddling together in the cold.

Conor was afraid of the dark—always had been. But during the summer with Ashleigh, he had made an important discovery: it didn’t take an act of bravery to overcome fear, only a sense of purpose. Terror crept in when you let your mind wander. To keep it at bay, you had to become the hunter, the chaser instead of the chased. You never let the darkness sneak up on you—you snuck up on it, and let the rumblings of monsters guide you in for the kill.

Tonight, he was the predator at his peak. The cold that had filled him outside Ashleigh’s house had taken root in his chest as he walked away, leaving no room for doubt or anxiety or fatigue. Restless, he had started to run, and when a wall loomed in his path, he discovered that there was something in him that would not be turned aside. Flowing over and past it, he became Batman—only instead of honing his skills, he was using them, blazing a trail at odd angles to the maze of streets and houses. He climbed fences, pushed through hedges, and jumped creeks until he reached the commercial district, and then he made his way across the rooftops, scrambling up drainpipes and dropping down into dumpsters. After only a week and a half of running, his stamina was already markedly better. It was life itself, this feeling that welled up inside him. It lit up the ice in his chest like a sunbeam striking a prism. Throwing back his head, he laughed out loud for what felt like the first time in years, letting loose an unbridled whoop that echoed between the empty buildings. Spreading his arms, he collapsed onto his back, looking up at the stars as the sting in his palms grew louder, sharper, realer.

Nothing was different. Nothing had changed. The weight upon his shoulders had not shifted half an inch. But for the briefest, the most fleeting of moments, it had ceased to matter. The pressure had vanished in freefall, and the memory of weightlessness was like a talisman.

There’s a world outside of all of this.

One where vicious bullies and broken friendships simply didn’t matter, where all of the thoughts that had filled his head for the past ten days were small and meaningless. A world of places, not people—of things, of paths, of possibilities.

And in that world—

(The real world.)

—Conor Moreton could fly.
