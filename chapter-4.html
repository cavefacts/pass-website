Conor’s eyes snapped open at the first chime of his alarm. He had slept dreamlessly for three hours, during which the fire in his blood had cooled, leaving behind only ashes and smoke.

Everything hurt. He staggered as he rose out of bed, an icepick pain in his heels, a dull millstone grinding in his knees. It took him a full minute to peel off yesterday’s clothes, and most of another to limp down the hallway to the bathroom, leaning heavily against the wall the whole way. The mirror showed filth everywhere, dark bruises blossoming on his shoulders, cuts on his face and his forearms. There was something oddly familiar about his eyes, dark and sunken, and he stared at his reflection for a dozen heartbeats before it clicked.

Hello, Guyler.

He turned on the shower as hot as it would go and leaned against the tile, watching the dirt and dried blood pooling at his feet. Bit by bit, the heat leached its way into his body, loosening him up until he was able to reach for the soap without wincing.

You may have overdone it.

This wasn’t the usual next-day soreness. Where normally there would be tightness and a dull, constant ache, he now felt a series of short, wild spasms with almost every movement, as though his muscles had lost the ability to control themselves, were one misfire away from contracting into black holes. The worst of it was in his back—when he reached up to lather his armpit, everything to the right of his spine seized up, dragging his vertebrae out of alignment.

…hm.

Slowly, carefully, he bent to wash his shin, where a shortcut through a thornbush had left a spiderweb of thin, black scabs. That one, at least, he remembered—as the water cascaded across his back, he tried to think, to match the various pains with specific moments, specific impacts. But it was all a blur. He hadn’t done anything new, really. Just…

A lot.

Too much.

Too much for your mom last night.

Conor stilled, an unpleasant weight settling into his chest.

(It wasn’t that he’d forgotten—wasn’t that Ashleigh’s voice in his head had suddenly brought it all crashing back, or anything like that. But he had been looking in the other direction, and the words had turned him back.)

Right.

Right.

…fuck.

He began moving again, filling his palm with shampoo, dumping most of it onto his head and saving a drop for the eight wispy hairs above his groin. By now, Ashleigh’s mother would have come home from her graveyard shift, would no doubt have seen what he had done to Ashleigh’s bedroom window, and…

And what?

He leaned forward, letting the water wash the bubbles from his hair, eyes closed against the deluge. Long seconds passed as he waited—for an idea, a next action, anything.

But there was nothing. No response, from his wrung-out brain—just a blank, animal awareness, like a deer in the path of a speeding car.

Incoming…

The word was still echoing in his thoughts when he entered the kitchen ten minutes later. His mother was already there, packing up her lunch, the coffee maker gurgling quietly on the counter. Conor shuffled past her, grabbing a stack of frozen waffles from the freezer and folding himself carefully into his seat.

“Plate,” she said, without looking up.

He groaned.

Smiling, she took one down from the cabinet and slid it to him across the table. “I’ll never understand how you can eat those things cold,” she said. “They taste like frozen cardboard.”

Conor shrugged, immediately regretting it as a muscle seized at the base of his neck, making him wince. “Syrup,” he said, reaching for the bottle in the lazy susan as he dug into the knot with the fingers of his other hand.

“Incidentally, congrats. You’re ungrounded today.”

He looked up. His mother was still smiling as she poured half of the coffee into her thermos and the rest into a mug. “Surprised?” she asked.

“I thought I had four days to go.”

“Time off for good behavior.”

You gonna tell her?

“Plus, your soccer tryouts are tomorrow, and neither one of us can get off work to drive you.” She blew lightly across the surface of her drink and took a careful sip. “Take the trail, not the street, and wear your helmet—Elena told me they’ve had three bike accidents come through the ER this week.”

Conor said nothing, his eyes unfocused, the waffle frozen halfway to his mouth.

Soccer.

He had completely forgotten about soccer.

It wasn’t a big deal, exactly—it was a small rec league, put together by the neighborhood parents, and none of the teams were any good. All the real athletes ended up trying out at school, or leaving to join the traveling championship circuit. It was mostly just a cheap alternative to a babysitter, a way for the grownups to force the kids to get some fresh air, for once.

But Ashleigh would be there…

The sound of his mother clearing her throat made him twitch—which made him wince again—and he looked up to see her standing with arms crossed, waiting.

“Helmet,” he echoed, trying to sound earnest. “Trail. Got it.”

Mollified, she turned back to the sink, began rinsing out the coffee pot. “Oh, and by the way,” she continued, speaking over her shoulder. “You’ll be going out for the Jaguars, not the Vikings.”

Conor’s thoughts, which had just begun to move again, screeched to a halt for a second time. His mouth opened—closed—opened again. “What?”

You mean ‘what’ like you didn’t hear her, or—

“Why?”

His mother’s eyes flickered briefly in his direction, then away. There was a long pause, during which she shut off the water, dried the coffee pot, and came over to sit down in the seat across from Conor. “A few reasons,” she answered guardedly. “For one, I’ve never liked the way Coach Matthews kept you stuck in the same position for year after year.”

“You’ve been a fullback since second grade, though, except for one season playing goalie. You’re supposed to run around, not just stand there waiting for the ball.”

Like marijua—

Hssst.

Conor clamped down, cutting off the intruding voice. “Why can’t I shake things up on my team?” he demanded.

“Don’t you think it’ll be nice to get to know some new people?”

There it is.

“I already know them,” Conor argued. “We’ve all been in the same classes since forever. I don’t like any of them.”

“Come on, Conor, half of them go to Blessed Sacrament. You haven’t even given them a chance.”

“But all my friends are in the Vikings!”

Conor’s mother arched an eyebrow, and he shut his mouth with a click, realizing too late that he had come perilously close to shouting. She held his gaze for a moment, then spoke in a steady, level voice. “First of all, that’s not even true. You told me last week that Holt and Nolan are going out for the school team. It’s just Ashleigh and Sam and Eddie on the Vikings, and you despise Eddie—”

“But—”

“And furthermore,” she continued—this time with an edge in her voice that made Conor sit very still—“if you’ll think back, you made most of those friends through the Vikings, and so there’s every reason to believe you’ll make five or six new friends by trying out another team.”

Conor’s mother waited pointedly, clearly expecting another outburst, and gave a curt nod when he held his tongue. “If you don’t like the Jaguars, fine,” she said. “Try the Stars, or the Hawks, or pick a different sport for all I care. But you’re not going back to the Vikings, and that’s final.”

“But why?”

“Because I said so, Conor.”

“What if I don’t want to be on any team, then?” Conor asked, hearing the sullenness in his voice and making no attempt to rein it in.

“Not an option. You need to get out and socialize. You spent the last two weeks moping around in your bedroom—”

“I was GROUNDED!”

“—and you will be again if you do not immediately lower your voice, young man.”

Her own tone had turned glacial, and her knuckles were pale as she gripped her mug. “This is not a negotiation. You are going to come home from school tomorrow and get on your bike. You are going to put on your helmet, and you are going to ride to the park—on the trail—where you will find Coach Pool and tell him that you’re there to try out for any position other than fullback. You’re going to make the team—heaven help you if you don’t and I think you did it on purpose—and then you are going to ride home and tell me all about it. Is that clear?”

“This is totally unfair! It’s my life—I should get a say.”

“Contrary to what you might think, Conor, you are not an adult. You are a child, which means that half the time your brain is physiologically incapable of telling a good idea apart from a bad one.”

(She said the word physiologically in pieces, their edges razor-sharp, as if each syllable was its own whole argument—fizz-ee-oh-lodge-ih-cull-ee.)

“…considering your recent track record when it comes to making decisions, you should count yourself lucky I haven’t pulled you right out of East Binder and dropped you into home school—”

Seriously, you gonna tell her?

Shut up, Ashleigh.

You know it’ll be worse if you wait until my mom calls.

“—you get a say once you’ve convinced me that we’re past this little rough spot, and I can trust you to be mature and act in your own best interest. Want to speed up the process? You could start by not throwing a childish temper tantrum over a change in your little league soccer career, which is clearly so central to your ambitions.”

Neatly trapped, Conor could do nothing but fume as his mother raised her mug and drained the rest of the coffee. His thoughts churned, mental fingers scrabbling, digging, looking for some lever that might move her—

Not like I was going to talk to you at soccer practice anyway.

Conor felt his throat tighten. The voice in his head had never been…been harsh like that, before—almost savage.

Lot of things happening for the first time, lately.

He finished his waffles in trembling silence, dropping his plate into the sink as he shuffled back to his bedroom. Moving as quickly as his aching limbs would allow, he gathered his school things, put on his shoes, and headed for the front door, giving his mother a wide berth as he passed once more through the kitchen. “I love you,” she called out after him, her voice still taut. “Have a good day.”

He let the door swing shut behind him without reply.

Yeah, that’ll show her.

Shut UP, Ashleigh.

The ghost of the other boy’s voice was like a soft wind, feathering over the embers of his frustration, fanning them into open fury. Stalking to the end of the driveway, he threw his bag to the ground and kicked it. None of this was his fault, none of this was fair, he hadn’t done anything wrong—

(An image of broken glass sparkling in the moonlight.)

Furious, he kicked the bag again, then tumbled to the ground as a muscle in his calf suddenly tightened into an iron knot. A wordless howl made it halfway out of his mouth before he bit it off, and he lashed out mindlessly, hammering the side of his fist into the driveway until his arm seized up too.

There you go, whispered Ashleigh, as he curled into a ball on the cold concrete. Nothing a little punching won’t solve.

Strangely, that did it, the anger vanishing as suddenly as it had spiked, leaving behind only a tired, clinical calm. Slowly, the tension drained out of his limbs, and he slumped, boneless, his cheek against the rough surface, the rhythmic throbbing in his muscles like the ticking of a clock.

This, pronounced a soft voice, is what happens.

For once, he wasn’t sure who it was, him or Ashleigh or somebody else.

What does that even mean? he shot back.

There was no answer.

Pulling himself up into a sitting position, Conor began to work out the cramp in his arm, kneading and squeezing his bicep until it finally unclenched. He did the same for his calf, then carried on stretching, slowly rebuilding his composure as he waited for the bus.

He felt almost human again by the time it arrived, managed to climb the three steep steps without staggering. Standing at the top of the aisle, he braced himself against the first two seats as the driver hit the gas, taking advantage of the brief imbalance to sweep his gaze across the battlefield.

If the afternoon bus ride was a hurricane, the morning one was a steady downpour of the forty-days-and-nights variety. There was the same social brinkmanship, the same jockeying for position, but it played itself out over minutes rather than seconds, the rules subtly but meaningfully different.

It was common knowledge that sitting down next to someone when you didn’t absolutely have to meant that you were either humping them already or you hoped to start soon. As a result, the seats filled up in waves, even best friends sitting opposite one another rather than sharing, the number of available options dwindling until every bench had a rider, then opening up again as students began doubling up.

Conor’s stop was in the second wave, but Ashleigh’s was right at the end of the first, meaning that the pair of them had spent all of sixth grade at the mercy of the system, bouncing around from place to place. This year, with Ashleigh on suspension, the short end of the stick had passed to Timothy, who more often than not found himself deep in enemy territory. Conor sat as close as he dared each day, unwilling to leave the younger boy entirely without backup but also eager to avoid pissing him off. That usually put them both somewhere in the rear third of the bus, with Conor a few rows closer to the front.

Today, though, Timothy had taken a spot only four seats back from the door, and he made a point of lifting his bag off of the seat as Conor started down the aisle.

Ah.

Right.

Great.

Conor sat down, noticing as he did so that the younger boy’s bag was brand-new, a tag still dangling off one zipper.

“What did you do?” Timothy hissed, without preamble.

Conor studied the sixth grader’s face, looking for clues. “Does your mom know?” he asked quietly.

“Sort of. She thinks he did it.” Conor shot the younger boy a look of surprise, and he shrugged. “We took all the glass and tossed it out through the hole, along with that bronze Spiderman thing.”

Something in Conor’s torso squirmed. He had given the little statuette to Ashleigh as a Christmas present, two years earlier.

“Did he say anything?” he asked.

“No,” Timothy said. “He just kept crying.”

The world lurched to a halt.

Crying?

“Same deal when Mom got home. He wouldn’t say anything, just sat there. He cut himself on some of the glass—just a little cut, on the side of his wrist, but she was super freaked about it. I think she’s taking him to a therapist today.” Timothy’s scowl deepened. “Which we can’t afford, so there goes my birthday. What did you do?”

“Nothing.”

“Bullshit.”

“No, really, nothing. Just the rock.” Timothy’s expression grew even darker, and Conor hastened to explain. “I came over to talk. He—at school, Eddie was—I just—I needed to talk to him, okay? I threw some pebbles up at the window to wake him up. You know, like usual. But he just stood there looking at me. So then I, um. Uh.”

Conor trailed off, his face burning with embarrassment. It sounded incredibly, incredibly dumb.

Timothy apparently agreed. “That’s it?” he spat, whisper-quiet and razor-sharp. “That’s why you broke my brother’s window? Because he didn’t want to talk to you at one in the goddamn morning?”

“He’s been talking to Eddie.”

Timothy rolled his eyes. “What are you, spoiled little girls?” he asked. “What is with the two of you? Why can’t you just fucking text each other, like normal people? Jesus Christ, of all the stupid, pathetic bullshit—”

The sixth grader broke off, his nostrils flaring, and as if to demonstrate an almost infinite capacity for irrelevance, Conor’s brain chose that exact moment to note that the younger boy smelled exactly like his brother.

“Look,” Timothy said, the word flashing out through clenched teeth. “Here’s a brilliant idea: instead of throwing fucking rocks at my house, why don’t you just give up?”

Conor swayed, as if Timothy had slapped him. “Huh?”

“Give up. He’s an asshole, he’s always been an asshole, he’s never going to stop being an asshole. Case in point, it’s been like two weeks and he’s still ducking you. Why not take a hint? And either way, how does throwing ROCKS through his WINDOW accomplish ANYTHING AT ALL?”

Conor was silent. The full reality of what he’d done was only just now sinking in, and he could hear in Timothy’s words a preview of what his parents would say, of what Ashleigh’s mother would say. Just when he had been on the verge of pulling clear, of putting enough distance between himself and the bag and the fight to start breathing again—

But they’re not going to say anything. They don’t know, and they’re not going to find out.

Because Ashleigh had covered for him.

(While crying, apparently.)

There was confusion there, a whole mystery to vanish into. But before that there was something else, something else he had to ask, he knew just how low the words were going to take him but he said them anyway, because a part of him had to know, had to be sure…

“You’re not going to tell, are you?”

The look of withering contempt on Timothy’s face hit him squarely in the gut. But before he could backpedal, another voice cut through the chatter, making both boys jump.

“Tell? Oh, hell no, man, ain’t no way little man’s gonna tell. Tell him you ain’t that kind of man, little man.”

It was Devon. He had somehow appeared in the seat immediately behind them, a giant out of place amid the middle schoolers, leaning in conspiratorially, his arms draped across the seat back. Timothy shifted his glare without losing a single degree of heat and snarled, “We were whispering, little man. Ever heard of a private conversation?”

The older boy grinned. “Y’all might want to double check the definition of the word ‘whisper.’ Besides,” he said loftily, leaning back. “You know what they say. Knowledge is power.”

He turned to Conor. “Which is exactly why ol’ Tim here ain’t telling nobody, Rocky. That’s the kind of card you can only play once. No sense wasting it, know what I’m saying?”

It took Conor several seconds to work out the implication behind Devon’s words, and when he did, he felt a small shiver of fear.

“Leave us alone,” Timothy snapped. “We’re not talking to you.”

Devon looked back at the younger boy. “You know, you’re kind of a grumpy kid, Tim. Surly. Hell, I might even go so far as to say mean. Anybody ever told you that before?” He shook his head sadly, pursing his lips. “Damn shame. You’re going to hurt somebody’s feelings someday, you keep hatin’ like that.”

The high schooler leaned back even further, lacing his fingers behind his head. “That’s okay, though. I don’t need to interrupt. I’ll just sit back here by myself and whisper, and you can sit up there and whisper, and we can be secret friends. Don’t no one have to know.”

Timothy’s face was a mirror for Conor’s own as he flushed red, his mouth working soundlessly in a mix of confusion and anger. Once again, the older boy turned his attention to Conor. “You ain’t got nothing to worry about, kid. I seen ol’ Tim here get his head swirled, lunch stole, books soaked, and gut punched, and he never said a word to nobody. I don’t think he’s a very big fan of authority. I mean, imagine somebody shitting in your shoes in the locker room, and you don’t even ask the coach for another pair.” He glanced at Timothy and flashed an expression of exaggerated horror, covering his mouth with one hand. “Oops. Don’t think I was supposed to tell you that. But no worries. I mean, we just whispering, right?”

Conor felt movement at his side and looked down to see Timothy’s fists clenched and trembling. The sixth grader’s face, though, was oddly calm, a closed sort of expression coming over it as he fixed Devon with an empty, emotionless stare.

“How did you know about that?” he asked quietly.

“Like I said, amigro, knowledge is power.” Devon flashed another grin, then paused, glancing up and past the two of them as the bus ground to a halt and a taller boy in an East Binder uniform began making his way down the aisle. Holding up a finger to Conor and Timothy, Devon dug into his pocket and pulled out what looked like a pair of bills, tightly folded. He handed them off without a word as the tall boy walked past, then continued on as if nothing had happened.

“Let you in on a secret,” he said. “Lot of knowledge floating around in schools, man. Shit’s omnipresent. Eight times eight is sixty-four, the capital of Djibouti is Djibouti. Facts out the ass…people be, like, giving them away.”

“What was that?” Conor demanded, pointing down the aisle.

“Oh, just paying some debts is all. Man did me a favor yesterday, and I’m not a big fan of hanging out in the red.”

“Forty bucks doesn’t sound like a favor,” Timothy said flatly. “Sounds like a job.”

Devon shook his head. “Nah, just thirty. Big bills on the outside. And I don’t expect nobody to do no job for anything less than a C-note. Thirty’s a good rate for favors. Helps ’em remember to get themselves done.”

He winked, and both Conor and Timothy stared.

What.

It wasn’t a question so much as a mood—everything about Devon was what, the past two minutes no less than their conversation the week before. The older boy was like—like a cartoon, a character, not like a person at all…

It had to be on purpose. An act.

But why?

“Are you selling drugs?” Conor murmured, the words rising all on their own from somewhere in the lower parts of his brain. A high schooler, someone from Central—

(Someone black, he carefully did not think, but seriously, it wasn’t like the black kids who lived in the Central district were rich.)

—throwing around hundreds of dollars like it was nothing—

“Not much of a salesman if I’m the one paying, wouldn’t you say?”

“What kind of favor is worth thirty bucks?” Timothy asked quietly.

“Oh, you know,” the older boy drawled. “This ’n’ that. Depends. Tell you what—you want to guess, start naming things you might could do for me for thirty bucks, and I’ll let you know if you get warm.”

He winked again.

Conor glanced at Timothy, but the younger boy’s eyes were still locked on Devon, a look of hard suspicion on his face. “How about if I volunteered to terrorize some little kid for you?” Timothy asked, his voice still quiet. “Say, maybe a sixth grader with a bit of a mouth. That the kind of thing you pay people for?”

Devon’s ever-present smile slipped, and his answer came out with none of its usual stageplay lightness. “Cold,” he murmured. “Job like that needs doing, I do it my fucking self. ‘Sides, type of thug you can buy for that shit ain’t usually wise enough to stay bought, and then we all got problems.”

He held Timothy’s gaze for a long moment, then tipped his head toward the back, where Eichardt was sitting. “Besides, last I recall, I was offering to get that motherfucker off your back, not paying him to stay on. An offer that still stands, by the way.”

“And that costs what?”

“Well, I don’t know. Kind of depends on whether calming him down works out to be more of a favor or a job, know what I mean? Want me to do you an estimate?”

Timothy’s only answer was to spin around and settle his back against the seat, eyes front and arms crossed. Devon chuckled softly.

“How about you, Rocky?” he asked, turning to Conor. “Looking to get the little man an early Christmas present?”

“Don’t you fucking dare,” Timothy hissed.

Devon laughed again. “Well, damn. Blue balls. Remind me not to start conversations around here unless I’m ready to finish ’em off myself.”

And then he leaned back once more, closing his eyes as if napping. Nonplussed, Conor turned back toward the front, throwing Timothy a sidelong glance. They rode in silence for a while, the jolting and lurching of the bus sending spikes of pain through Conor’s battered, aching body. When they stopped on a hill at the railroad crossing, his bookbag slid backwards under the seat, and as he bent down to retrieve it, he saw that Timothy was wearing a pair of Ashleigh’s old shoes.

Come on, say something.

Like what? Where would he even begin?

He fidgeted uncomfortably.

Maybe Timothy has a point, he heard himself think. Maybe it’s time to give up. Maybe let them both take care of themselves.

Except—

Ashleigh hadn’t told.

Ashleigh hadn’t told, and he’d been crying.

(Crying!)

So what? He’s being an asshole.

He was. He was.

And what he did to Guyler…

That, too, had crossed the line. But—

But what?

Conor looked over at Timothy.

But that.

In the cold light of day—with his whole body aching, with the sobering realization that he had fucked up, deeply, in more ways than one—

Timothy was alone.

Angry, determined, hurting—and alone.

And Conor, having just gone through a week and a half of life without Ashleigh, was more aware than ever of what it was he stood to lose. What it meant, to be on your own in a place like East Binder. What he’d thought he’d already lost—

Except that Ashleigh hadn’t told.

Not yet, Conor said, to the voice in the back of his head. He wasn’t ready to give up yet.

So last night was what—a warm-up?

He was unnerved, thinking about how far he’d swerved the night before, and how easily he’d turned back. Afraid that maybe this was just more denial—that he was only making things worse, digging himself deeper, making the eventual break even harder to take.

But still. Not yet.

Taking a deep breath, he reached out to tap Timothy’s shoulder.

“What?”

He felt the impact in his knees and at the base of his spine—unpleasant, but certainly not painful, and no worse than the roughest of his normal landings. “I don’t know,” he admitted, turning back to the other boy. “It just…feels better?”

“Yeah, but it’s harder,” Timothy said. “It’s like you’re landing on a railing on every jump.”

Conor smiled. “Which means you’re practicing for landing on a railing on every jump.”

Together, they leapfrogged down the sidewalk, jumping until their aching legs could take no more and they reverted back to their ambling pace.

“I wish you guys had told me,” Timothy grumbled, as they turned a corner and entered the neighborhood. “Would’ve been better than sitting around bored all summer.”

Conor gave him a sidelong glance, again unsure what to say. In all honesty, he’d never even considered inviting the younger boy, because—

“He never lets me do anything,” Timothy continued, his voice growing dark with bitterness. “Never. Even though I cover for him all the time. Even though I’ve never ratted him out for anything. A friendly part of the background?

He winced.

That, Conor was honor-bound to respond to.

“What do you mean?” he asked. “How?”

On the first day, we rode that golf ball ride at Epcot, the one that’s all about the future and stuff. I kept begging and begging to go on it again, but nobody else wanted to, so we never did. On the last day, like two hours before we were supposed to leave, we’re eating lunch, the golf ball is right there, and Dad says Ashleigh can pick any ride he wants, any one at all. And he picks Splash Mountain, and we have to get on the monorail and go all the way back to the Magic Kingdom for this ride that he’s already been on seven times. Timothy kicked at another piece of gravel, this one skittering off the sidewalk and out into the street. “It’s always like that,” he said. “He’s always, like, just barely justified, so you can’t really do anything about it. We’ll be getting pizza, and Mom’ll be short on money, and he’ll point out that plain cheese is cheaper than what I want. Or he’ll do something stupid, like stick gum under the seat in the car, and Mom’ll find it and go berserk, and he’ll swear up and down it wasn’t him, and then we both get punished, because it’s either that or snitch, even though the whole keeping quiet thing only goes one way because I never do stupid stuff that gets us both in trouble. Did you know he got a new bike when I wrecked and bent his handlebars? And then I had to ride the broken one for, like, two years? And the only reason I wrecked it in the first place is because four times he didn’t show up to teach me when he said he was going to, and I had to try to figure it out all on my own.”

Conor’s throat was tight, and his jaw was tight, and his eyes were tight and stinging. There were feelings swirling around inside of him that he didn’t have names for, something awful slowly growing.

I didn’t know.

But he had—hadn’t he?

“That’s just brothers, though, right?” he asked feebly. “I mean—lots of guys don’t get along with their brothers. He still looks out for you. He punched Eichardt for you.”

Timothy gave a hollow laugh. “Oh, yeah, Ashleigh’s big fight scene. The one where he didn’t stop me from getting roughed up or getting all my books dumped on the ground but he did show up in time to stop you from getting pounded.”

Conor frowned. That…didn’t exactly seem fair…

“And then the next day, everybody’s talking about him like he’s some big hero, even though he threw one weak punch—not to mention that you’re the one who actually stepped up and put the whole thing on pause. Meanwhile, I’m stuck being that stupid little sixth grader who can’t take care of himself.”

“Come on,” Conor objected. “You can’t pin that on Ashleigh. It’s not like that’s the reason he stepped in.”

“Of course that’s why he stepped in,” Timothy shot back. “That’s the only reason he ever does anything, is to make himself look good, or show off. Or to impress you—though I guess that’s over, now.”

Conor swallowed. “So you’d rather take a beating than let your brother help you out?”

“I already said, didn’t I? I don’t want protection.”

Could’ve used some today.

(Conor held his tongue.)

“Then what do you want?” he asked carefully. “I mean, you can’t blame him for not taking care of you and then get pissed when he does.”

“It’s not about whether he’s looking out for me or not. It’s about the fact that he decides one way or the other based on what’s convenient for him. He doesn’t actually care about what happens to me. He doesn’t give a shit about what happens to anybody. He only sticks his neck out when he’s getting something out of it, himself.”

Conor felt his head shaking. It was like they were talking about two completely different people. It wasn’t much of a stretch to accept that Ashleigh was a shitty older brother—Conor had seen them together, after all—but even off the top of his head, he could think of at least half a dozen times when his friend had put his freedom or his reputation on the line without hesitation—taking the fall for this or that bit of mischief, making a fool of himself for the sake of solidarity, or to provide a distraction, or simply to make Conor laugh. There was the time in fourth grade when Conor had accidentally shattered the decorative clay turkey in the center of the cafeteria, or the time when Ashleigh had screamed and yelled at their asshole of a counselor at summer camp, or the time when he’d literally pissed himself to stop everyone from laughing at Conor over the apple juice he’d spilled on himself—

“Yeah, he’ll do that stuff for you. You’re his friend. I’m just his brother.”

Conor had no answer to that.

“Besides—what about the past two weeks? Hasn’t gone out of his way to help you much lately, has he? Doesn’t matter what you’re going through. I’m telling you, this is what he’s like with everybody. You just…aren’t immune anymore, for some reason.”

There was too much truth in that to ignore, and Conor’s brow furrowed as he fell silent, considering.

Was Ashleigh…bad?

Conor had never had reason to wonder, before. But Guyler, that morning—those awful bruises, wrapping all the way around his ribs—

This is what your psycho butt buddy decided to do after he knocked me out.

The carelessness that Timothy was describing…Ashleigh had always been the kind of kid who gave no fucks. He was notorious for it. In many ways, it was his superpower.

But was that a good thing?

There was a word for people who didn’t care about anyone, who lacked the basic building blocks of empathy. Guyler had used it more than once. But Ashleigh wasn’t actually a psychopath…

Yeah, he’ll do that stuff for you. You’re his friend.

Had Conor been giving him a pass, just because he was nice to Conor?

Once more, he traced his mind back over their adventures together, the myriad scrapes they’d gotten into, the rules they’d bent or outright broken. The lies they’d told together, without ever doubting the other’s word—some for their own protection, some as a means to an end, some just to see if they would be believed.

None of that seemed bad. Mean, maybe, some of it—but nobody was nice all the time. Selfish and immature, perhaps, but come on, that was normal, they were kids, everybody was selfish and immature sometimes.

But—

(He could feel himself flinching, the push like two magnets set wrong way to each other, sending his attention anywhere else. But he resisted, fighting the fear, refusing to let it drive.)

There was just one thing that Conor could not explain away, one act so monumental and terrible that it seemed beyond all possible defense:

Guyler.

There was no question that Timothy would point to that as clear evidence of moral bankruptcy. Guyler himself certainly thought so. And standing in the courtyard, looking at the aftermath still written on the other boy’s body, Conor had found it hard to disagree.

No one has ever made you do anything, said Ms. Palmano’s voice, rising up from memory. Somebody could put a gun to your head, and if you had a good enough reason, you’d still tell them no.

What had happened to Guyler—

(You mean what Ashleigh did to Guyler, some stern part of him corrected.)

What Ashleigh had done to Guyler, he’d done on purpose. Had chosen, freely—or if there had been some reason, some excuse that made it all make sense, made it all somehow okay, he’d chosen not to let Conor know it, for two weeks and counting.

That’s why you’ve been eyeballing me? Guyler had said. You’re trying to figure out a way this is all my fault?

No. Not Guyler’s fault, and not right, not a good thing, but there had to be some explanation that would cause it to fall short of outright evil.

…didn’t there?

Not to dig myself a hole, here, but you really sound like you’re trying to talk yourself into something.

Yeah. He was. And it was getting harder and harder to do so, with every passing day.

“Maybe you’re right,” Conor said softly, breaking the silence. “Maybe he is just a self-centered jerk.”

The younger boy glanced at him, saying nothing.

“But if so, he’s not the only one.”

Conor thought about the other kids in his grade, most of whom he’d known for years, none of whom seemed even half as real, half as alive.

“And I think—”

Conor swallowed, feeling the fear rise up once more, the sense that he couldn’t say that, that isn’t okay—that he couldn’t get away with it.

But it was the truth. And Timothy deserved that much, at least.

“I think I’d still rather be friends with him than with anybody else.”

(The knife sliding across their palms—Ashleigh’s first, and then Conor’s—the grin on the other boy’s face as they clasped their hands together, and the light in Ashleigh’s eyes, brighter than the setting sun.)

“Oh, were we talking about you?” Timothy asked sarcastically, though without much heat. “In that case, problem solved. Guess I’ll just get over myself—not like I can go swap him out for a new brother anyway.”

Conor winced. “Sorry,” he said, feeling sheepish.

Not the only self-centered jerk, indeed.

Timothy sighed. “Whatever,” he heaved. “It doesn’t matter. It’s not like any of this is new. I should be used to it, by now. It’s just—”

He hesitated, and Conor held still, waiting.

“East Binder is nothing like Littleton, you know?”

Conor knew.

“And—and whatever, it’s fine, I can take care of myself. But like. You have a big brother, you kind of think that maybe might give you a little bit of a leg up on the whole situation. Friends in high places, and all that. Just once, it would be nice if Ashleigh pulled his head out of his ass long enough to make my life just a little bit easier. I’m not saying I expect it. I’m not saying he owes it to me, or anything. I’m just saying it’d be nice.”

Conor had no answer, and Timothy didn’t seem to be waiting for one. They covered the last quarter mile to the Falhanner house in silence, each alone with his own thoughts.

(There was a black plastic bag taped over the broken window, rippling gently in the wind.)

“—thanks.”

It was six o’clock in the morning, and there were choices to be made.

Conor rolled out of bed, his body abuzz with a Christmas morning tingle of anticipation. He showered quickly, donning his uniform and slipping quietly into the kitchen, where he forced down a pair of frozen waffles and half a glass of chocolate milk, pouring the rest into the sink.

It was six fifteen, and he wheeled his bike out of the garage, pausing at the end of the driveway. In one direction lay Ashleigh’s house. In the other direction lay the school—the courtyard, the shed, the rooftop—a dozen places where the other boy might go, might wait, each tinged with its own special significance.

It was six thirty, and Conor was on the road, the morning fog whipping his cheeks bright red, his heart pounding as he pedaled as hard as he could. Six thirty-five, and he was chaining his bike up in front of the cafeteria, considering his next move.

It was a quarter to seven, and he was standing on the roof of the auditorium, looking out at Binder’s Gap. He paced back and forth, his thoughts moving in circles, the morning ahead playing itself out in his mind in a hundred different ways. He kicked out a second runway for the jump, heedless of the noise he was making, restoring the path to its summer shape.

It was five after seven, and he was sliding back down the pipe, bracing himself for the final, bone-rattling drop at the bottom, walking past the roots where Guyler had never really fallen and never hit his head. It was seven twenty, and he was standing in the courtyard with the other early birds, shuffling and stamping, their breath misting in the air. It was seven forty-five, and he was frozen with trepidation as he spotted bus 134 pulling into the parking lot.

Calm down. It’s just me.

And it was. There, emerging through the doors, two hundred yards away but utterly unmistakable, was Ashleigh. He sauntered across the blacktop, bookbag slung over one shoulder, his neatly combed hair disarranging itself in the breeze.

Conor took a deep breath. This was the first test.

He stepped away from the nearest clump of students, positioning himself out in the open, directly in the other boy’s line of sight. If Ashleigh pretended not to notice him—

Ashleigh waved.

So huge was Conor’s relief that several seconds passed before he managed to raise a hand in answer, and even from a distance he could see the other boy’s smirk. Blushing, he waited as Ashleigh closed the distance, unrushed and untroubled. He was suddenly hyperconscious of his hands, hanging awkwardly at his sides, and he felt no small amount of gratitude when the other boy raised a fist for their traditional greeting.

“Batman,” Ashleigh said coolly, brushing his knuckles against Conor’s.

Conor felt his mind divide in two, one half dissolving in celebration, the other sharpening into a tense and wary vigilance. After fourteen days, sixteen hours, and nine minutes, Ashleigh had acknowledged his presence—smoothly, effortlessly, without even a hint of hesitation or unease. It was better than Conor had dared to hope, and worse than he’d steeled himself to expect—out of all the angles Ashleigh might have chosen, he’d settled on pretending like nothing was up.

“Hey, Ash,” Conor replied, matching the other boy’s tone exactly. Calm. Casual. Carefree.

“What’s our spot this year?”

“There isn’t one.” Conor gestured vaguely toward the flagpole. “Holt and them usually chill over by the bushes, but there’s nowhere to sit.”

“Lame,” Ashleigh pronounced, dropping his bookbag on the spot and plopping down on top of it. “So, what’d I miss?”

Conor twitched. This wasn’t just carefree—it was careless, the other boy’s tone almost offensively nonchalant…

“A lot,” he heard himself say, dropping his own bag and settling in beside the other boy. He began sifting through the events of the previous two weeks, lifting out memories and shaping them into stories, filling Ashleigh in on rumor, suspicion, happenstance. “—there was a ton of crap going on with the new kids from Central, graffiti and bullying and stuff, but that’s quieted down a little. They kicked a bunch of people out in the first week, and Mr. Sykes is nailing anybody else he catches to the wall.”

Conor grinned in spite of himself. “Not very,” he admitted.

Conor shrugged, feeling more unsettled with each passing moment. The conversation between them was flowing too easily—was almost unnaturally smooth. Every time a promising pause appeared, Ashleigh filled it, leaving Conor no openings into which he might insert a statement of substance. The grin on his face looked exactly the same as it had then, the intervening chaos having left no visible mark.

They set off for the doors of the front office together, Ashleigh drifting slightly ahead. Sixty more seconds, and their reunion would be over, a critical window closed.

Say something!

It was his own inner voice, Ashleigh’s having disappeared from his head the moment its owner stepped into view.

Like what? he shot back. “We need to talk”?

Well, don’t you?

Conor looked at the other faces in the crowd around him. Most were preoccupied, incurious, but every now and then he thought he caught a flicker of interest, of cool hostility—the legacy, perhaps, of Guyler’s speech. He came to me, Conor thought slowly.

Not to Holt, not to Nolan, not to fucking Eddie—Ashleigh had made a beeline straight for the place where Conor was waiting, had greeted him as a friend and ally, shown no desire to go anywhere else. He was back, and if he needed to pretend—if he needed this first day to feel normal—

Well, Conor could play along. For a while, at least. It was little enough, compared to everything else.
