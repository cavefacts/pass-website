August 31st, 1:59 PM

“D’you ever think about dying?”

“Forty-eight.”

“No, it’s okay.”

“I just—”

“Really, it’s fine. It’s been long enough, you know? I’m okay.”

“Okay.”

“I just—I think about it sometimes. Not just Dad, I mean. All of it. What it would be like. Whether it matters.”

“Matters?”

“Either. Both.”

“No.”

“What’s gotten into you?”

“What?”

“This is weird. You don’t—you’re not usually like—”

“I don’t think it would be all that bad.”

•••

“So, they make us sit in that fucking circle every day, huh?”

Conor smiled faintly as Ashleigh slammed his locker closed and straightened from where he was kneeling on the floor, rebalancing the stack of books in his arms.

“Yeah,” Conor answered. “They don’t even call it homeroom anymore. It’s ‘morning meeting,’ and there’s always some kind of touchy-feely thing.”

“We had to write down something we thought nobody knew about us on a little piece of paper and drop it in a hat,” Ashleigh grumbled, as they began making their way down the packed hallway toward first period English. “Then everybody tried to guess who was who.”

“Us too. Turns out Michael has a glass eye. Nobody noticed. What’d you say?”

“Pet fox,” Ashleigh said, shrugging. “Classy. Believable. Keep ‘em guessing.”

“Anybody peg you for it?”

“Bell rang before it came up.”

They joined the line of kids waiting outside Ms. Palmano’s classroom.

“What’s this one like?” Ashleigh asked.

“Don’t fuck with her.”

The other boy raised his eyebrows. “Oh?” he said. “That a warning, or an order?”

Conor shook his head. “For real. Don’t fuck with her, you’ll regret it. She went off on Meredith and it was scary. She doesn’t play fair.”

“Meredith,” Ashleigh said, his tone skeptical.

“I warned you,” Conor said. “Don’t expect backup if you start something you can’t finish.”

The stack of books fell to the floor as Ashleigh clutched at his own chest with both hands, a look of mock betrayal spreading across his face. “Abandoning me…for a teacher?” he gasped.

“Shut up,” Conor said, nettled. Together the pair of them bent down and began gathering up the scattered books. “I like this class, okay? She’s good. Better than Mr. Marowitz, even. I’ve got a B right now. I don’t want to mess around just because.”

“Oh, I get it,” Ashleigh said, peering ahead as the door opened. “It’s all becoming clear to me now. It’s boobs, isn’t it?”

Conor gritted his teeth, fighting back a surprisingly powerful wave of irritation. Ashleigh’s irreverence—his irrelevance—it was the same banter as always, but…

But that was the problem.

Ashleigh didn’t get it.

He couldn’t, because he hadn’t been there. Had been gone for two full weeks—lacked the context to even recognize just how much he didn’t know.

It’s like talking to Eddie.

Lost in thought, he followed the other boy on autopilot as the line began moving, then bumped into him as he came to a dead stop just inside the doorway. Someone else ran into Conor from behind—

“Don’t stop and gawk,” came Ms. Palmano’s voice. “Get in and get out of the way.”

The traffic jam dissolved.

Inside the room, the neat rows of forward-facing desks were gone, replaced by two long ranks on either side of the room, facing each other like bleachers on a soccer field. Atop each desk was a sheet of paper, annotated in red. The center of the floor was clean and empty, the handful of seats that hadn’t fit along the walls clumped together near the back, giving the whole setup the look and feel of a stage.

“Find your résumé and have a seat,” Ms. Palmano said. She was standing at her usual post at the front, watching them as they fanned out and began searching.

Conor glanced briefly at Ashleigh, who shrugged and made a beeline for the front of the room. Turning, Conor found his own paper almost immediately, on a desk just a few feet from his usual spot. The ever-present 85 was circled in red at the top, along with what was proving to be Ms. Palmano’s most common comment—the letters NSNB, meaning not special, not bad.

He settled into his seat, noticing as he did so that Holt, William, and Jennifer had all found their papers on the same side. Looking up, he saw Ashleigh waiting in silence as Ms. Palmano scanned the résumé he’d completed as homework. She spoke to him briefly, then handed the paper back to him, pointing toward a desk on the opposite side of the room, directly across the central space from Conor.

It could have been Conor’s imagination, but he thought he saw a slight ripple of hostility as Ashleigh walked down the row—a tightening of lips, a barely noticeable shift as students already seated leaned back in their chairs. Their eyes seemed to linger on the other boy just a little bit more than usual, and a few of them threw furtive looks at Conor, their eyes flickering away when he tried to meet them.

Guyler.

The other boy’s speech had clearly gone onto the grapevine.

Conor turned toward the board, looking for the instructions Ms. Palmano usually left there. There were none, but the blurbs from the previous lesson had been rewritten in a corner in smaller lettering. Beneath them were two neat lines of text; squinting, Conor was just able to make them out:

THEMISTOCLES OF ATHENS

HUANGDI, THE YELLOW EMPEROR

The-mist—no—them-is-toc—

He shook his head. Athens—that was either Greece or Rome, and Hu-whatever sounded pretty Chinese. There was nothing else written on the board, no hints or clues visible in the rest of the room.

Ms. Palmano began to speak, her voice cutting off the low chatter as if it were a switch. “I have divided you into two groups based on one very specific principle,” she said, without preamble. “Your first task for today is to figure out what that principle is.”

Conor and Ashleigh exchanged a look across the dividing line as the rest of the students took stock. There was no immediately obvious pattern—there were boys and girls on both sides, smart kids and dumb kids, a mixture of heights and weights and races.

Holt raised his hand. “Do we get a hint?”

“Are you sure that’s how you want to phrase that question?”

Holt lowered his hand.

Another girl raised hers. “Can you give us a hint?”

“Yes.”

An expectant silence, followed by groans and grumbles as the realization sunk in.

“Grades?” William asked, hastening to clarify: “I’m asking us, not her.”

A moment’s discussion ruled out grades.

“Could it be something with the alphabet? Last names, maybe?”

Also no.

“Stop guessing and think,” Ms. Palmano said.

Rami spoke up, from the far side of the room. “Our résumés.”

Ms. Palmano winked.

Thirty-five chins dropped downward, thirty-five pairs of eyes scanning the papers before them for anything that might resemble a clue.

Jennifer spoke up. “Can we have a minute to look at each other’s papers?”

“No,” said Ms. Palmano. “A minute won’t be enough. But you get an extra question for an idea that’s on the right track. Let’s try this—”

She reached into her desk drawer, pulling out two boxes of dry erase markers, one red and one blue. “Everyone on this side of the room, pick an item off your résumé and write it on the board on this side of the line, in blue. Everyone on that side of the room, same thing but in red. See if putting them all side-by-side helps you make sense of things.”

There was the usual moment of hesitation as everyone waited for someone else to step up first, and then Holt and Ashleigh stood up, followed quickly by the rest of the class. Space around the board soon proved to be the limiting factor, and Conor and Ashleigh came together at the back of the pack as their classmates wrote up their accomplishments two by two.

“So,” Ashleigh remarked, keeping his voice low. “Everybody’s acting scared.”

“Told you.”

“You know this just makes me want to try something even more, right?”

“Your funeral.”

They shuffled forward. The red list was filling up faster than the blue one, its entries shorter on average. At the top was Juggled four eggs, followed by 12 point buck, compared with All-county first chair clarinet and Published article in Binder Review, Community section. As Conor watched, a girl he didn’t know very well finished writing Toght myself gitar and handed the red marker to Ashleigh.

The sound of a brisk double knock drew Conor’s attention away, and he turned along with most of his classmates as the door to the room swung open, revealing the solemn, unsmiling figure of Mr. Sykes.

There was a collective stiffening as he entered, sharing a curt and professional nod with Ms. Palmano and proceeding silently toward the row of chairs at the back of the room. Neither of them spoke. The students watched with bated breath as the grey-haired man settled himself in one of the chairs and, either heedless of the apprehension his appearance had caused or simply accustomed to it, pulled out a tablet and began to tap quietly.

Conor turned back to the board, hoping to catch Ashleigh’s reaction, but the other boy was already stepping away, heading for Ms. Palmano’s desk. Turning back to the board—

He stopped short, his entire body freezing in place.

There, in the red column, right at the bottom in Ashleigh’s tight, blocky capitals, was a single sentence:

I CAN JUMP FROM THE AUDITORIUM TO THE GYM.

Dumbfounded, Conor actually reeled—had to take a small step backward to avoid losing his balance. Someone—he didn’t even register who—passed him a blue marker and made space for him at the board. He stood gaping for several seconds until the sound of Ms. Palmano’s gentle cough penetrated his astonishment, and he realized that he was the last one, alone at the front of the room. Fumbling with the marker, he scribbled something and lurched back to his seat, only thinking to actually look for Ashleigh when his brain no longer had to devote all of its power to keeping him upright.

The other boy had returned to his seat, was sitting slightly hunched, fingers laced together under his chin as he eyed Mr. Sykes warily. His expression was one of sudden sobriety and second thoughts, and Conor realized that he must have written his line before registering the presence of their AP.

(Conor wasn’t the only one looking. Though Mr. Sykes seemed utterly indifferent, the rhythm of his tapping steady and unbroken, at least a quarter of the class had made the connection between Ashleigh and his entry, and some of them were nudging their neighbors, pointing and whispering.)

Look at me, Ashleigh.

Look at me.

God dammit, LOOK at me.

Ms. Palmano gave a second cough. “I believe we were looking for a pattern?” she said.

Gradually, grudgingly, the class refocused.

(Most of them. Conor’s attention remained on Ashleigh, was aimed at Ashleigh, was being beamed at Ashleigh with as much force as Conor could muster, as if he could move the other boy with sheer mental pressure.)

“Is it, like, school stuff versus sports stuff?”

“No, look, it can’t be—soccer shows up on both sides.”

“I’ve got it! Half of us wrote in red, and the other half wrote in—”

“Shut up, Holt.”

That was a secret, Ash.

“Maybe the blue side is, like…jobs? And the red side is more hobbies?”

“—just for fun—”

“—you can get paid to juggle, though—”

“—circus freak—”

A secret, and it belonged to both of us.

“It’s got to be something simple.”

“She didn’t say simple, she said specific.”

“Same diff.”

It belonged to me. All you did was spot it. I’m the one who got us up there. I’m the one who did it.

“There’s no pattern. She’s messing with us again.”

“There’s totally a pattern. Look—blue is stuff your parents want you to do, and red is stuff you want to do.”

You have no right. You can’t—you haven’t even—you wouldn’t have even made it up there without me. You’re just giving it away and it’s mine.

“Doing what you’re told,” said Ashleigh.

Preoccupied though they all were, most of the students had left some small scrap of attention tied to Ashleigh’s corner such that when he spoke, they noticed, their heads twitching toward him as though tugged by strings, the other chatter falling away.

“Go on,” Ms. Palmano said, her voice carefully neutral.

“All the stuff on the blue list—it’s like Shireen said, it’s stuff your parents tell you to do. Stuff they brag to Grandma about when she comes to visit.”

“They don’t brag to Grandma that you can juggle four eggs?”

No one laughed.

William raised his hand. “My parents didn’t tell me to take Tae Kwon Do,” he said. “They wanted me to play football.”

“Bullshit,” Holt blurted. “You’re the one who broke a cinderblock?”

Several students gasped, their eyes darting to Mr. Sykes in the back of the room, who ceased tapping and looked up, his face unreadable. Realizing his blunder, Holt stiffened, his face paling.

Mr. Sykes said nothing, however, his own gaze shifting to Ms. Palmano as she crossed the room to add Holt’s name to a small list in the corner of the dry erase board, under a placard reading Minor purchases.

“Price, Mr. Avery?” she asked calmly.

Holt swallowed visibly. “Silent lunch?” he replied, a mixture of hope and apprehension coloring his tone.

“Just one? I don’t know if I can go that cheap. Maybe if we added a call to your mom?”

“Um. Three lunches instead? Please.”

Ms. Palmano nodded, making a small note next to Holt’s name. “Was it worth it, Mr. Avery?” she asked, her voice still mild.

“No, ma’am,” Holt answered, his knuckles white as he gripped the edge of his desk.

At the back of the room, Mr. Sykes began tapping again, and the room relaxed fractionally.

During the whole exchange, Conor had continued to stare unrelentingly in Ashleigh’s direction, watching as the other boy followed along, willing him to turn, to look up, anything. There were echoes in his head of Guyler, of Timothy, of his mother—all of their spoken and unspoken condemnations of the other boy’s character resurfacing as his own thoughts sprouted thorns.

Liar.

Traitor.

Thief.

“You were saying, Mr. Thompson?” Ms. Palmano continued.

William finished smirking and returned his attention to the front. “Nobody told me to take martial arts. I did it because I wanted to. So it’s not about doing what you’re told.”

“What if I told you it is, though?”

William frowned, and Ashleigh let loose a short bark of laughter.

(Conor caught the joke a half-second later, but it wasn’t funny enough to leave a dent in his anger.)

“Take a closer look at the lists, with that hint,” Ms. Palmano said.

Ashleigh did, along with everyone else. Conor didn’t. And then—

As the conversation continued, other students raising their hands, Ashleigh leaned back in his chair, turned his head, and locked eyes with Conor—just for a moment.

Nothing. Not even a crack in his easygoing façade. A grin, a nod, a casual disconnect. As if nothing was weird, as if nothing was wrong. Already his attention was back on the board.

You could try the rock thing again.

It was the worst possible time for his shoulder Ashleigh to speak, and something inside Conor snapped. Reaching down under his desk, he fumbled around until his fingers found the little leatherbound journal, still with him out of inertia and habit. Pulling it out, he opened it to the first empty page, a pen materializing in his hand. Without any intercession from his conscious mind, words began to spill out.

Other students were still talking—Holt, Jennifer, a few more he didn’t know, with Ms. Palmano’s crisp interruptions channeling the flow. Conor ignored them all, his head down as he scribbled, blood roaring in his ears. It wasn’t until five minutes and two full pages later, when his pen began to run dry, that his fury cooled enough for some part of the discussion to force its way through, catching his attention again. Looking up, he saw that Ms. Palmano had drawn an arrow between two entries on the board, annotating each:

SYSTEMATIC ANARCHIC

(top down)(bottom up)

“—Ben, on the other hand, just ran fast,” she was saying. “Sure, the conventions of miles and minutes are what let us measure how awesome he is, but the simple fact is, Ben’s achievement stands on its own, without need for context.”

“Hey, my achievement stands on its own,” Holt protested, his arms crossed. “Scoring from a goal kick is hard. Most people couldn’t do it even if the field was empty!”

Conor felt like he was waking up from a dream, a nightmare—felt drained, unbalanced, as if the act of writing had burned something out of him. He looked across the room at Ashleigh, who still wasn’t looking back—

“Right,” Ms. Palmano continued, “but notice that you didn’t say you kicked a ball eighty-five yards, into a five-yard target. You said you scored a goal as keeper. See the difference?”

They were too far in, the thread too difficult to catch, and Conor was just about to give up and turn back to the journal when Ashleigh spoke again.

“So this is about, like, internal or external? Whether you’re doing it for yourself or for somebody else?”

“Probably better to say intrinsic and extrinsic, but no—not quite. It’s not about why you do it. William earned his black belt because he wanted to, but he just as easily might’ve done it to make his parents proud. The difference is in the game itself—William and Holt were playing one. Ben wasn’t.”

There was something horribly familiar in Ms. Palmano’s tone, something about the way her eyes lingered on Ashleigh’s for a fraction of a second longer than they had to, and suddenly the fire in Conor’s chest rekindled. A once-bitten part of him seemed to surge forward, awakening with a sense of white-hot purpose.

Ms. Palmano was his, too.

His eyes darted about, scrambling for some kind of connection, something useful he could say. They landed on the journal—

Rules.

He was late to the conversation, was shooting in the dark, but he had to say something—

His hand shot into the air. “What if you’re making up the rules yourself?”

Ms. Palmano nodded approvingly. “That’s exactly the point,” she said, and Conor felt a wild, euphoric rush. “Why does Holt brag about the fact that he scored from a goal kick? Because we told him that it’s impressive, and worthy of respect. We invented a game called soccer, and built fields for it in our parks, and once or twice a week half the parents in the country drive their kids out to those fields, and meanwhile on television even the American soccer champs are making hundreds of thousands of dollars a year, easy. Holt’s achievement comes pre-measured and pre-cut—we know exactly how we’re supposed to feel about it. Holt knows exactly how he’s supposed to feel about it. Compare that to—let’s see—whoever it was that built their own PC. No trophies for that, not even in seventh grade.”

Jennifer broke in. “What if whoever built the computer—”

“Like we don’t all know it was Jordan—”

“—what if they did it because their parents wanted them to? Or whoever? Like, they’re on a computer science team, or something.”

“Context,” Ms. Palmano said. “Remember, I got to read your whole résumé. Sure, there are lots of things that could fall into the grey area, but when you look at the whole picture at once, it’s pretty clear. Somebody writes down spelling bee champion, they probably also wrote down AB honor roll, cub scout, student body rep, team captain. Somebody writes down highest flying paper airplane, I give you ten-to-one odds they also wrote about arm wrestling, breath holding, cake baking, and teaching themselves guitar—although please, don’t demonstrate, Kaitlyn, because there’s a pretty low ceiling on how good any seventh grader can be at guitar.”

There was some scattered laughter.

“Point is, there isn’t a lot of overlap. Most of you fell pretty clearly into one bucket or the other. Which leads us to Big Picture, Part One, because this distinction isn’t just about hobbies. We’re talking about two very different ways of viewing the world.”

Ms. Palmano was now pacing back and forth across the front of the room, her hands clasped behind her back. “Let’s imagine two hypothetical people for a moment, okay? We’ll start with Sid. Sid takes his education very seriously—he’s the kind of guy who already knows which colleges he’ll be applying to. He’s the one who gets irritated when you throw the substitute off track and she doesn’t make it through the material, because excuse me, have you even considered the fact that this stuff is going to be on the quiz next week either way?”

Everyone but Conor was looking at William.

“If there’s a group project, Sid is the guy you want to work with, even though it’ll probably end up with him yelling at you for not doing your part well enough. When Sid’s mom asks how his day went, he’s got seven different stories—one for each period, because that’s the stuff that counts—and then he’ll go wash his plate without being asked. Raise your hand if you know Sid.”

The hands went up.

“Now, let’s talk about Anna. Anna couldn’t care less about school. It’s not that she’s dumb, or lazy—it’s just that it all seems kind of pointless and arbitrary. She goes through the motions, but she’s not fussed about the difference between an A or a C, and she’s not going to get upset if the teacher forgets to take up the homework—it’s not like she spent more than ten minutes on it anyway. She’s got more important stuff to worry about—like the doodle she’s got going on under her desk, or the trip she’s planning to go on this weekend, or her friend who’s going to the dance with some jerk loser. Most of Anna’s stories take place in the hallway, or the cafeteria, or the bathroom, because that’s where real life happens. I assume you’ve all met Anna, too?”

They nodded.

“Okay, good. Now, let’s take Sid and Anna and put them on fast forward for about ten or fifteen years. We’re past college—let’s say we’re even past grad school, because let’s face it, Sid’s not going to be satisfied with a bachelor’s degree. Where are they now?”

Conor felt his brain surge into overdrive, once again scrambling for an answer—he had no idea where she was going with this, but he wasn’t about to let Ashleigh get there first—

Hey, uh, just checking: do you even hear yourself right now?

Ms. Palmano continued. “The party line says: Sid’s doing great, and Anna not so much. Right? That’s what your parents and teachers have always told you—you need to buckle down, focus on your studies, do the right thing now because it’ll pay off later. It’s not hard to imagine Sid as a brain surgeon or a CEO, and it’s pretty easy to imagine Anna cutting hair or waiting tables for eight bucks an hour. Maybe, if she’s lucky, she hit it big in music or Hollywood, but let’s be real, yeah?”

She paused again, gesturing at the words on the board behind her. “For better or worse, society is built for people like Sid. It’s built for them, and it’s built by them, and it works the way that Sid likes things to work. It’s systematic. It has to be—you can’t get millions of people all living together unless they’re willing to fall in line, follow the rules, work smoothly together. So people who tend to be good at that—who are good at doing what they’re told, who find that meaningful and fulfilling—they tend to do well, inside the machine.”

Conor felt his body tensing, as if bracing for a blow. The anger, the urgency—they were still there, but they were flickering, wavering, as the words Ms. Palmano was saying fought to claim his full attention. There was a but coming, he could feel it—

“But here’s the thing,” Ms. Palmano continued. “Anna’s not stupid. At least, not necessarily. There’s certainly a perspective that says it’s dumb to refuse to play the game, if playing makes you rich and not-playing makes you poor. But there’s another, equally valid perspective that says it’s dumb to play the game if doing it makes you miserable, and not-doing-it gives you at least a chance at happiness. Anna’s not wrong to value things the system can’t really give you. It’s maybe okay for Sid, because he likes that stuff, and all the work he’s putting in now is going to come back around someday, with interest. But Anna’s already bad at the game, and the prizes don’t tempt her, either.”

Conor was fully distracted, now—or perhaps it was more accurate to say that he had let go of the distraction, was no longer thinking about Ashleigh, had become entirely absorbed in the lecture, because Ms. Palmano was suddenly talking about him—

Wait a sec—which side of the room do you think you’re sitting on?

“So what does Anna do?” Ms. Palmano continued. “She chooses anarchy. Not chaos-in-the-streets anarchy, necessarily—although not necessarily not that, either. But she doesn’t look to the system for meaning and purpose. She builds her own life, bottom up, based on the things she finds fulfilling. Friendships. Hobbies. Adventures. Things that make memories.”

The teacher nodded toward the hallway, still visible through the door that Mr. Sykes had left ajar when he entered. “The thing is,” she said, “there isn’t really some real life waiting to kick in, five or ten years down the road. Things don’t just suddenly change when you turn eighteen. The adult world is somewhat more flexible than middle school, but a lot of it is just…more systems.”

She raised a finger. “I want to be clear—I’m trying not to take sides, here. I think it makes sense to be Sid, in that following the rules and doing what you’re told really does actually tend to pay off, in the end. College grads have much easier lives than high school dropouts.

“But there’s a certain kind of person that can’t bear to wait that long, and that person makes sense, too. If every single school day feels like seven hours of suffocation, then maybe it makes sense to give up on the dream of a six-figure salary and just go ahead and start living life, for real, right now.”

Conor’s heart was beating faster than usual, fast enough that he noticed it.

“It’s funny,” Ms. Palmano said, giving a soft, sad little chuckle. “Sid and Anna both look at each other and say gosh, grow up. Sid’s like, don’t you get it, this is how the world works—and he’s not wrong. And Anna is like, man, you spend your entire life jumping through other people’s hoops, fitting yourself into other people’s boxes, being a box for other people. And she’s not wrong, either.”

She turned to face them, straightening, her hands clasped behind her back as if she stood at an invisible podium. “Look around, kiddos,” she said. “The people next to you, the people across from you. Sid and Anna. Like it or not, change it or not, this is who you are today. Or at least, this is who you were yesterday, when you wrote out your résumés.”

“Come on, you can’t just cut people up based on stereotypes like that!” a girl protested.

“Says someone sitting on Anna’s side,” William pointed out.

Conor felt dizzy, disoriented. In spite of everything, he found himself looking over toward Ashleigh, catching a small frown on the other boy’s face and feeling its mirror on his own.

What did he write?

Ms. Palmano had barely glanced at Ashleigh’s résumé before assigning him to the far side of the room. Ashleigh, the math wizard, a literal choir boy who’d earned more merit badges in his single year of scouting than half the fifteen-year-olds in their troop, and who to this day still absolutely refused to jaywalk.

Ashleigh, who gave you a joint on a rooftop two weeks ago. Who put Guyler in the hospital. Who put cuss words in his fifth grade graduation speech and kept going until they literally dragged him off stage.

In fourth grade, he’d cut power to their entire school because he’d found a big red switch behind the cafeteria and wanted to know what it did.

And what about you, Bat-boy?

Conor looked down at his own résumé, the empty sentences he’d scribbled in haste after tearing up his first draft.

…why?

Why had he written those things down? Why had he torn up the other?

Which side of the room did he belong on, really?

Conor raised his hand.

Ms. Palmano nodded to him.

“What if we lied?” he asked.

For a moment, his teacher was speechless, her mouth hanging open around an unspoken word. Then her breath escaped in a laugh, and she shook her head ruefully.

“Touché, Mr. Moreton,” she said. “That honestly did not occur to me, as a possibility, and it should have. Care to switch sides?”

Conor shook his head, uncomfortable. He hadn’t meant it that way, just—

“I was just wondering,” he said, feigning indifference. “It’s just—some of the stuff on the board looks like it might not be true.”

There was a soft rustle as heads turned toward Ashleigh. Conor’s wasn’t among them.

Ms. Palmano glanced over her shoulder at the pair of lists and shrugged. “Generally speaking, Anna’s a better liar than Sid,” she said. “She’s got more practice, after all.”

There was a pensive silence, and Ms. Palmano picked up a marker and popped the cap, drawing a line under the words THEMISTOCLES OF ATHENS.

Conor felt a small knot form in his stomach, an unpleasant twist that was half anxiety, half shame. He raised his hand.

He had to stay late to finish a quiz in Science, got caught in a line in the bathroom after Social Studies, and ended up being dead last in the cafeteria when he knocked all of his things onto the floor just before the bell at the end of math.

They’d locked shins half a dozen times in every game they’d played, culminating in a double trip that ended with each of them slinging grass and mud at the other while the ball spun uselessly into the parking lot.

That was the same year that Ashleigh’s father had passed away, and Ashleigh’s mother sold their house and moved into Conor’s neighborhood, switching the other boy to Conor’s team in the process. Guyler Stevens in the hospital.

Okay, well, hitting him in the face with a ball could be fun, too.

Nope.

Maybe Mr. Sykes had some questions about what he wrote on the board.

No.

No, no, no.

“—think an air conditioner exploded, or something?”

“—gas pipe—”

“—just thunder, you guys—”

Conor’s eyes were glued to the closet, where the first coach had disappeared upward. It’s fine. They’re not going to find anything. If it was him, he was gone before they even blew the whistle.

(Conor stopped breathing.)

Mr. Sykes. Mr. Sykes, and the school nurse, her overlarge first aid kit slung over one shoulder.

Ashleigh.

I did it! See? Told you so. It wasn’t even that hard, really. Ankle’s fucked, but who cares. And the air time! That totally counts as flying, has to, I was in the air for like two whole seconds. Worth it. And right in front of everybody! They’re going to be talking about this one for years…

Did he want one?

Hollow.

The bag, the Gap, the bullies on the bus—even that morning, in Ms. Palmano’s class, as she shared with Ashleigh the same look that had previously been reserved for Conor, only Conor. Nothing you accomplish in life will matter.

That’s what Ms. Palmano had said, and she’d seemed to think it was a good thing, ultimately. That it might free them from trying to do what they thought they were supposed to do, encourage them to chase what they actually wanted.

Which is what, Bat-boy?

That night, when he couldn’t find the little leatherbound journal, he went digging through his desk, and then his drawers, and finally his closet, turning everything upside down. Everywhere he looked, he found mementos of his friendship with Ashleigh, and always with himself just half a step behind:

The trophy from their fifth grade soccer championship, where Conor had blocked the crucial shot, and Ashleigh had scored the winning goal.

The photo from the fourth grade play the pair of them had starred in—Ashleigh as Sherlock, Conor as Watson.

The game the other boy had given him for Christmas last year, with the final boss that only Ashleigh had been able to kill.

I don’t want to beat him. I just don’t want him to always beat me.

Equals, that was all. He wanted to be—to feel like—an equal. Not someone you could just ignore for two straight weeks, and then refuse to apologize to.

(There were tears, then, but he ignored them.)

He pulled another box off the shelf in his closet, and two small, soft objects came tumbling down—the masks they’d made for Halloween, Siphon and the Tracer. He studied them for a long moment—the curve of the lines, the arrangement of colors.

His was better, for all that that mattered when they were both just sitting on a shelf in the dark.

Okay, Ash. You want to show off?

Fine. I’ll play.
