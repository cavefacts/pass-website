Twenty-five Mississippi. Twenty-four Mississippi. Twenty-three Mississippi.

With a scattering of gravel, Conor skidded to a halt at the edge of the roof, catching the lip with one hand and turning sideways as he vaulted past and down, sneakers slamming onto the breezeway six feet below. Whirling, he ran down the center of the aluminum awning, his footfalls like thunder. When he reached the end, he ran over the edge without hesitation, this time dropping onto the grass at the corner of the eighth grade building.

Fifteen Mississippi. Fourteen Mississippi.

The momentum of his roll took him right behind one of the school’s giant holly bushes, into the dusty, musty space between its branches and the wall. There was a small, industrial-looking metal box bolted to the brick, with pipes coming out of it and disappearing into the ground, and that gave Conor the boost he needed to reach for the window that he knew was hanging open, eight feet off the ground. Hauling himself up and through the narrow gap, he grabbed the top of the stall frame and dropped to the tile floor, sending up silent thanks for the fact that there were no girls in the bathroom.

Eight Mississippi. Seven Mississippi.

Despite the countdown in his head, he spared a moment to kick his shoes against the wall, anxious to avoid leaving a trail of incriminating footprints. The hallway was empty as he burst through the bathroom door, the voice in his head calling out one Mississippi as he hurled himself across, pushing through the doorway opposite and into the boys’ bathroom—

The bell rang.

—where he sprinted to the first stall and locked himself in, his heart pounding, his chest heaving. Outside, the hallway began to fill with students, the sounds of squeaking shoes and locker doors and the babble of conversation swelling until they masked the sound of Conor’s coughing. There was a notable rise in volume when the bathroom door swung open, and he hastily pulled off the mask he was wearing, unzipping his pants as he turned toward the toilet. Still shaking, he spilled several drops onto the seat as he peed, electing to use the last three squares of toilet paper to wipe his face and neck instead of cleaning up the mess.

He counted out another thirty seconds as his breathing slowed fractionally, trying to judge the best moment to leave the stall. He wanted the bathroom to be full, but not so full that people would be standing around waiting for their turn, with nothing to distract them from his flushed face and slightly too-small frame. Making the decision based on feel, he pulled open the stall door and wormed his way out, skipping the sink and heading straight for the exit.

The hallway was packed with eighth graders, twin streams pushing through the double doors in opposite directions. Conor joined the crush, trailing slightly behind a pack of girls as they stepped out into the sunlight and headed for the cafeteria. He stuck with them until the last possible second, breaking off as they went inside, pretending to read the student council fliers posted in the vestibule as he waited for another source of cover.

There were plenty of stragglers still throwing their food away from second lunch, and soon enough Conor was drifting along with a clump of his fellow seventh graders, walking right back toward the middle school cluster. A pair of angry-looking teachers brushed past them, striding purposefully toward the eighth grade wing, and Conor allowed himself a tight little smile. It wasn’t until he was back at his locker that he felt the last cord of tension loosen in his shoulders, the hairs on the back of his neck shifting from attention to parade rest. Surreptitiously transferring the mask from his pocket to his bookbag, he shut his locker door and turned to head back toward the cafeteria for third lunch.

He hadn’t even gone three steps before a hand fell on his shoulder, prompting an explosion of adrenaline in his chest that sent shrapnel tingles into every muscle of his body. Jerking instinctively away, he whirled, barely suppressing a gasp of relief as he registered the face of Nolan Brinkley, looking almost feverish.

“Did you hear?” the other boy asked, his voice quivering with excitement. “Ashleigh—he came back again.”

There were rules to this game, and each time Conor put on the mask, he added another, or two, or three.

(That was almost a rule in and of itself, a way to stay sharp and focused, keep himself from growing cocky or complacent.)

Some of them were obvious, inevitable, included only for completeness—like the cardinal rule, his First Commandment:

Don’t get caught.

Others were niche, narrow, cobbled together from chance observations and careless mistakes. These were the ones that Conor disciplined himself to remember, repeating them over and over. Each was a tool in his toolkit, a tiny trade secret that might one day make the difference between victory and failure.

Never lose track of your shadow.

Roll your feet—don’t tiptoe.

Mulch sticks to clothes worse than grass does.

He used them like a scalpel each time he planned a new mission, cutting away the bad ideas until only viable ones remained.

Never drop where they can see you.

Always have a second way down.

Once you’re on the ground, go where there’s a crowd.

There was a limit to how far they could take him, he knew. This couldn’t last forever—all it would take was one grownup in the wrong place at the wrong time, one loose brick or shaky pipe, one teacher who spotted the sweat leaking through his uniform and put two and two together—

Cross buildings—never run along them.

If you’re not wearing the mask, don’t carry the mask.

Wash your hands as soon as you can.

—but so far, it was working.

There was a subtle irony to the way it had all come together. Ashleigh’s defiant leap—

(Leaving Conor feeling something; he hadn’t had words for it, hadn’t been able to name it, but it had burned inside of him like magnesium.)

—then Conor’s encore, a daring stunt aimed at puncturing the other boy’s reputation—

(Except that it had ended up reinforcing it, instead, everyone drawing exactly the wrong conclusion.)

—and afterward, the school’s response, a massive mobilization aimed at preventing a third incident that no one had been planning.

(At which point he basically had to, right?)

There hadn’t even been a goal, in the beginning. Just pressure, building and building and building, until Conor found himself alone on the roof of the library on Wednesday morning, hiding behind a ventilation shaft, watching the buses roll in as he wrestled with himself.

The route itself was simple, no technical skill required—down onto the breezeway, up onto the office roof, back down onto the breezeway opposite, and then back up onto the cafeteria, where he would disappear from view as soon as he climbed down the pierced brick wall surrounding the dumpsters. Thirty seconds, at a generous overestimate—

—but it would take him along the entire length of the courtyard where the middle schoolers were gathering, the courtyard that the high schoolers were passing through on their way to the inner quad.

You can’t seriously be afraid that they’ll catch you.

His own voice, Ashleigh’s voice—it was hard to tell which was which, in that moment. They were blending together, switching sides at random, each arguing caution and brashness in turn.

You’ll be gone before the teachers even move. Most of them probably won’t even see. Even if there’s randomly someone on the ground by the dumpsters, you can just double back around to the magnolia.

But still, the fear. The same fear, familiar—he wasn’t afraid he would hurt himself, wasn’t scared he might fall, but it was the same feeling regardless, no different from what he’d felt on the rooftop all summer, looking out across the Gap—

You could just go home.

He could just go home. No one would ever know what he hadn’t done; there was no one to judge him, dock him points.

And that was the problem, really. Was why (Conor realized) Ashleigh had written it on the board—to make himself do it, set up an expectation he would then feel obligated to fulfill.

(And it had worked.)

(All those times they had trained together, over the summer, and Conor had never once insisted, never once told Ashleigh look, this one isn’t optional. He’d tried to be reassuring, supportive, gentle with the one thing they both knew he did better. Right from the start, he’d made it clear that he had no expectations of the other boy, and he was only just now beginning to see that there were two sides to that coin…)

How many of you have been told by your parents that you can be anything you want to be?

Conor had thought about it as he climbed back down, defeated. As he rejoined his classmates in the courtyard, hearing Ashleigh’s legend spreading all around him, the story being told and re-told and re-told again. He had forced himself to listen, taking it as penance for his cowardice, and that afternoon, he’d followed Timothy right up the stairs and let himself into the other boy’s bedroom.

(The door had not been locked.)

“I’m going to cut across the rooftops,” he had declared. “Tomorrow, before first bell, while everyone’s in the courtyard.”

He had tossed Ashleigh the pair of masks, and the grin that had spread across the other boy’s face was like sunlight, like gold. They sat together for almost two hours, hashing out the details, thinking through potential problems, chipping away at the uncertainties until the plan was so solid that the actual act felt almost a formality.

And then he’d returned to Ashleigh’s room the following afternoon, triumphant, to share the hilarious news—that nearly every single person in the school had concluded that the mysterious figure had been Ashleigh.

“So that’s why Mr. Sykes called this morning, looking for me,” the other boy said.

(Serendipitously, the call had gone to voicemail—Ashleigh had simply not felt like picking up, and so had utterly failed to establish an alibi.)

“I’m going to run with it,” Conor said. “It won’t fool anybody for long, but—”

Again the grin.

The other kids bought it—the teachers seemed to buy it, even—the only person who seemed to harbor any suspicion at all was Holt, his voice unusually quiet, his eyes lingering until Conor met them, then shifting uneasily away.

The second mission had been the closest of the four—in a reckless gamble, they’d decided that he should go for an exact repeat, crossing the rooftops at the same time on the very next day. It was such a terrible idea (they reasoned) that the grownups would assume that no one would be stupid enough to try it, and therefore they wouldn’t actually bother to defend against it.

(They were right, but they had underestimated just how quickly the teachers would make it around to the far side of the cafeteria, now that they were on high alert. Conor had ended up taking a torturous detour across half the buildings in the school, doing his best to throw walls in the paths of his fastest pursuers and circling back on himself until he found a quiet corner he could drop into. Luckily, more than half the students in the school had come pouring into campus, whooping and cheering, and ultimately his was just one flushed, breathless face among many.)

It might have stopped there, except that the following Monday he’d disembarked from the bus to find that the entire student body was being funneled straight into the gym, as if in preparation for a hurricane. Three days went by before they let the students gather in the courtyard again, and there were fewer teachers supervising them than usual, the rest of the faculty scattered across campus, guarding all the obvious spots.

Ashleigh had scoffed.

“They really think that’s going to stop you?”

And it might have—before. Of the two of them, Conor had always been the cautious one, the anxious one, the drag on Ashleigh’s recklessness. He’d drawn a hard line at half mad, and on more than one occasion his cold feet had saved them both from disaster.

But he was changing, now—had maybe already changed, the two runs he’d done transforming him, the old Conor sloughing away. This newfound feeling of power, of potential—it was addictive, and he was finding that his gift for anticipating danger was just as useful for troubleshooting crazy plans as it was for torpedoing them.

They’d given it one extra day, to lull the staff into a false sense of security, and then he’d gone for his third target—a small, level gap between two outstretched wings of the music building, facing a commons where the upperclassmen went for break. He’d turned in his Social Studies quiz a few minutes early and asked to be dismissed to the bathroom, whereupon he’d grabbed his books from his locker, darted over to the high school side, scaled the patterned brick façade on the back of the math building, crossed the rooftops to the music wing and broken the jump without skipping a beat, slinging his bookbag down to the ground and dropping meteorically after it as shouts and cheers rang out behind him.

(With no teachers present, he’d had only students to dodge, and he’d made it back to the middle school side just as the dismissal bell rang. By the time third lunch rolled around an hour later, the story had already spread throughout the school, another feather in Ashleigh’s cap.)

Ashleigh, for his part, was doing everything he could to draw attention to himself. Twice he’d limped theatrically away from his driveway as Timothy got on the bus, and he invited Holt, Eddie, Nolan, and Sam over to his house nearly every afternoon, offering ludicrous alibis and non-denial denials whenever the topic turned to Conor’s latest exploit. His ankle injury—a modest sprain, as it turned out—was healing rapidly, and he frequently “forgot” his crutches when he stood, only to stutter and collapse dramatically as soon as someone noticed. With the notable exception of Holt, they seemed to be buying it, awe and admiration clearly visible on their faces.

(Eddie was particularly enthusiastic about playing along, his usual stream of nonsense littered with retractions and corrections as he repeatedly pretended to almost give the game away.)

Still, there was only so much they could do to muddy the trail. Conor’s complicity in the first two missions had been covered by the fact that no one kept track before homeroom, and they had judged the between-class excursion acceptable in light of Ms. Nease’s absentmindedness. But there weren’t that many moments during the day when Conor could disappear unnoticed…

“You should start skipping classes when nothing happens,” Ashleigh suggested.

“What? Why?”

“So people don’t notice that Clark Kent disappears every time Superman shows up,” the other boy explained, in a come on, keep up sort of tone. “Maybe get caught making out in the bathroom, or something—”

“I dunno if your mom’s willing to come all the way out to East Binder during the work day.”

“I mean, she was kind of hoping you’d move on soon anyway. She was mostly just kissing you out of pity; she prefers guys who’ve hit puberty.”

“Dick,” Conor said, sticking out his tongue.

He wasn’t at all sure what to make of the other boy’s easy camaraderie, the abrupt and total reversal of their previous estrangement. On the surface, their friendship had returned to normal, full of brag and bluster. They were messaging one another every night, and Conor continued to bring Ashleigh his homework assignments, collecting them from Timothy on the bus each morning.

Yet in light of all they’d left unsaid, the lack of awkwardness was itself suspect, the laughter too easy, the banter smooth and almost pat.

I don’t get it. Isn’t this what you wanted?

No. Not quite. This was how Ashleigh was with everyone else—the untouchable good humor, the self-assured exuberance. Gone were the rough edges, the savage honesties and quiet sincerity that had marked their something-special.

He probably just needs a little more time.

To fill the hours, Conor poured himself into his training. He was strong, now, and getting stronger, with even his longer training sessions leaving him only moderately sore the next day. He’d climbed Mr. Baker’s little brick wall so often that he’d rubbed a hole in the sole of one shoe, and he was landing one target jump for every one he missed.

(Almost, anyway—thirty-seven out of seventy-five.)

His newfound endurance was serving him well in soccer, where he had been assigned to midfield and was tentatively confirmed as a second- and third-quarter player for their upcoming game. He’d had only one really embarrassing moment, and that one not entirely his fault—while chasing a wild ball past the bench where Guyler was sitting, he’d felt something catch his ankle and gone face-first into a puddle.

His schoolwork, on the other hand, was suffering. Ms. Palmano’s class was now officially his only B, and math was threatening to tank him entirely. It wasn’t just that he was splitting his evenings between jumping and scheming instead of doing his homework—he was losing focus during the day as well, his thoughts increasingly drawn to nebulous ideas for his next mission.

He’d been caught on a new possibility, a gap unlike any of the ones he’d broken so far. Just as with Binder’s Gap, the solution had been obvious almost immediately, with the one small drawback being that it was completely, utterly insane.

He had first noticed it during his mad escape after the second mission, when he’d been crisscrossing the rooftops, searching frantically for a way down. He’d seen it out of the corner of his eye, come back to it later that day, studying it from below in spare minutes between classes.

It was roughly twelve feet across, one side made up of the squat, garage-like shop classroom, the other the brick wall of the high school science wing. With a running start, and a boost from the raised lip at the edge of the roof, Conor could cover the distance easily. The catch was that the second roof wasn’t at the same height as the first, but was instead a good three feet higher.

He’d thought about trying to make up the difference by simply running faster, jumping harder, but a few test runs in his neighborhood playground had put that idea to rest—the distance was simply too great. And so, he was forced back to his original plan.

Some kinda crazy Wile E. Coyote bullshit—

Shh.

Whenever Conor climbed a wall—whether it was the tiny one in Mr. Baker’s yard or a real one like the ones by the bank—there was a moment when he hung just below the edge, arms extended, elbows around his ears, his fingers gripping the top and his feet against the vertical surface, knees pulled up tight to his chest. It was a surprisingly solid position—after working on it for a few sessions, he’d found that he could hang like that for almost a full minute before his forearms started to shake.

But that’s just hanging. What you’re talking about is way worse.

He could jump the twelve feet between the two buildings. He just couldn’t make it up the additional yard. But if he went for it anyway, and managed to get his shoes to a spot three-or-so feet below the edge…

…that would put his hands at exactly the right height to catch the top. Instead of landing downward, on the roof of the science building, he could land forward, on its vertical face, trading his own height for the last bit of wall.

There were half a dozen reasons why it was a terrible idea. He could come in too low and bounce off, or slice his hands open slipping off the raised metal edge. He could smash his face, his chest, his knees, break his ankles or his wrists. He could even make it, only to have his arms rip out of their sockets—for all he knew, it would be like trying to catch a moving train.

But the more he thought about it, the more he became convinced that these were merely technical problems, a set of logistical hurdles between him and a solution that was fundamentally sound.

It could work. The question was, could it work for him?

It was that question that had brought him back to East Binder, four hours after sunset on Saturday evening, his nose running in the chill night air. He would have preferred to study the problem in daylight, but between soccer, homework, and the increasing vigilance of the teachers, nights were now the only option.

(He also found himself strangely reluctant to share his new idea with Ashleigh—a feeling which he deliberately did not examine—and there were fewer questions from the other boy when he disappeared after dark.)

A harsh, yellow light illuminated the gap, its quiet buzz the only sound now that the crickets were going to ground for the winter. Conor stepped forward to the edge of the shop roof, looking out at the science building, its wall deceptively, tantalizingly close.

He tried to picture it, in his mind—what it would take, how it would feel.

It was hard. The thing he was imagining was unlike anything he’d ever done—the only remotely relevant experience he could think of was the time he’d overjumped coming down from the roof of the supermarket. He’d been aiming for one of the large, green electrical boxes, and he’d almost missed it entirely, his feet landing just a few inches shy of the edge, his palms slamming down to stop himself from tumbling headfirst onto the grass below. The pain in his wrists had kept him out for over a week, and he didn’t relish the idea of repeating the experience.

But I won’t be falling this time, so—so the impact shouldn’t be so extreme. Right?

Well. He would be falling, still, actually, but it should be more or less incidental, with most of his energy going forward.

Unless you end up higher or lower than you think.

Conor grimaced. There were just too many variables, too many questions he had no easy way of answering—should he be more concerned about the strength of his fingers, or the placement of his feet? Was he going to have more trouble with gravity, or with the fact that he was basically hurling himself straight into a wall at top speed? Should he try to lead with his hands, or his feet, or both? Did it matter?

It probably matters.

Sighing, he turned, hopping up onto the breezeway, walking softly around to the science building, lowering himself down into a hang, testing the grip.

If I come in too steep, my feet are going to slip out—skid down the wall.

But was that better or worse than trying to absorb all of the shock with bent knees?

Conor didn’t know.

Okay, then. The hard way, just like Binder’s Gap.

He could get part of the way there by approximation—finding smaller, similar gaps and attempting the same sort of technique in miniature. But there were only so many ways to mock up a given obstacle. At some point, he would have to commit to the real thing, and when he did, it would become binary—he would succeed, or he would fail.

And he could not fail. A mistake here wasn’t as obviously-lethal as a forty-eight foot drop, but it was still something he couldn’t afford.

My arms.

His arms were the weak point.

With Binder’s Gap, there had been only one real factor to take into account: impact. He would make the lateral distance or he wouldn’t—the question had been whether or not he would drive his knees up through his ribcage on the landing. In the months leading up to it, he’d been able to get a sense of just how much shock he’d be dealing with by dropping into mulch beds and sand pits, gradually pushing his way up to twelve feet, strengthening his legs as he went. The actual jump had still been terrifying, but there hadn’t been any surprises when he’d finally broken through.

This problem was a whole new level of complex. He would have to cover all of the bases, preparing equally well for any number of possible mistakes. And he was naturally further behind—however extraordinary Binder’s Gap might be in scale, he’d spent his whole life using his legs to take impact. Up until a few months ago, it had been years since he’d put all of his weight on his arms. A drop of just a foot basically doubled your effective weight—if he came in at the wrong angle and his feet slipped out, he might end up taking as much as two or three hundred pounds with just his fingertips.

This was going to take time.

Time, and work.

He turned to climb back down, his body switching to autopilot as his thoughts began to wander. A part of him was already brainstorming—weighted topovers and one-armed hangs, maybe?—while another part was reluctantly crossing monkey jump off the list of possibilities for his next mission.

(Everything else on the list was crossed off, too. If he didn’t think of something soon, he’d be back to the idea of retracing his previous paths, which was risky and lame.)

Mulling, he drifted through the deserted campus, his eyes on the rooftops, looking for inspiration. He did two full circuits, looping his way around every building—

Nothing.

He checked his watch. It was only a few minutes after eleven.

Still early. You could hit the Gap before you go.

He hadn’t been back since Ashleigh had broken it.

End on a high note?

Five minutes later, he was halfway through the magnolia, picking his way across by feel in the darkness—

His foot dislodged something.

His foot dislodged something, and the something fell down through the branches and out of the tree, making an oddly familiar sound as it went—something both commonplace and out of place, not like a stick or a pinecone at all.

What…?

Curious, he lowered himself down a couple of branches and peered at the ground. The mulch below was in deep shadow, and he couldn’t see anything unusual in the dark…

He checked his watch again. Still not even eleven fifteen—he could drop down and take a look and still have time to climb back up if he wanted to.

Swinging sloth-style on the lowest branch, Conor let go with his feet and dangled, released, wincing at the jolt as he hit the ground a split second earlier than expected.

(He hated dropping in the dark.)

He circled the tree, peering through the darkness, shuffling his feet as he walked. On his fourth pass, his toe brushed against something small and hard, and he bent down to pick it up.

It was a book.

Holding it by the cover, he shook it gently, letting the mulch and bark it had collected during its fall drift away. He couldn’t make out any of the pages in the gloom, so he stepped out from under the magnolia, back into the cold September starshine.

He frowned.

It was a small book, bound in black leather, and he recognized it immediately.

His journal.

For a long moment, Conor just stood there, trying to make sense of it. The little book had gone missing, what—a week and a half ago? Sometime around Ashleigh’s jump, anyway—he remembered searching for it that night and being unable to find it. After a while, he’d simply stopped looking, and it had dropped to the back of his mind.

I must have never got it back from Ashleigh, he thought. He’d been leaving it in with the other boy’s homework, after all. Maybe Ashleigh had found it, hung onto it, and then—

And then what?

And then brought it to school and left it in the tree?

Conor inspected the cover. It was hard to tell for sure, but it looked pristine, not weathered at all, and they’d had a thunderstorm only a couple of days earlier. However the little book had gotten there, it hadn’t been waiting long.

A note, maybe?

Flipping it open, Conor squinted down at the first page.

Still too dark. He began walking toward the auditorium. There was a light on the far side, and if he could just take a closer look—

He stalled.

The page was covered in red marks, looking almost black in the harsh yellow light. They draped across his writing like twisted shadows, a mass of corrections and annotations. There were strikethroughs and carets, whole sentences rewritten with punctuation where Conor had left none. In the margins, a narrow, angular script spelled out a series of smug little taunts:

Don’t you have to know “your” and “you’re” to pass third grade?

Oh, man. “They’re” and “we’re,” too. Maybe the problem is “re”?

Never mind, I figured it out. You can’t read, so you can’t spell check.

Plus I bet it’s hard to write when your hand is permanently wrapped around someone else’s dick.

Conor felt his body go cold and still. Somewhere else, somewhere distant, a storm was rising—panic and humiliation and outrage—but here all was icy calm.

He thumbed to the next page and skimmed it, then flipped past the following four in quick succession. Every single one of them had been defaced, scarcely a line left unmarked. Even his math notes, jotted down when he hadn’t had another sheet of paper—half of the problems had been crossed out and completely reworked, while the other half had been overwritten, mistakes corrected with heavy, dark strokes.

And in the margins, on the blank pages—

A constant refrain of cruelty, variations on a theme, the same two or three insults clothed in a hundred different words.

Come on, you KNOW how to use an apostrophe. You’re just being fucking lazy.

Oh, are we making fun of retards now? Is this like black people saying the N-word?

Man, if THIS boring shit is what you pick to write down…

I was going to make fun of you for not knowing what to do with periods, but I guess you’re a late bloomer.

Would HAVE, not would OF. Moron.

Loser.

Faggot.

Dipshit.

A complicated sort of feeling was growing, swelling, filling the hollow space inside of him as anger continued to rage around the edges. People stole journals, sure. People read them, spread their secrets, maybe even trashed them.

But this?

Conor felt indignant, horrified. Violated, even—as if the journal were an extension of his self, the jagged words an unwanted touch. This was the sort of thing that was Not Supposed To Happen. It was barbaric. Gross. Uncivilized.

Who?
