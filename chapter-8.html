“Okay, how about…invisibility, telepathy, or super speed?”

“How super is the speed? Like, if I run really fast uphill, can I fly?”

“No, it’s just, like, Quicksilver. Normal stuff, but like a hundred times faster.”

“And my clothes don’t burn off? No sonic booms?”

“What? No. The fuck? You just go fast.”

“Okay. What about the telepathy? Is it like Professor X? Or just mind reading?”

“Ashleigh—”

“Look, if we’re gonna do this, let’s do it.”

“Fine. Mind reading only.”

“Range?”

“Seriously? I dunno…a hundred yards?”

“Through walls?”

“Sure, why not.”

“Can I, like, filter? In a crowd? Or is it like trying to eavesdrop in the cafeteria?”

“Filtered.”

“Can I dig through their memories? Or do I just hear what they’re thinking right now?”

“Ashleigh.”

“Hey, fuck me for asking questions. Super speed, then invisibility, then Costco telepathy.”

“You’re the only person I know who would actually be disappointed if he woke up one morning and found out he could read minds.”

“In a world where mind reading works, there’s like fifty other more awesome things that should be possible.”

“Whatever. Your turn.”

“Okay. Option A, telekinesis, but it works by just transferring muscle power, so you get tired and sore and if you push too hard you end up pulling power from, like, your heart, and you die.”

“What the fuck, Ashleigh.”

“Option B, you have the power to send your mind back in time, but only by thirty seconds, and you can’t chain jumps together to make it longer. Option C, you can shapeshift, but while you’re shifted, you’re blind and deaf unless the thing you shifted into has eyes and ears.”

“Is this what you think about all day?”

“Helps me last longer with your mom.”

“The shapeshifting thing. Can I turn into stuff way bigger or smaller than me?”

“Max ten ex in either direction.”

“If I get hurt while I’m shifted, am I hurt when I change back?”

“Yeah.”

“So…why wouldn’t I just shift into an indestructible robot?”

“Now who’s asking too many questions?”

“You’re just mad that I found a loophole.”

“Let’s say…it takes more concentration and energy to stay shifted, the more different from your natural body you are.”

“How fast does it work? Can I like turn into water right before somebody shoots me?”

“Let’s say it takes ten seconds.”

“One Mississippi. Two Mississippi. Three Mississippi…this is a shitty power.”

“Made up your mind, then?”

“Time jumps, shapeshifting, telekinesis.”

“Same. I could do a lot with a thirty-second do-over.”

“Man. Tell me about it.”

Conor’s forty-seventh monkey jump was very nearly his last.

He was tired—on the verge of collapse, really, his limbs trembling, a hair-trigger cough lurking in the back of his throat—and to compensate, he’d leapt from the column with every scrap of power he could muster. Better to make it, even if it meant a heavy landing, than to kamikaze straight into the unforgiving corner where the wall met the pavement.

But he’d underestimated his obstinate muscles, had overshot and come in too high, his momentum driving his feet toward a point just barely below the top of the wall—and to make matters worse, he had reflexively leaned back, positioning his legs to take the impact as he would for a target jump, leaving his hands to grab at nothing.

Oh, fu—

Rebounding, he fell, twisting frantically in the air as the ground rushed up toward him. A series of impacts occurred in quick succession—

—his shoe, the concrete—

—his palm, the concrete—

—his hip, the concrete—

—his shoulder, the concrete—

—and he disappeared into a blank whiteout of pain and shock.

For a time, there was nothing. Awareness returned in steps, the wash of agony draining away into bright, narrow rivers of pain, which gradually deposited their contents into a handful of deep pools scattered across his body.

Oh god how bad is it is it broken did I break my wrist my hip did I hit my head what if I can’t walk—

With a feeling like slamming a door, Conor shut off the flood, steering his mind away from panic, from damage assessment, from conscious thought of any kind.

Breathe.

Breathe.

Breathe.

Four breaths, then five—ten—by the time he lost count, his higher self had reasserted control, was calm enough to open his eyes, shift his head. He was lying on his side on the pebbled concrete, halfway between the wall and the column, and there was no blood.

(Not on the outside, anyway.)

He wiggled his fingers and toes, more relieved than he cared to admit when they responded without hesitation. Gingerly, glacially, he rolled over onto his back, lightning dancing around his body but never quite striking home. Ignoring the protests of his overworked muscles, he sat up, pulling his heels underneath him and wrapping his arms around his knees.

Lucky.

He was lucky. His head seemed fine—there was no nausea or dizziness, and he felt nothing out of the ordinary when he ran a hand through his hair. His shoulder, hip, and wrist were ablaze with pain, but it was thick and uniform, without the sharpness he remembered from when he’d broken his arm in elementary school. A dozen steps and a handful of cautious shrugs confirmed it—he was hurt, he was bruised, but he wasn’t broken. Only his wrist seemed genuinely injured—it was the same one he’d tweaked the night before, and the double trauma was bad enough that moving it brought tears to his eyes. Even so, though, there was only a little swelling, with no indication that he’d done any worse than sprain it.

Idiot.

A part of him had known that he was making a mistake, that he should have stopped after ten—after five, even—that he shouldn’t have been there in the first place—that pushing forward in his current state was crazy, reckless, asking for it. He had rules about this sort of thing—rules that would have saved him, if he’d bothered to follow them.

Rule number one: protect yourself.

But Conor had not come for rules, was not there to be safe. Was not even there for the monkey jump, really—had come instead for the fear, the exhaustion—for the chance to beat himself up, wear himself down, dash himself to pieces and sink into oblivion.

He had come to disappear.

(But he was back, now, the pain like a leash, tethering him to reality.)

Somehow—

(He had no memory of how, none whatsoever.)

—somehow, he had made it home the night before, as if he had teleported, found himself pacing the space between his desk and his bed at four thirty in the morning, unsure whether he had slept, unnervingly unsure whether he was sleeping. The world had been somehow not quite there, as drink-muffled dullness gave way to livewire jitters. His mind had buzzed, his muscles had buzzed—even his skin had seemed to buzz, somehow, as if the air in the room were charged with static.

Did that really happen of course it happened what do you think you made it up or something are they going to arrest me nobody saw you Ashleigh saw me no he didn’t that was before—

His thoughts had run wild, looping back on themselves, lurching suddenly in new directions, sometimes drying up entirely for long, long seconds before bursting forth in sudden flood.

Why would he do that why did I do that that was crazy that was awesome that can’t have been real he just kept crying he just kept screaming well you broke his shoulders why did he KISS me—

He had felt himself spinning, spiraling—had sensed, somehow, that some critical guard rail was missing. That he was losing himself, losing his grip—that his thoughts were wobbling further and further away from familiar territory—that the mental landscape he usually traveled was tiny, insignificant, a mere oasis of sanity, and the untraveled wilds his mind was capable of reaching were vaster than he had ever suspected, a yawning, incoherent void.

This must be what Ashleigh felt like, when he—

(Flinch.)

The snap of the high schooler’s collarbone, the shriek like a tortured animal—

The rapist, you mean.

This must be what Batman—

Batman isn’t real!

Guyler’s blood on the sidewalk, Ashleigh’s breath whispering across his lips—

He lied to me. He’s been lying to me for—since—why didn’t he just TELL me—

Ms. Palmano’s voice, low and sultry like he imagined when he locked his bedroom door, tickling in his ear, shiveringly close—The first step in taking control of your life is realizing that you already have it—

I did it it was real I finally did it I stopped a bad guy was it what you wanted no are you sure yes isn’t this what you were hoping for training for isn’t this exactly what you expected let me take it back please let me take it back—

And under and through and over it all, the fear—that he felt like he was going crazy because he was going crazy, that this was exactly how people went about losing their minds, it wasn’t like there was any special reason why he, Conor, couldn’t go crazy, if there was nothing stopping Ashleigh from—

(Flinch.)

And over and over, in a kind of short-circuit:

I have to talk to him.

Ashleigh. He had to talk to Ashleigh.

But I can’t he won’t let me he ran he didn’t stop well you didn’t stop him what was I supposed to do I don’t know maybe anything at all instead of just standing there—

Eventually—

(Six A.M.? Wasn’t it four-thirty, like, TWO minutes ago?)

—eventually, some frightened part of him had gathered enough strength to hack its way through the static, to scream at him that he had to do something, that he could not just stay in this room by himself while his sanity boiled away. His hand was on the door handle—

Parents.

He’d gone back to scribble a note, registered that his bedsheets were flat and unconvincing—

So I didn’t sleep, then.

—had rumpled them and then left, the newborn November sky still fully dark, his breath misting, the scattered Halloween decorations seeming to squirm and slither whenever he wasn’t staring straight at them.

(The crunch of bone, a scream that went on and on and on and on—)

(What’s the matter, Bat-boy—didn’t like getting your wish?)

And then finally: the plaza, its familiar shapes eerie in the buzzing yellow light, everything somehow colder and harder and sharper, the walls carved from ice. Conor had stood on the column, praying—

—and mercifully, the world had snapped back into focus, drawn in its wings and settled, shrunk, solidified. His first jump had pulled him halfway back to sanity, and the second halfway again, the wildness slowly receding as reality reasserted itself.

And then he had pushed too far, and fallen, and that, too, had been healing, somehow—had reminded him that the infinite void he had touched was just that, the void, there was a reason people didn’t cross the yellow lines on the road and it was because that was crazy.

Crazy. You could go crazy.

Conor could still feel it, the gut-drop sense of awful possibility, the wide-open anythingness, but the ache in his body had anchored him, and he was grateful for it.

There’s a billion—no, more than that, a trillion—no, a, a, a whatchamacallit, a googolplex—

There were an almost infinite number of ways that he himself could be—infinite rearrangements of the uncountable atoms that made up his body—but only a few of those—a very, very few—added up to anything like a boy named Conor Moreton. Most of them added up to nothing at all. Just a pile of matter, empty, meaningless.

(It was a strange thought, still touched with the wildness of the sleepless night, and almost too grownup—like the sort of thing his mind produced when he was trying to impress Ms. Palmano. But it fit, somehow, and as he turned it over and over in his mind, he felt the last pieces of himself jostling back into place, felt the craziness fade and was himself again.)

Which…

…wasn’t great, actually, given the circumstances.

The first question, he said to himself—

(Slowly, buying time to think as he began the long walk back to the neighborhood, limping only a little.)

The first question is…

The sentence refused to finish itself.

Eh. Worth a shot.

It was the voice of his shoulder Ashleigh, and upon hearing it, some small part of Conor relaxed, unclenched, realizing only in that exact moment that he’d been afraid that somehow the voice in his head would have fled, too—that this time he wouldn’t even have the pale echo of his friend’s memory to comfort him.

…I don’t think it works like that?

Yeah, sure, fine, that was obvious now.

But anyway, isn’t the first question “where did I go?”

Conor stopped walking.

I mean, either way, right?

Either way. Whichever came first, talking about—that, or—or that—

Either way, Ashleigh—

(The real Ashleigh.)

—Ashleigh was where the conversation would happen. Was, himself, the other half of it.

Okay, Conor thought.

Okay.

But…shouldn’t he figure out what he was actually going to say, first?

I mean…

(Ashleigh’s voice again.)

…it’s a long walk, isn’t it?

“No, he isn’t here.”

Conor blinked, feeling like he’d tried to climb a dozen steps only to find that there were actually just eleven.

Mrs. Falhanner stood in the driveway, the car door hanging open beside her, her purse still dangling from her shoulder. She had the look of someone who had only been home for a few moments, and not to rest—there were dark bags under her eyes, and her makeup was cracked.

She had been coming out of the front door of the house as Conor rounded the corner, had seemed not to see him—or at least, had locked the door behind herself and walked straight down the brick steps and to her car without pausing, only turning to look up when he’d called out to her.

“Do you know when he’s coming back?” Conor asked. “I—”

—need to talk to him, were the words that had almost slipped out of his mouth.

(But repeated experience had taught him not to use words like need with the sort of grownup who didn’t quite believe that a kid could need anything.)

(That, and there was something new in the way Ashleigh’s mother was looking at him, some subtle strangeness that he did not recognize and could not decode.)

“—thought we were going to meet up at the Plaza, but he wasn’t there,” he finished.

Mrs. Falhanner stood unmoving, her tired eyes fixed on his, her face still set in that unsettling, unfamiliar shape. “You can text him,” she offered flatly. “Or call.”

Conor opened his mouth, paused. “Uh.”

Saying I already did that, he didn’t answer suddenly didn’t seem like a good idea.

(Wariness rising, a wordless warning from somewhere in the back of his mind—)

She hadn’t answered his question about when the other boy would be back.

She hadn’t answered his question in the specifically unhelpful way that Conor himself didn’t-answer questions he did not want to answer, from people he had no intention of helping…

Mrs. Falhanner shifted, a sort of pointedly intentional motion, her keys jingling loudly, and Conor realized he was standing still with his mouth open like he was trying to catch flies.

“Right,” he said.

He drew his phone from his pocket. Ashleigh’s mother just watched, silent, inscrutable.

Scruted your MOM last—

Feeling a bead of sweat forming on the back of his neck, Conor sent a quick message.

Seconds passed.

“No answer?” the grownup asked, her voice crisp.

“Uh,” he said again.

“Guess he’s not available, then,” she said. “If you’ll excuse me, Conor, I need to get to work. You boys will have to catch up later.”

She gestured—a sharp, authoritative motion—and Conor backpedaled, falling away until his shoes found the damp, untrimmed grass that bordered the driveway. Bending into the car, Mrs. Falhanner shut the door, started the engine, threw him one last unreadable look through the window, and then backed down the driveway and disappeared around the corner.

That…

Conor stared after, his thoughts stuttering. He felt the way he felt when Mrs. Atkins called on him to answer a question he hadn’t even heard—had the sense that something had just happened—

No, scratch that, something had definitely just happened, it wasn’t just a sense, but he had no idea what. He had called out to her with—with a flow, of sorts—a plan, if only the most basic kind—is Ashleigh here? No? Okay, then, on to the next place—

But the look in Mrs. Falhanner’s eyes, that strangely empty quality in her voice…

She had looked at Conor like—like—

Like she didn’t like me very much.

The way Eddie sometimes looked at him, or Eichardt. Except—

Except she was a grownup, that wasn’t the sort of look grownups gave kids, and besides, she did like him, she always had, she was warm and friendly and welcoming, even when she was tired, even when he and Ashleigh were in trouble—

He paused, his throat tightening.

Could Ashleigh have told her? About—

No.

No way.

(But he didn’t sound very sure, even to himself.)

And then an even stranger thought—

Could she have been lying?

Grownups lied all the time, of course. Conor knew that. Everyone knew that. They lied about time, about food, about money, about sex. About anything that would embarrass them, or make them wrong—anything that might pierce the illusion of competence that they honestly seemed to think they were maintaining.

But they didn’t—

Conor squinted at his own thought, suspicious.

They didn’t lie like people.

They didn’t lie personally—not that he’d ever noticed.

Except—

Except of course they did, it was an odd way to think about Mrs. Falhanner but she was a person, Conor wasn’t used to thinking of his best friend’s mother in those terms but of course she was a person, of course she had thoughts and opinions and—and goals, things that she wanted, things that were every bit as real and important to her as Conor’s goals were to himself, the way she lived her life was ridiculous and incomprehensible just like every other grownup but that didn’t make her not a person.

Which meant that maybe Ashleigh was inside the house. Maybe she was just lying to him.

(Because she was mad at him, somehow—it was hard to picture a grownup being, like, actually properly mad at a kid, as opposed to just Disappointed or whatever, but if Ashleigh really had told her, or—or if she’d just sort of picked up on it, somehow—)

Conor looked down at his phone.

If he won’t answer your text, he’s not going to open the—

The sound of a door opening made Conor jump.

It was Timothy, looking down from the landing, his face taut, eyes wide and slightly wild beneath a brow drawn down like a knight’s visor.

“Is Ashl—”

“No,” the younger boy said, cutting him off. “What happened?”

“What?”

“What happened last night?”

The boy’s voice matched his eyes, and the set of his shoulders—tense, pressured, slightly accusing. Conor blinked, shook his head, tried to force his thoughts back on track, kick his battered brain into gear.

“I,” he began, before giving up and starting over with “He,” only to scrap that as well, finally settling on “What do you mean?”

Timothy’s eyes narrowed, the hint of wildness kindling into fire. “Don’t fuck with me,” he growled. “Something happened. Last night. And you know what it was.”

For a split second, Conor felt an impulse to laugh.

You think I know what happened just because I was there?

“He came home last night?”

“Around two in the morning. Drunk. Really drunk. He had scratches all over his hands, and his clothes were all torn up. He threw up twice. I had to wash the—look, fuck, Conor, what happened?”

“Where is he?”

“You tell me!”

(Mrs. Falhanner hadn’t been lying, then.)

“When did he leave?”

“Maybe an hour ago. Before Mom got home. Conor. What. Happened.”

Conor opened his mouth, hesitated—

Probably should’ve come up with a story ahead of time, huh.

What could he say? What could he say that definitely, absolutely, one hundred percent would not count as a betrayal?

“He didn’t tell you?” Conor asked cautiously.

“The fuck you think I’m asking for, fun?” Timothy seethed. “He didn’t say anything. Literally nothing. Not a single goddamn word. And he—he looked like he’d been crying again, it was hard to tell with how smashed he was, but—”

“If he didn’t want to tell you—”

Slam.

The rest of the sentence popped like a bubble as Timothy flung the door shut behind himself so hard the house shook. He stalked down the steps—crossed the driveway—came right up into Conor’s face as if trying to loom over him from below.

“He is my brother,” the younger boy hissed. “My brother. And something happened last night, and whatever it was—whatever it was, Conor, it fucked him up worse than I’ve ever seen him, I thought I was going to have to take him to the hospital.”

The younger boy reached up, slowly, as if to grab Conor by his shirt, or maybe his neck—

—but the fingers twitched, curled, and two tight fists sank slowly back down to his side.

(Conor began breathing again.)

“And now, he’s gone, and—and you know why, and I don’t know—I don’t care—whatever it is that’s going on between the two of you, you are going to tell me right now because he’s my brother.”

Quiet, where the younger boy would usually shout. Slow, where the words would normally tumble over one another. Glacially cold—

No. Not cold.

A laser, the fire compressed, controlled, hammered down into a narrow beam, giving off no heat. Timothy’s eyes burned into Conor’s and Conor searched, scrabbled, scrambled—digging through his exhausted mind for some way, some words to say, something he could offer the younger boy that wouldn’t mean crossing a line he couldn’t uncross—

But there was nothing.

(Or if there was something, Conor was too stupid and tired to find it.)

“I don’t know, Timothy,” he said, his own voice equally quiet. “We were on the roof at East Binder, and Ashleigh—there was—he tried to do a thing and he got, um. Scared. He got scared and he ran off and I—”

Conor swallowed.

“I don’t know what happened after that.”

•••

I don’t know what happened after that.

The words haunted Conor as he walked, as he searched, crisscrossing the city and the wilderness around it.

Ashleigh wasn’t at the railyard.

He wasn’t at the culvert.

He wasn’t at the mall, or the playground, or the park.

He wasn’t at Nolan’s, or Eddie’s, or Holt’s.

He wasn’t even at school—after nearly three hours of wandering, Conor had finally caved and gone back, had even gone all the way up to the auditorium roof. But there was nothing there except two narrow paths in the gravel and the empty bottle that Ashleigh had dropped.

And so, dirty and aching and more tired than he’d ever been in his life, with only the pain in his wrist keeping him from falling asleep on his feet, Conor turned back toward home.

If only.

If only Conor had gone with him.

Or stopped him.

Or said anything at all.

But he hadn’t.

A shower.

He needed a shower, and sleep, simple things, now-things, things that would keep him here, in the present—

Like your parents?

He winced. He knew he would not be able to dodge them, not without more of an effort than he was currently capable of. It was going to be a problem—he knew it would be a problem if they caught him, if they started asking questions—but there was nothing to be done about it. His universe had shrunk to the size of his next five steps, his vocabulary reduced to tiny, atomic chunks like bed and pillow and totally fucked.

(And Ashleigh, of course—even in his stumbling fugue, the other boy never left his thoughts for long. But those thoughts didn’t go anywhere.)

By the time he reached his driveway, Conor had gone beyond shuffling and was almost staggering, his movements stiff and zombie-like. He had walked at least ten miles—had scaled fences, crawled through bushes, taken shortcuts through ditches and alleys and cluttered back lots. He paused for a moment to consider his reflection in the window of his mother’s car—

No point.

Feeling each and every muscle and every single bruise on top of them, he dragged himself into the garage and up the steps to the laundry room, shedding his now-noxious shoes. It still wasn’t quite noon, and it was Saturday—there was a chance, at least, that his parents were still in bed, or watching TV together—

“Conor? Is that you?”

He couldn’t even muster the energy to swear inside his own thoughts.

“Come into the living room—look who’s here!”

For a moment, some fuzzy, befuddled part of him poked its head up and wondered Ashleigh? But no—even with his brain running on fumes, he could hear that his mother’s tone was all wrong for that. This was how she sounded when there was A Guest In This House, Young Man, and Won’t You Please Be On Your Best Behavior? Miss Helen, from next door, or his mother’s friend Miss Julie…

“Coming,” he croaked. He peeled back his soiled jacket, briefly considered trying to wash his face and found that no part of himself felt like lifting a finger to do so. Drawing in a heavy, resigned breath, he walked through the kitchen and turned the corner into the living room—

Probably his heart didn’t actually stop. Probably it didn’t even skip a beat (not that Conor was entirely sure he knew what that was supposed to feel like).

But something tore through him, as his eyes landed on the slim figure sitting across from his mother, something that felt an awful lot like the time they’d grabbed the electrified fence on the border of Mr. Plumblee’s pasture. He was aware that he had stiffened, straightened—that his eyes had gone wide—that his jaw was suddenly clenched and his pulse was thundering in his ears—

It was Ms. Palmano.

Ms. Palmano was in his house.

His teacher, Ms. Palmano, was here, in his house, sitting across from his mother, sipping tea.

There was a moment of spinning panic in which his poor overworked mind skipped straight past the impossibility of the situation and leapt, horrified, to the pair of soiled boxers that lived in the cramped space between his bed and the wall, a mad, mindless fear that she had been in there, that she’d seen, somehow, or—or smelled, that she knew, that they both knew, oh god, his mother, too—

His mother was talking.

She was talking, and he should probably pay attention to that, but his eyes were glued on what was she doing here, though—

“…Conor?”

He blinked—shook his head—forced himself to focus.

His mother’s eyes were gentle, and creased with laughter. “Too many Snickers,” she murmured, with a sidelong glance at how, what, why—

"A noble tradition," said the dry voice of his teacher, coming from the face of his teacher, attached to the body of his teacher which was definitely there, right there, sitting on the couch in front of him in defiance of all reason—

“As I was saying—now that you seem to be here with us—guess who shops at the same Food City!”

Conor’s eyes tore away from Ms. Palmano, landed on his mother. “Uh,” he said. “…she does?”

For some reason, both grownups laughed.

“We ran into each other in the condiments aisle,” his mother said, waving him into his father’s armchair. He limped over and sank into it, the leather cool against the back of his arms, his neck. “She’s been right around the corner this whole time! She moved into that lovely little house with the green shutters—the one over on Aspen Avenue—last June. And since she’s the first teacher you’ve ever said anything good about since Carol Browning back in second grade…”

She trailed off, and shrugged, as if to say of course, it’s COMPLETELY NORMAL, that’s totally enough of a reason for me to bring your TEACHER into our HOUSE—

“I’ll confess, I had an ulterior motive,” Ms. Palmano broke in. Her voice took on a slightly different tone, a more elevated cadence. “‘In every corner of the humble house a memory; in every nick and scuff upon the walls a story—in the shape of his family I at last perceived the boundaries of the mold into which he had been grown, and in tasting the soil of his roots the tree of him made sudden sense.’”

There was a long silence—confused, on his mother’s part; expectant, on Ms. Palmano’s; reluctant, on Conor’s.

Why.

Finally, he spoke.

“Uh. Yours?” he said.

She grinned. His mother quirked an eyebrow.

“She gives us quotes,” Conor explained, feeling as if the room around him were spinning. “We have to guess if they’re real or not.”

“Tsk, tsk,” said his teacher, still grinning. “I hope I never said real—I wouldn’t want you thinking that a given thought is fake just because no one else thought it before. But yes.” She shifted her gaze back to Conor’s mother. “Your son is quite good at the game; I don’t know if I’ve managed to slip any past him, yet.”

“Fascinating,” his mother replied. “We’ve always been very proud of Conor’s intellect, but I don’t know if we’ve ever thought of him as being particularly…literary.”

“One need not be well-read to have the soul of a reader.”

“Which reminds me—is it true that you don’t assign any reading at all? I would have thought that reading lists were required by the state…”

Conor felt his brow furrow as the conversation continued, the two women batting sentences back and forth as if playing ping pong. They were doing that thing that grownups did, where their voices got all on-purposey, and they started saying words that didn’t quite mean what they meant, expecting other people to figure out the riddle. It was standard procedure for his mother, but it was downright odd to hear Ms. Palmano doing it.

If it had just been Miss Helen, or some other ordinary visitor, now would have been the time to slip out of the room, or to let his attention wander completely, but the weird sideways words were about him, or at least they seemed to be. It was like he wasn’t really all the way there—like he was on the other side of the house, overhearing.

“—no exaggeration, one of the students whose adult writing I’m most excited to see.”

“Mmmm,” said Conor’s mother, her lips thinning just a bit as she lowered her cup. “I would hope that would be reflected in his grades, somehow. Last I checked, I think you’re giving him a B. A low B.”

Conor groaned aloud. Neither of them seemed to notice.

“Oh, I wouldn’t say I’m giving him any sort of grade at all,” Ms. Palmano countered, her voice somehow reminding him of Devon. “The specific number or letter is entirely under Conor’s control—moreso than you might think. But your son’s grade does reflect the fact that he has more potential as a writer than his final drafts reveal. But no. No—even if everything had been fine between them, even if that last awful day of summer had never happened, he couldn’t really say, with a straight face, that he would have told the other boy about his burgeoning crush on their English teacher. It was too—too—

Sacred.

Sacred was the word, was the right word, it felt ridiculous to think it under the circumstances but it was true regardless. And Ashleigh—if there was one thing Ashleigh did not do, could not seem to comprehend and might, in all seriousness, despise—

Conor looked down at the battered, filthy shape of his best friend, lying motionless on the bed, his head still hidden beneath the pillow.

(He could smell him. Could smell them both, the smell of both of them together, that end-of-day smell after hours and hours of climbing around, warm and gross and familiar.)

No, Ashleigh and sacred did not get along. And there was something here that Conor did not want touched, would not have wanted the other boy to see, to know about, the jokes would have been good-natured but they still would have been jokes—

Joked your mom last night.

Ashleigh shifted on the bed, and the rest of the thought unraveled, Conor’s poor exhausted brain unable to draw it back together. Something about irreverence, and kryptonite…

So, obviously I’m in love with you.

(Said coolly, casually.)

…but it was gone. Gone, and with it Conor’s sense of direction, of purpose. He dropped his head into his hands, scrubbed at his eyes, glanced blearily over at the clock. It read 12:15, and he didn’t know, had absolutely no earthly idea what it had said the last time he’d looked, nor how long they’d been sitting there, nor how long they had left.

“What now?” he asked dully.

“I’m sorry,” the other boy answered, from under the pillow, his own voice sounding no less worn-out. “I want to just—can we just go back?”

Conor frowned. “You mean, pretend you didn’t—”

“No, that’s not—I mean, yeah, I’m sorry about that too, but that’s not what I—”

He broke off, and Conor’s frown deepened.

Time passed.

“Ash?”

The other boy grunted.

“What were you—I mean, what did you mean?”

“I’m giving up,” said the voice beneath the pillow. “It didn’t work. Control-Z.”

“What didn’t work?”

“Forget it. Doesn’t matter. It didn’t.”

It felt like Conor’s entire body was frowning, now, his muscles drawing tight around his confusion.

“What didn’t work, Ashleigh?”

The other boy lifted his hand off the bed and waved it vaguely around.

“What didn’t work, Ashleigh?”

Both hands, this time, in a what do you want from me? sort of shrug.

“Ashleigh.”

“Your mom.”

“Ashleigh.”

Silence.

“Ashleigh.”

“Truth or dare.”

“Truth.”

“What do I have to say to get you to drop it?”

More silence. Conor could feel his anger heating up again, there was something here and Ashleigh knew it, was—was trying to hide it, to shrug it off, but he’d messed up, had let the mask slip for just a second, and for once Conor had managed to not-miss-it.

“I’m not going to drop it,” he said, the words like steel striking against flint.

“Why are you doing this?”

A note of pleading, a hint of panic—

“Because I think you want me to.”

The words had come from nowhere, had emerged from Conor’s mouth without any associated thought or intent, and it was only after they had been said that he heard them, himself—held them up against the previous hour and noticed how well they fit.

“Right?” Conor pressed, feeling his way forward.

He was here, he came here, he was waiting all morning.

“If I’m wrong, you can just tell me to fuck off.”

“Fuck off.”

The words came swiftly, without hesitation, but they were hollow, unconvincing.

“Really?” Conor asked quietly. “Do you really, actually, for-real want me to fuck off?”

“Yes.”

“For real?”

“Yes.”

“Then say the safeword.”

The silence that followed stretched, and stretched, and stretched, as Conor tried and failed to imagine what was going on inside of the other boy’s head, he was clearly torn about something, but what it might be he had no idea—

“You’re going to be mad,” Ashleigh whispered.

“So?”

“I can’t—I don’t want you to—”

“To what?”

More silence.

…Ashleigh didn’t want him to be mad?

Since when?

Ashleigh had never cared what Conor thought. Cared for Conor, sure, but they had fought and argued over practically everything since the very first time they’d locked shins on the soccer field. Conor couldn’t think of a single time when Ashleigh had held his tongue or changed his mind just because Conor thought differently, just because Conor might disapprove—

Oh.

Oh, shit.

The pieces were falling into place, suddenly, had not yet fully clicked but were coming together, the overall shape visible to Conor for the very first time.

That was before.

Ashleigh, who was aloof and invincible, who had always been untouchable precisely because he did not give a single fuck—if Ashleigh had started to feel, about Conor, the way that Conor had started to feel about Ms. Palmano—if Conor’s opinions had started to matter to him, even a little bit—

So, obviously I’m in love with you.

It fucking sucks.

I’m giving up, it didn’t work.

Somewhere inside of Conor, he could feel pressure building, feel the temperature rising—

And then he tried to—to—to drive me away, except he couldn’t, I wouldn’t let him, and anyway he didn’t really want to, at least not all the way—

“You tried to stop caring about what I think.”

He was amazed at the steadiness of his own voice. Over on the bed, the pillow shifted as Ashleigh nodded underneath.

“But it didn’t work.”

Another nod.

“You do care about what I think.”

Another.

“But you wish you didn’t.”

Stillness.

“Ashleigh?”

The other boy’s fingers had curled into fists, pulling on Conor’s sheets, the knuckles whitening.

(Inside Conor, the pressure was still rising.)

“Ash—”

“I said I was sorry,” the other boy whispered.

And the pressure inside Conor doubled, then—tripled—rose to a horrible, intolerable peak and then kept rising because there was nowhere for it to go, no way for him to let it out—

“The thing is,” Ashleigh said, and it sounded like he was crying, now, crying underneath the pillow. “The thing is, you’re going to—to—to this, it’s going to be the same, but it’s going to be at somebody else, fucking Jennifer probably, you’re going to be off having this—this whole other life and I’m not—I’ll just—it won’t have anything to do with me, I won’t be part of it, you won’t want me there, you won’t want me to see—”

Conor’s limbs were trembling, now, they were trembling and his face was hot but he couldn’t move, couldn’t think, he knew somewhere in the back of his mind that he wasn’t supposed to shout but he was having a hard time remembering why.

“—and I don’t—I don’t want to give a shit, whatever, it’s fucking lame, I’m lame, I just want to g-g-go back to how it was before, but I can’t, one way or another it’s just—it’s just over, you’ll be at fucking prom or whatever and I’m just going to be stuck with this—this—this fucking hole, it didn’t used to be there, I don’t want it, nobody asked me if I wanted to want—”

“So you fixed it,” Conor heard himself say, and his own voice was like iron, like ice, cold and flat and glassy smooth. The other boy was still under the pillow, so he couldn’t see the way that Conor’s face was twisting, twisting as he fought with himself, as two opposing winds tore at him like hurricanes.

On the one hand, he finally, finally, finally understood. There were no more mysteries, no more confusions—the why of all of Ashleigh’s bizarre, inscrutable, cruel behavior had finally been revealed.

And on the other—

He tried to kill our friendship.

Knowingly. Intentionally. Unilaterally. Had decided, all on his own, that the parts of himself that had fallen in love with Conor were a problem, a problem to be dealt with—had tried to burn those parts out of himself, and the effect that this would have on Conor hadn’t stopped him, hadn’t given him pause. Ashleigh had looked ahead to the future and decided that the most the two of them could have didn’t count, wasn’t worth saving, was not even worth trying.

(And he hadn’t told Conor, and it had to be because he’d known, on some level, that Conor would have stopped him, would have pulled them both back from the brink, Conor believed in their friendship and Ashleigh knew that and so he’d just—he had just—just routed around it, like unplugging the smoke detector, buckling the seat belt behind your back so the car wouldn’t beep.)

And for months, Conor had been trying so hard—had been hanging on with every last scrap of patience, of resolve—and now Ashleigh was sad because it hadn’t worked, had the sheer audacity to complain to Conor about how he couldn’t make himself stop caring—

Conor felt himself rising, watched as if from the outside as he stepped back and stood upright in the corner of the bedroom.

“Get out of my house,” he heard himself say, the words like ashes crumbling.

(It was the wrong move, the wrong moment. The wrong moment to end the conversation, the wrong moment to make decisions, the wrong moment for anything. It wasn’t the end of anything—except for Conor’s own ability to think, to cope, to continue existing. He had—at last—reached his limit, the true and final end of his thin and fraying rope.)

The other boy sat up, bewildered, his face a wreck of snot and half-dried tears. “Wh—what?” he said.

“Get out of my house,” Conor heard himself repeat.

“Conor, wait—I’m trying to tell you I’m s—”

“GET OUT OF MY HOUSE!” Conor heard himself bellow, and twenty seconds later, after the front door had slammed shut, he watched himself step out into the hallway and begin the long, slow walk to the office where his father stood in the doorway, waiting.
