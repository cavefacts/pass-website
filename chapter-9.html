Conor stood alone in the empty, echoing hallway, feeling awkward, feeling apprehensive, feeling trapped. There had been a note waiting on his desk when he’d walked into first period—face down, Ms. Palmano’s crisp, neat handwriting instantly recognizable when he flipped it over:

Go out into the hall and wait;
I’ll be there as soon as everyone is settled.

He was still holding it—had dropped his bag and slipped out through the flow of incoming students and only realized that he’d brought the little slip of paper with him after the tardy bell had rung. It was like a magnet, heavy in his hand and in his mind—four separate times he had spaced out and snapped back into the moment to find that his eyes were fixed on it, his gaze hanging on the semicolon like a coat on a hook.

It had not occurred to him to wonder in any specific way what his teacher wanted, or whether he was in trouble. He was anxious, but the anxiety was fuzzy, formless. For days, the loudest parts of his brain had been silent, as if on strike.

Beside him, the door opened, and he looked over in time to see Ms. Palmano slow as she stepped out into the hallway—like a fight scene shifting into bullet time, her teacher-briskness melting into something softer, nearly half human. Her eyes met his, and they looked at each other for long enough that some other Conor, in some other mood, might have squirmed.

“What day is it?” she asked abruptly.

Huh?

A panicked scramble, his brain rummaging through its pockets—

“Wednesday,” he said.

(Too late.)

Ms. Palmano’s face didn’t change, though—just bobbed up and down in a gentle nod, as if he’d answered exactly as she’d thought he would. “What do you have fifth period?”

He was quicker this time. “Math.”

(For a moment, he felt a whisper of confusion, a tiny flicker of curiosity, but before he could decide what to do about it, it vanished.)

“And you’re on third lunch?”

He nodded.

“Were you planning on paying attention in math today?”

His eyes narrowed.

“Probably not,” he admitted.

“Fifth is my planning period,” she said. “The room is empty. If you want, you can come by straight after fourth, and skip lunch. I’ll let Mrs. Atkins know. You’d have until 1:35—over an hour.”

He blinked, slowly. “To do what?”

“I haven’t the slightest,” his teacher said. “I can try to help you figure it out then, if you want. But you’ve been a zombie for the past two days, and between that and all the cuts and bruises, the next step is getting the social worker involved. Seemed like maybe giving you a little extra breathing room was worth a shot, first.”

This ominous pronouncement produced very little reaction in the place where Conor felt things. Very little in the way of conscious thought, either—it was the wordless, animal parts of him that raised their heads, and considered, and concluded that an hour and a half alone in Ms. Palmano’s room sounded just fine, actually, thank you very much.

“Okay,” he said.

She held his gaze for a moment, as if searching, and then nodded, slowly. “As for this period. Do you want to sit at your desk, or out here?”

Again a quiet weighing.

“Out here.”

She nodded a final time, and turned, and vanished back inside the classroom, leaving him alone.

You’ve been a zombie for the past two days.

Yes, that was fair.

Conor had fallen asleep on Saturday afternoon, long before the sun went down—had slept like a corpse until 8AM Sunday, when his father had shaken him awake for his punishment: a full day spent together as a family, the first of what would allegedly be every Sunday until Christmas.

It hadn’t been bad. It hadn’t been good, either, but his father and mother had been quiet, gentle, inoffensive. There had been no interrogation, no needling, no demands for fake enthusiasm. They’d simply spent time together—a morning walk, brunch at Badger’s, shopping in the early afternoon. Reading together in the living room. Conor’s mother had recruited him to handle the pasta while she prepared veggies and chicken for dinner, and she had filled the silence with comfortable chatter that only required him to nod and grunt.

It had been healing—sort of. Grounding, at least—stabilizing, some part of him that had been vibrating almost hard enough to come apart gradually stilling to something more like a tremble. At the end of dinner, his father had asked him how he felt—not how he was feeling about anything, but how he felt right then, in that moment.

“Fine,” he had answered.

His father had shaken his head. “Close your eyes, bud. Take a deep breath. Pay attention.”

Conor had closed his eyes, taken a deep breath, paid attention.

“Slow,” he’d amended.

“Slow like a cloud drifting? Or more like a turtle walking?”

Conor had considered. “Like honey.”

His father had nodded, and let him go, recommending (but not ordering) that Conor stay off of his phone, his computer.

(He had, but not for the reasons his parents wanted. Because that was where Ashleigh lived, where his green dot might be lurking, where any number of messages might have piled up over the course of the day.)

He had ricocheted slowly around his bedroom for the rest of the evening, fiddling with LEGOs, scribbling with colored pencils, opening up science fiction books and closing them again when he couldn’t make his eyes focus. He’d tried to masturbate—almost dutifully—but had been unable, his shoulder Ashleigh uncomfortably close, uncomfortably present, the pillowcase still holding the other boy’s scent. He’d fallen asleep sometime around nine, and dreamed of avalanches.

Monday and Tuesday had been a blur. His mother had offered to drive him to school, and he had accepted gratefully, quietly relieved that he hadn’t had to ask, unsure if he would have asked. Both days, she had dropped him off just as the bell had rung, and he’d headed straight in to homeroom without lingering in the courtyard.

Ashleigh was there, of course—in English, in Activity—but Conor had kept his distance, avoided looking in the other boy’s direction. For his own part, Ashleigh hadn’t forced the issue. He’d been uncharacteristically silent in Ms. Palmano’s class, with none of his usual brash challenges or clever comments.

Conor had been silent, too. English, Spanish, Science, Social Studies, Math—there had been a brief moment when he had locked eyes with Guyler, and felt the urge to write something, but he hadn’t had the slightest idea what. Besides, the little leatherbound book was at home, lying on the desk in his bedroom, not forgotten so much as forsworn.

He hadn’t thought about it—hadn’t thought about any of it—had gone now more than three full days without giving any real thought to anything that had happened on Halloween, or on the day after. It wasn’t that he was trying not to—there was no resistance, no sense of clamping down. The words simply weren’t there, the part of his brain that was responsible for generating them lying dormant.

And now it was Wednesday, and he found himself sitting in the hallway outside of Ms. Palmano’s classroom, still not thinking about it, but for the first time in days aware of that fact—like becoming lucid inside of a dream without fully waking up.

Hi, said the voice of his shoulder Ashleigh, and it was shy, tentative—almost timid.

(It hadn’t spoken since Saturday.)

Hi, he thought back.

(Thought to himself, because the voice of Ashleigh in his head was him, after all—was just the part of himself that liked to pretend, that had adopted the other boy’s mannerisms as a shield.)

He let his eyes drift lazily around the empty, echoing hallway, taking in the double rows of lockers, the chipped and shabby tile, the harsh, industrial lighting. Down at the far end, almost a football field away, a lone student was lingering at a locker that might have even been their own. A few minutes earlier, a teacher Conor didn’t know had walked briskly past, running whatever inscrutable errands teachers run.

(But those people weren’t real, didn’t feel real—were just background detail, procedurally generated. Even as the occasional kid shuffled by on their way to or from the bathroom, Conor was alone in the hallway, separated from the rest of the world by some vast, invisible barrier.)

Do you, uh. Want to talk about it?

No. He did not.

Okay. Cool. It’s just—

Conor gave a soft sigh.

I don’t know, he thought to himself. I don’t have answers.

Okay, well. Are we going to talk about the other thing, then?

(Conor could feel it, pressing against his thigh, the pocket of his uniform khakis bulging—the mask. His mask—the face of the Tracer.)

He had picked it up off the floor of his bedroom on Sunday night, had shoved it into his bookbag and refused to tell himself why. For two and a half days, now, he had carried it with him in every class except Activity, just in case.

In case what?

Again he had no answer to give to himself.

(Even in his apathetic state, he had listened, on Monday—waiting to hear what the rumor mill would make of the mysterious masked figure that had stopped a date rape and put a high schooler in the hospital. But there had been almost nothing—just a collective shit happens shrug, a moving-on to more tangible gossip, petty crime and drunken shenanigans and costume-enabled romantic drama. A thousand things had happened at East Binder Secondary School that Friday night, and Conor’s contribution had mattered to almost no one.)

Silence. It was too soon for feelings—for named feelings, for things-in-boxes. For the moment, he was just Conor. Words would come later, or maybe not at all.

I’m not gay, I’m ASHLEIGH.

The shard of memory drifted up, was acknowledged, faded away.

A door opened, and then closed, halfway down the hallway. Another seventh grader walked by, a face that Conor didn’t know. They shot him a wary look—stumbled—fumbled the stack of books they were carrying and in the process dropped the vape pen they’d apparently been hiding inside of it. It bounced off of their shoe and clattered across the floor, the sound almost comically loud, coming to rest against a locker thirty feet away.

They froze, eyes wide and waiting.

Conor shrugged.

They unfroze.

Yeah, so…what ARE we doing?

Conor watched idly as the kid retrieved the pen, reshuffled their stack of books, pushed through the double doors and vanished out into the quad.

Sitting, he thought.

…and?

By 12:15, he was something like half-thawed, enough to second-guess himself as he hovered just inside the doorway to Ms. Palmano’s classroom. Was he supposed to sit down at his desk? Go up to the front of the room and talk to her? Something else?

“Up to you,” his teacher called out, from the front of the room where she was erasing the words from the previous period.

Conor caught his jaw before it could fall fully open. It wasn’t that wild of a guess, as guesses went…

But still.

Feeling naked, he set his stuff down on his usual desk, slid into the chair, then stood up again. He took two steps toward the front—hesitated—stepped back toward the desk.

Get a grip.

Up at the board, his teacher finished erasing and turned to face him. She took one look—

“Never mind, not up to you. Here.”

She snapped her fingers, pointed toward one of the desks in the front row. He walked slowly toward it, his shoulders strangely heavy, lowering himself into the chair as she leaned back against the board, her arms crossed.

(Though still not as close as on the previous Saturday.)

The ghost of a smile played around his teacher’s lips, the barest crinkle forming at the corners of her eyes. “To be clear, I’m not recommending this. We can also get you into more classes with him, if that’s somehow better. Or we can leave things as they are. I mostly mention it because it occurred to me that it might not have occurred to you that you can actually ask for help, here. Including without explaining why. You can just…ask for things.”

Conor struggled to find words. His teacher waited, her expression gentle, radiating patience.

How did you—

No. Obvious. She paid attention, and/or Conor’s mother had given her an earful on Saturday morning.

WHY are you—

Also obvious. Although—

The more complicated answer is, having visited your house and talked to your mom, it seems pretty unlikely that the cuts and bruises are child abuse, and they’re not straightforwardly self-harm, so…nothing to see, here? No need to call in the National Guard.”

“Oh, no, you’re not wrong,” she said cheerfully. “This isn’t a normal sort of offer. It’s blatant favoritism on my part.”

“Really?” said Ms. Palmano. “I thought you knew. I don’t just randomly let parents bully me into home visits, you know.”

Four different emotions all tried to claw their way out of him at once.

I mean, there’s nothing she can do, right?
