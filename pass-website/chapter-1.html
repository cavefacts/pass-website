There were police cars waiting in the front circle—four of them.

Conor swore as he ducked back behind the corner of the cafeteria, then swore again as his brain caught up. Oh, nothing suspicious here, officer, just playing hide and seek—

Slowly, carefully, he eased back around the corner, slipping quietly behind one of the squat holly bushes planted along the front of the building. No one had noticed his sudden movement—at least, no one was looking or walking his way—and after a moment he relaxed, settling his back against the wall.

Like the library opposite, the cafeteria extended out well beyond the rest of the school, the two buildings like arms sheltering the courtyard and front circle between them. Conor had come around the back side, where the squat structure butted up against the school’s outer wall, avoiding the crowded interior. His roundabout path had brought him out to the edge of the parking lot, so that he now stood looking in, peering past the parked cars toward the main entrance.

His original plan had been simple. This far out, there was nobody watching; he could simply join in with the flow of students getting off of the parked buses.

But in the circle, where there should have been a double row of parents dropping off the car riders, a single line of traffic crawled past four patrol cars parked in the curbside lane. They were widely spaced, forming a long, broken wall, three pairs of uniformed officers standing like guards in the gaps. Conor watched as they funneled the arriving students between them, speaking briefly with each student, sometimes pulling one aside for a few moments before letting him—it was always a boy—move forward.

Conor sucked in his breath, adrenaline once more seeping into his veins. He sank deeper behind the bush, pulling his pale arms into his dark sleeves to make himself less visible.

Think.

The voice was stern, and slightly impatient.

Conor thought. No one knew about the bag, or the gap, and he himself hadn’t done anything else even remotely deserving of police attention. Trespassing on school grounds the day before maybe, but four patrol cars?

No. If they were after anyone, it was Ashleigh.

Which was not particularly reassuring, actually—the only reason for them to be after Ashleigh was Guyler, and Conor tried not to imagine what might have changed overnight to cause the police to send four cars to East Binder Secondary School. Once again, the dread seized him, the wild sense of helplessness—

Where are you, Ash?

For the hundredth time that morning, the thousandth time since the night before, Conor wished his friend were there beside him, that they weren’t each facing this alone.

So go find him, retard.

If they were looking for him here, it meant he wasn’t at home.

(Which also meant he wasn’t grounded, as Conor had assumed—that it wasn’t his mom who was keeping him from answering his phone.)

Conor straightened, peering through the leaves at the cordon, where the cops were still filtering kids through, one by one.

He’ll see that a mile away.

He would see it, and then he would do…what? Would go where?

Slipping back out from behind the bush, Conor turned the corner and began retracing his steps behind the cafeteria. Now that he’d clawed his way back from panic, he was fairly sure they would have let him through, but there were other ways to get to the courtyard, and there was no point in taking the risk. Rule number one was protect yourself—confidence wasn’t the same as certainty, and he couldn’t know until too late which of his assumptions might be wrong.

Better safe than sorry.

There were hundreds of students waiting in the quad behind the office, sprawled across the open space in knots and clumps, laughing and shouting as they revived half-remembered rituals with half-forgotten friends. Those nearest to Conor were all frighteningly large—juniors and seniors, some of them close to twice his height and three times his weight. He pushed in among them, dodging high-fives and hacky sacks, ignoring cries of “Go, Diego, go!” and “Watch it, fun size,” as he threaded his way toward the library on the far side.

(The voices followed him, but nothing else. There was an art to moving through a crowd like this one, and Conor had the knack—head up, eyes down, bookbag tight on both shoulders, and above all keep moving, even if it meant turning sideways or doubling back.)

It would have been easier, he knew, to walk all the way around the edges, hugging the buildings, or even ducking deeper into campus, the depopulated areas on the far side of the quad. But the press of bodies made for good cover, especially if any of the grownups had happened to notice a small shape slipping out from behind the cafeteria.

It was a strange feeling, the need to avoid notice. Conor was familiar with furtiveness, had run from neighbors and teachers and construction site managers, hidden from security guards while playing soldier at night near the old folks’ home.

But always before, the wariness had been part of the game, and had vanished as soon as he stopped playing. This was different—a sort of lingering paranoia, as though he still carried the bag in his belt and Guyler’s blood on his shirt. He felt vulnerable, and vaguely unclean, and was glad when he passed between the library and the office and out into the courtyard, where he was officially allowed to be.

The crowds on the middle school side were thinner, and other students began to call and wave, casual acquaintances from last year’s classes. He nodded back, not slowing as he made a beeline toward his and Ashleigh’s usual spot. There were maybe five minutes left before first bell—if Ashleigh was there, they wouldn’t have much time to talk.

Approaching the library, Conor angled for the entrance, a long, wide passageway sandwiched between two extended wings of the building and shaded by another metal breezeway roof. Not many students chose to wait there—it was comfortable enough, but the library doors were locked, which meant you had to walk all the way across the courtyard when the bell rang, trailing along behind everybody else. Their usual spot was in a corner all the way at the very end, as isolated as it was possible to be—

Conor blinked.

The corner was occupied, but not by Ashleigh, nor by any of their other friends. By three small boys, boys Conor didn’t know, had never seen before, sitting on their bookbags—

Right.

Sixth graders.

What with the climb, and the bag, and the police cordon, Conor had managed to completely forget that two hundred new kids would be entering East Binder for the very first time that day. Looking around, he confirmed it—the other kids he’d brushed past on his way in were almost all undersized, and one of them he remembered from elementary school. An echo of a voice floated up in his memory, the drone of a teacher directing traffic the year before: “Sixth graders by the library, eighth graders by the flagpole, seventh graders in between. Sixth graders by the library…”

Conor sighed. That meant that his spot was no longer his spot, and Ashleigh could be anywhere, if he was anywhere at all. Frustrated, he turned to head back out into the courtyard—

He stopped again.

It was a sign of their naïveté that none of the kids in the passageway had reacted to Conor’s presence. East Binder would cure them of that soon—one of the first things new students picked up was an almost magical ability to detect older kids who didn’t belong.

(It was a necessary talent in a school where half the bullies had more than two years on you.)

But with less than an hour under their belts, and still accustomed to having been at the top of the food chain as fifth graders, none of the newly minted middle schoolers were on the lookout for trouble. They had paid no attention to Conor, just as they were now paying no attention to the three older boys swaggering in from the courtyard.

The middle one was familiar, a seventh grader—

No, an eighth grader, now.

—named Craig Eichardt. He rode Conor’s bus, alternating between tormenting the smaller kids near the front of the bus and sucking up to the thugs at the back. He’d had a few run-ins with Conor and Ashleigh the year before. Conor didn’t know the names of the other two boys, but he knew the type, could read the casual cruelty on their faces.

Not your problem.

He began walking slowly back toward the exit, head down, watching from the corner of his eye as he drifted to the opposite side of the passageway. The three bullies had zeroed in on a group of smaller boys and were looming, now, laughing, pressing them back against the brick wall of the library. One of the thugs reached out and snatched the hat off of a kid’s head—in response, another dropped his bookbag and pushed to the front, hands clenched into fists.

Not. Your. Problem.

Conor drifted even further to the side, hoping—and hating himself for it—that he would be able to slip past unnoticed.

Ashleigh. Your job is to find Ashleigh.

Besides, there really wasn’t anything to be done—giving the bullies something to push against would just make them more eager, like sharks in a feeding frenzy. They weren’t looking for trouble—just the opposite. They’d call some names, maybe push the sixth graders around a bit, then slouch off before any grownups could show up. Establishing the pecking order…

Conor was almost past them, only a few steps away from the courtyard proper, when three things happened in quick succession:

Eichardt reached out and shoved the kid with the fists, who staggered back into the wall behind him.

The kid rebounded, shouting in fury, and as Conor heard his voice again, looked directly at him for the first time, his jaw dropped in recognition.

And as Eichardt turned to laugh with his cronies, Timothy Falhanner dealt him a savage kick that landed squarely in the center of his left shin.

Timothy—Ashleigh’s little brother.

Shit.

Eichardt went down, hard, and before the other two eighth graders could react, Timothy had snatched back his friend’s hat and darted away, the rest of his group scattering. One of the bullies followed, chasing three of the younger kids out into the courtyard; the other leaned over to help a fuming, cursing Eichardt back to his feet.

“The fuck you looking at, faggot?”

Eichardt was standing, now, snarling, his face red as he tried gingerly to put weight on his leg. Conor took a step back, ducking his head meekly, raising his hands in silent answer. Satisfied, the eighth grader snorted and turned away, taking short, shuffling steps as he and his friend headed back out into the courtyard.

And just like that, it was over. It had all happened so quickly that Conor had not had time to even form a coherent thought. The other kids around him looked equally stunned, more than one mouth hanging open.

Get used to it, kiddos.

There was a bookbag lying on the ground where the confrontation had taken place—familiar, the initials AMF written in faded sharpie on the front pouch.

Ashleigh’s old bookbag.

Conor looked around, feeling helpless. None of Timothy’s friends remained anywhere in sight, and he doubted he’d be able to find the younger boy in the courtyard with only a couple of minutes left before first bell—

“Conor?”

Turning, Conor saw a man entering the passageway from the courtyard. It was his math teacher from the previous year, one he’d gotten along well with.

“Hi, Mr. Marowitz.”

“What are you doing here? Seventh graders should be out in front of the office.”

“Yeah, I…” He hesitated. “I was, uh, trying to give Timothy his bag. Ashleigh’s little brother. He forgot it…”

Mr. Marowitz looked down at the bag, then back out into the courtyard, a shadow of suspicion coming over his face.

“I didn’t take it,” Conor hastened to explain. “That was—there were some kids giving him a hard time and he dropped it.”

“I see,” the teacher said, nodding slowly.

For a moment, Conor considered filling him in in greater detail, but just then the bell rang.

“Give it to me,” Mr. Marowitz said, holding out a hand. “I’ll get it to him.”

Conor handed it over.

“They want you in the office, by the way,” the grownup said.

Conor’s stomach lurched. “They do?” he asked.

“Yes, they were calling for you a couple of minutes ago. Shall we?”

Together, they turned and walked back out into the courtyard, following the rest of the crowd.

So. They were looking for you after all. How about that.

A part of Conor tried to panic, but by this point the shocks had drained him of adrenaline, leaving behind only a tired, defeated sort of feeling.

“Ready for seventh grade?” Mr. Marowitz asked.

Ha.

“Guess so.”

“Sad that summer’s over?”

Not after yesterday. “I kind of like fall.”

The grownup nodded. They joined the scrum at the double doors, pushing through and into the front hallway, where a vast blue-and-gold banner proclaimed WELCOME BACK, BULLDOGS! When they reached the office, Mr. Marowitz hesitated briefly.

“Well, good luck,” he said, and then continued onward.

Conor turned right and went inside.

He looked around for Ashleigh, but the other boy wasn’t there. There were six or seven other students waiting in the chairs around the edge of the room, a handful of teachers and parents vying for the lone secretary’s attention. Several minutes passed before she finally got around to him, at which point she ushered him down a short hallway and into a small, deserted lounge. Depositing him on a couch, she knocked sharply on a door marked Asst. Principal, told him to wait until called for, and disappeared just as the tardy bell rang, leaving him alone.

Conor put his head in his hands.

Ready for seventh grade?

Not really, no.

The lounge was warm, stuffy, and quiet. A clock ticked softly on the wall across from him, and he closed his eyes, feeling the heartbeats in his forehead.

Too much.

The weed, the jump, the fight, the ambulance, Ashleigh’s disappearance—the bag, the cops, and now Timothy and Eichardt, of all things—it seemed to Conor that he had spent days off balance, always reacting, never quite able to catch his breath. Finding Ashleigh before the grownups did had been his final gambit, his last desperate attempt at regaining control, but he’d missed it, he had no idea where the other boy was, and now it was out of his hands.

The silence stretched.

Suddenly, there was a sharp click, and an unfamiliar voice came over the intercom.

“Good morning, students and staff. Welcome back to another year at East Binder Secondary School. My name is Everett Sykes, and I will be joining you this year as your new assistant principal.”

Conor’s eyes flickered back toward the closed door.

“I have just a few brief announcements for you, before we all get down to business…”

The words were dry, clipped, and precise, wasting no time or energy. Conor hadn’t known they were getting a new AP, and wasn’t sure whether that was good or bad, all things considered—he’d met the previous one, Mr. Benson, on the first day of last year, when he and Ashleigh had been caught impersonating absent students to steal their locker combinations…

Hey, it’s not like we were going to do anything bad with them.

They hadn’t even had a plan, really. They’d just…seen the opportunity, and taken it.

They’d been sent to see Mr. Benson a handful of times after that—never for anything serious, just minor pranks and hallway scuffles.

…more than half of which had involved Guyler Stevens, now that Conor thought about it. There was the time Guyler stole Ashleigh’s shoes during gym class, and filled the toes with yogurt. And the time he’d “accidentally” spilled his drink on Ashleigh’s phone. And the time he’d straight-up tripped Ashleigh in the hall during class change, almost breaking his nose…

And the time Ashleigh shredded Guyler’s research paper. And the thing with the frozen piss.

“…and for those new students who have still not purchased the school uniform, this is a reminder that you have until Friday to do so. After that, uniforms are strictly required for all classes and at all official school functions. Thank you for your attention, and go Bulldogs.”

The voice clicked off. Scarcely five seconds later, the door to the assistant principal’s office swung open, revealing a tall, grey-haired man wearing a dress shirt with a blue-and-gold tie.

“Hello, Mr. Moreton,” the man said, in the same taut, formal voice that had come out of the intercom. “My name is Mr. Sykes. Please come in.”

Conor stood, following the man into the small office. It looked different than it had the last time he had been there, Mr. Benson’s picture frames and knick-knacks and piles of paper all gone, with only a single photo resting on the corner of the clean, empty desk, a landscape with a rocky mountain over a mirrorlike lake.

“I’m sorry that our first meeting has to be across a desk,” Mr. Sykes said as they both sat down, “but I wanted to check in with you as soon as possible about the events of yesterday.” The AP’s chair was identical to Conor’s, the same molded plastic that could be found at every desk and table in the school. “You’ll be happy to know that Guyler Stevens woke up soon after arriving at the hospital last night, and neither his concussion nor the shock are going to cause him any lasting harm. The doctor particularly mentioned that the compress on his forehead probably saved him from needing a transfusion, and that you should be commended for acting so quickly.”

A tension that Conor had not even been aware of left his shoulders at this news, and he felt himself relax into his seat a little. “Will he be—uh—what about his face?”

“His nose was broken. It’s not clear how well that will heal. But the scar on his forehead should be quite thin once the stitches come out. At the moment, I’m told he’s having more pain from the fractured rib, which won’t be fully healed until November.”

Throughout this recitation, the grownup had been studying Conor’s face intently, and now he leaned forward, clasping his hands together, his eyes sharp. “Were you in the tree with Mr. Stevens when he fell?”

So random, so unexpected was this question that Conor, who had come into the office fully prepared to lie on Ashleigh’s behalf, was nevertheless caught completely off guard. “I—wait, what?”

The assistant principal nodded, his lips tightening into a thin, bloodless line. “I thought so. Let’s try a different one: did you by any chance move Mr. Stevens, before the EMTs arrived?”

Move him?

“Drag him, roll him, help him walk. No? Could he have moved himself? You told Officer Grimsley that you heard a shout, came over the outer wall, and found him by the sidewalk—is there any chance that he crawled there from somewhere else?”

“I…”

Conor was growing more confused by the second, and somewhere in the back of his mind, a small voice warned him to be careful.

Rule two.

“This is very important, Mr. Moreton. Are you quite sure that the place where you found Mr. Stevens is the same place where he first collapsed?”

The memory of Guyler falling, of Ashleigh vanishing around the corner—

“Yes,” Conor said. “But what—”

“How can you be sure?”

“I….” He hesitated. “The blood. There was blood on the sidewalk. Nothing on the grass. Why? What does this have to do with anything?”

Mr. Sykes ignored the question. “It was Mr. Stevens who started the fight, wasn’t it?”

Conor froze. That was exactly the story he had planned to run with, the best he had been able to come up with, without conferring with Ashleigh first. It was plausible, since half of the fights between them had been started by Guyler anyway, and it was the only way to draw sympathy for a kid who had put his classmate in the hospital—that it had been in self-defense.

(It might even be true.)

But the way Mr. Sykes had sprung the question—the intensity with which he was now staring at Conor—

“I don’t know,” he said, instinctively changing tactics.

“You don’t know who started the fight?”

“I don’t know anything about a fight.”

Mr. Sykes leaned back in his chair, still studying Conor’s face. “Last night, you told Officer Grimsley that you didn’t know what had happened to Mr. Stevens, that you found him that way, and that, I quote, ‘Ashleigh is gone.’ Apparently you repeated that last part quite a few times.”

Conor kept his face carefully neutral.

“Ashleigh was supposed to meet me in the park across the street.”

No, wait—too specific.

“I mean, he wasn’t supposed to. But I was looking for him there. We meet there all the time.”

“But he wasn’t there yesterday?”

“I don’t know. He might’ve been. I left when I heard the shout.”

“So you didn’t see Ashleigh at all?”

What is this?

Mr. Sykes was fishing, that much was clear. But why was he asking Conor, when Guyler was already awake?

“I don’t remember,” Conor said.

The grownup’s eyes widened. “You don’t remember?”

Conor said nothing. They sat there for a long moment, just looking at each other.

Slowly, Mr. Sykes’s expression softened. “He’s your friend, isn’t he, Mr. Moreton? Ashleigh Falhanner, I mean.”

Conor nodded.

“And you want to help him out of this, don’t you?”

Help me out of what, exactly?

“And you’re trying very hard not to tell me anything that will get him into trouble, aren’t you?”

Conor still didn’t answer, and Mr. Sykes sighed. “Listen, Mr. Moreton, I’m not here to interrogate you, or to trick you into betraying your friend. As it happens, you’ve already cleared up the major issue for me, and there’s no need to drag this out if you plan on being stubborn. If you want, I can write you a pass to homeroom right now.”

He paused briefly, as if to give Conor a chance to take him up on the invitation, then continued. “But there are still a few things that aren’t making sense to me yet, and I’d appreciate it if you were a little more…open…than you seem to be, right now.”

“Why don’t you ask Guyler?”

“I’m interested in what you have to say.”

Conor’s heart began to race.

He really doesn’t know.

Why? It made no sense at all—Guyler and Ashleigh had been enemies for a full year, and neither had ever hesitated to spill the beans on the other. Why would Guyler—

Holy shit.

Maybe the other boy couldn’t remember.

Mr. Sykes cleared his throat, and Conor jumped in his seat.

Say nothing.

“I’m sorry, Mr. Sykes. Like I said, I don’t know what happened. I heard a shout, came over the wall, and called 911. That’s—that’s all I know.”

The assistant principal’s face darkened. There was another long pause, and when he finally spoke, there was an edge of sharpness in his dry, serious voice.

“Let me tell you a story, Mr. Moreton,” he said, leaning forward and fixing Conor with a cold stare. “I came here from Central High. Do you know where that is?”

Conor nodded.

“We have our fair share of problems at Central. Enough that Officer Grimsley and I have become quite close, over the years. I’m sure you’ve heard stories. I wouldn’t be shocked to see five fights in a single week, and those would be just the ones we managed to find out about. Most of them never make it to the office.”

He smiled, but there was no mirth in it. “So there I am, last night, getting ready for my first day at this new school, this good school, where there are only a couple of fights a month and they only bring the drug dogs around twice a quarter, and I get a call from Mike, and he says Everett, I know this isn’t really your problem, it still being summer and all, but I thought you’d want to know that we just responded to a 911 call from a kid about another kid who got his face pasted on a sidewalk at East Binder.

“And so I ask, did the caller see anything? And Mike tells me no, the kid is in shock and isn’t making much sense, but he’ll stop by the hospital once things have calmed down, and interview the victim. And I say thanks, Mike, I appreciate it.”

Conor felt himself beginning to sweat. Nothing good could come from whatever it was Mr. Sykes was trying to do—intimidate him? Trick him?

“After an hour, Mike calls me back. You’ll never believe it, he says. Kid claims he fell out of a tree. And I ask Mike what’s wrong with that story, and he says that for one thing, this kid, Guyler Stevens, he doesn’t have any mud or bark in any of his cuts. None. Plenty on his hands and clothes, but none in his cuts. For another thing, Mr. Stevens was found on a covered sidewalk, and the closest tree was thirty feet away.”

Conor could see it clearly in his mind’s eye—the wet splatter on the speckled concrete, the distant trunk of the old magnolia. He hadn’t registered the bark on Guyler’s hands and clothes, but suddenly the point of Mr. Sykes’ opening questions became clear.

Wait, though—does that mean Guyler was in the tree at some point? Why would he—

“Now, Mike and I have both been in this game for a long time,” Mr. Sykes continued. “So before I can even ask, he tells me that he already checked the caller for busted up knuckles and came up empty. At this point, all he has to go on is another kid that the caller mentioned, a girl named Ashleigh. I offer to look into it, and soon enough I find that it isn’t a girl, it’s a boy, and furthermore that this boy has a long history of getting into fights with our very own Mr. Stevens. I give Mike the phone number and address, and I hang up.

“So now things are fairly clear. Mr. Falhanner is our suspect, Mr. Stevens is our victim, and you, Mr. Moreton, are our witness, but nobody’s going to talk, because snitches get stitches, yes? I figure we’ll do things the hard way, since our defendant has no alibi and probably has split knuckles and that’s usually enough for the judge. But then Mike calls a third time, with two bits of bad news.”

Conor kept his eyes steady through sheer force of will, kept them pointed in the grownup’s direction despite an almost overwhelming desire to run, to hide.

“First, he’s had no luck tracking down our suspect. He went by the house, but no one would answer the door, and apparently the house phone doesn’t have voicemail. Second, he’s been back to the hospital to talk to Mr. Stevens again, but the kid is really sticking to his story, so much so that Mike tells me he’ll have to drop the case entirely—Mr. Stevens insists that he’ll testify in court that the only thing that took a swing at him was the root of a magnolia tree.”

Conor struggled to process this.

Guyler…lied?

Why?

“Mike is concerned, naturally. Usually only two reasons for this kind of thing. Either the victim is scared out of his mind—and Mike says this kid doesn’t seem the type—or he has his own ideas about how to pay back his attacker, and they don’t involve juvie. Mr. Stevens seems perhaps a little young for that sort of thing, but hey, some kids are precocious. Mike’s hands are tied, so I agree to follow up here at school. I assume this means Mr. Falhanner will get away with it, but that we’ll talk to the parents and keep a close eye out for a month or two. I draft a letter to both families, and I go to sleep. Imagine my surprise, then, when Wendy knocks on my door at seven forty-five this morning to tell me there’s a boy outside my office with ice on his knuckles and dirt all over his clothes.”

Wait, what?

“And the icing on the cake: this boy isn’t saying a word. Not one word. Won’t even give his name, although a quick look at last year’s yearbook clears that up.”

Conor’s head was spinning. Ashleigh had turned himself in?

Ashleigh?

He’d once dug a hole five feet deep, to hide the broken pieces of his mother’s favorite flowerpot. He took his punishments cheerfully enough once they had him pinned down, but he always made them work for it. In Conor’s memory, Ashleigh had never, ever confessed.

But he hasn’t confessed. He hasn’t said anything.

So…

So…

Mr. Sykes was still watching, and gave a tight, satisfied nod. “You understand my frustration, then. I have a victim refusing to admit he has been attacked. A bully who surrenders, but refuses to confess. And you, a witness who clearly wants to do the right thing, but who says he saw nothing and is giving me no useful information.”

“Ashleigh isn’t a bully.”

(Conor had no idea what was happening, but honor demanded he say at least that much.)

Suddenly, Mr. Sykes dropped his fist against the desk, causing the picture frame to bounce. “I am not interested in your opinions, Mr. Moreton! I am in need of facts. I want you to tell me what happened here, so that I can deal with it. I have sixteen hundred students awaiting my attention, and I have neither the time nor the patience for games."

And then Conor saw that he was indeed trapped, though the trap wasn’t of Mr. Sykes’s making. For even though he’d been present for all but ten minutes, those were the ten minutes that had truly mattered, and he really didn’t have the answers the new AP wanted. There was a bitter irony to Conor’s next words, because he knew they would be taken as a lie—

(And he would have gladly braved the grownup’s anger to protect Ashleigh if they’d been a lie.)

—but they were nothing but the simple truth.

“I’m sorry, Mr. Sykes,” he said, his voice trembling. “I didn’t see what happened, and I really don’t know.”

The silence that followed was the longest and most uncomfortable of the entire conversation, but when the AP spoke again, his voice was flat and colorless once more. “So be it,” he sighed. “Two weeks’ suspension for your friend.”

“What? But—”

“Discretion, Mr. Moreton. As assistant principal, I may suspend any student for up to ten days for any reason, without appeal. A fact which you would do well to keep in mind, since you seem to have decided that our relationship will not be marked by an abundance of trust.” He reached over to a phone on the corner of his desk and pressed a button. “Wendy, please notify Mr. Falhanner’s parents that he will be taking a two-week suspension, beginning tomorrow. I’ll still want to talk to him, but I have enough information to make a decision. If the parents complain, schedule a conference.” The voice on the other end acknowledged, and Mr. Sykes released the button, turning back to meet Conor’s eyes one last time.

“I don’t like things that are messy, Mr. Moreton. If your friend is caught, he should be punished, and if he’s going to get away with it, then I should not have to deal with it anyway. English.

English.

Hey, remember that time Conor went nuts in Science and started eating his textbook?

Any of it.

(Conor could feel his thoughts racing, gathering speed. But he didn’t have the energy to stop them, nor any sense that trying would make anything better.)

Ashleigh.

Conor’s thoughts stumbled.

Ms. Palmano.

Another nervous chuckle.

This from another boy, one whose name Conor didn’t know.

“Writing down your grades.”

“What? What do you mean?” He sounded slightly panicked, and from the looks on the faces of the other students around him, he wasn’t alone. “For what?”

“You tell me. You’re the one who gave it to yourself.”

“No, I mean—what’s it for? Is that, like, a quiz grade, or—”

“Oh, I don’t know. I was just going to put it onto your report card.”

This triggered a wave of protest, which their teacher completely ignored. Turning, she hoisted an expression of exaggerated innocence onto her face and asked, “Is there a problem?”

A dozen voices spoke all at once, and she raised her arms in a calming gesture. Slowly, the voices quieted and were replaced by a dozen raised hands. She pointed to one.

“But we haven’t done anything yet!”

“So? You’re going to—isn’t this easier? Now we both know what to expect.”

Another outburst, this time involving at least twenty voices. Again, Ms. Palmano waited, and again the students slowly settled and raised their hands.

“I hope you’re not suggesting that it would be a good idea to cheat in my class, Miss—”

“Gibson. Susanne Gibson.”

Susanne fell silent, her face burning. Someone else spoke in a stage whisper. “Wish I’d put myself under A instead of C.”

“My name’s Ben.”

Not everyone had joined in—there were a dozen or so students who hadn’t spoken at all, Conor among them—but those whose hands were raised looked mutinous. Holt was shaking with suppressed laughter.

“The grade is for your work in this class, which I would have thought was perfectly obvious from the fact that I was writing it down in my gradebook,” Ms. Palmano said. She nodded toward the girl sitting directly in front of Conor.

“What work, though? And my name’s Jennifer.”

“You tell me, Jennifer. You’ve promised me”—here she paused, scanning the board—“an A’s worth of English work. What will it be?”

“Aren’t you supposed to tell us? You’re the teacher!”

“And you’re the student. Would you rather learn how to follow directions, or how to write them?”

There was a lull as the protesters digested this. Holt was still laughing silently; Conor reached over and punched him in the arm. “What’s so funny?” he hissed.

“This whole thing,” Holt whispered back. “Está jodiendolos—they’re all taking it so serious, and she’s just trolling them. I bet she didn’t even write those grades down. Bet she was just taking attendance. Oh, man, I hope she doesn’t get fired.” He started laughing again.

“Then you’ll fail.”

“There aren’t any failing grades up there,” Ben pointed out. “You only went down to D.”

“Of course you can fail, I just sa—weren’t you listening? I’m saying that your grade has nothing to do with it.” She threw her hands up in mock frustration. “Look, this is a very simple concept. I don’t know why you’re having so much trouble with it.”

“You’re not explaining it!”

“Exactly!”

The discussion dissolved into chaos. Conor watched with clinical interest as half of his classmates came unhinged, feeling oddly detached. And there in the center of it all, making no effort to temper the riot, stood Ms. Palmano, that same small smile returned to her lips as she let them wear themselves out.

Holt’s right, Conor thought. She was doing this on purpose—was winding them up.

Why?

It wasn’t a loud question. Not like the ones Jennifer and Jeremy and Ben and Susanne were currently shouting (to no avail). But it penetrated Conor’s fugue, somehow—caused his head to tilt a fraction of an inch.

What was this woman after, that this was her way of getting it?

He wondered what Ashleigh would do, if he were here.

Probably go up and erase the board.

Or declare the class dismissed, and walk out.

Or…

Conor hesitated.

A beautiful idea had just occurred to him—but it couldn’t be that simple, could it?

Oh, Ash would just love this.

Might as well get it over with.

If Guyler didn’t mention the roof—and it didn’t sound like he ever would—then there was no real need to take it back up to its old hiding spot. He wondered how much it was worth, and whether it would keep as well in the cool, dark damp as it had in the dry heat of the rooftop.

What, we selling drugs now?

No. Of course not. He was just wondering, that was all—

The bell rang. There was a vast roar as five hundred chairs slid backwards all at once, the teachers shouting to corral the sudden chaos. Together, the five boys stood, dumping their trash and their trays and joining the flow of students slowly filtering through the double doors.

As they passed out into the hallway, Holt and Eddie still speculating about the Central students, Conor realized that he had managed to go almost a full five minutes without thinking of any of the horrors of the previous day. And while Eddie had been annoying as always, once they’d quit talking about Ashleigh, the rest of it had been harmless, casual—even interesting, at times.

He looked at the faces of the other four boys as they walked, found—to his surprise—that he felt no desire to escape, to pull ahead or let the crowd swallow him. It didn’t rankle, the way it so often did, to be within arm’s length, to exist inside the bubble of their conversation. For the moment, at least, it felt fine to simply share the space. Normal, familiar, almost comforting.

It was something.

•••

Three quick spins of the dial, one solid shove to shut the overstuffed locker, and Conor was free. He weaved his way through the crowd, moving half a step slower than a jog as he headed for the exit. The gusting wind was cool against his face as he passed through the doors and out into the courtyard, but he left his jacket open, letting it ripple, capelike, behind him.

One down. One hundred and seventy-nine to go.

The afternoon had stretched on forever. His sixth class, Activity, had begun with the presidential fitness test, long stretches of waiting punctuated by bursts of breathless exertion. Conor was one of the few who actually enjoyed it—after a whole summer spent clambering around on rooftops, he was able to do twelve pull-ups in a row, now. But the test had been followed by flag football, and he had nursed an injured wrist all the way through Art.

Angling toward the flagpole, he stepped out of the flow of students and paused, turning on his phone. He was disappointed (though not surprised) to see that there were no new messages, and he argued with himself for a full minute before pocketing the phone without sending any of his own. Better at this point to talk in person—since he didn’t have to loop back to East Binder to move the bag, he could pick up his bike at home and ride over to Ashleigh’s before the sky grew dark.

He began walking again, heading for the long line of parked buses along the south side of the parking lot. They were a mismatched bunch, ancient steel-and-diesel relics alternating with sleek, flat-faced modern designs, most of them a classic school-bus yellow but some pale blue or dingy beige or peeling white. Already there were hundreds of students, strung out in clumps, waiting for the drivers to arrive and let them on board.

Conor made his way down the line, searching for number 134. Spotting it, he quickened his pace to a trot—

—until suddenly the shape of the crowd next to it registered in his brain, and he broke into a flat-out run.

God damn it—

There were precious few reasons for everyone to be standing in a circle like that, and none of them good. He saw phones out, filming—heard jeering laughter—felt a sudden premonition so strong that he heard himself shouting “Let him go!” before he could even see past the densely packed shoulders.

“What do you want, faggot?” Eichardt snarled, as Conor pushed his way into the open space at the center. He had only one of his lackeys with him this time, but that was enough—the hulking boy had no trouble holding both of Timothy’s arms behind his back as Eichardt pawed through his bookbag. Conor cursed himself for not making the connection earlier—that all three of them rode the same bus, that of course Eichardt would remember the morning’s humiliation, and seek revenge—

Did you have to pull this shit on literally day one, my fucking god—

If Conor had been there five minutes earlier, he might have been able to head the older boy off, rally the crowd against him. But it was too late, now. The thing was already in motion, had acquired a horrible momentum. There was a circle, and the circle was hungry; one way or another, there was going to be a show.

“Who’re you calling a faggot?” Timothy’s voice rang out, perfectly on cue. “At least he’s not grabbing all over little boys like your friend here.”

Conor winced. Eichardt’s eyes narrowed.

Maybe we should just get it over with. Push some buttons, take a couple of black eyes—

A memory of flashing lights, and with it a splash of fear, like cold water down the back of Conor’s shirt.

Sometimes it wasn’t just a black eye.

The fear made him hesitate, and the hesitation filled him with self-loathing, and the self-loathing curdled into something black and reckless, all of it happening in the space of a heartbeat, so that by the time he opened his mouth, he no longer cared about playing any particular part.

“Let the kid go,” Conor repeated, his own voice taut and burning.

“Or what, bitch?” The bully dropped Timothy’s bag, spilling pencils and books onto the sidewalk. He turned to face Conor head on, stepping forward, looming—

“Or we find out who’s the faggot when we put you on your knees, asshole.”

Eichardt’s head jerked toward the new voice as Conor’s breath caught in his throat. Off to the side, the crowd was parting, the circle opening up as another boy edged his way in. His face had been washed, and he was wearing an old, threadbare blue-and-gold East Binder t-shirt over his ruined jeans, and the look in his eyes was flat and cold and dangerous.

Ashleigh.

He stepped forward, stepped close, and now it was Eichardt’s turn to hesitate. Conor could see the calculation unfolding in his mind—the older boys still had the upper hand in terms of raw strength, but it was two against three, now, and the privacy of the circle wouldn’t last forever. He hadn’t actually come there to fight—had probably just been hoping to put Timothy in his place—

Before the eighth grader could recover, Ashleigh bent down, reaching for a small scrap of bark on the ground. Straightening, he balanced the chip of wood on his left shoulder, locking eyes with Eichardt.

The circle held its breath. That was a formal challenge, one that even the sixth graders were familiar with—either Eichardt would knock the chip off Ashleigh’s shoulder, giving the younger boy an opening to throw the first punch, or he would decline, and everyone would know that Eichardt had backed down from a fight.

With a seventh grader.

Whose little brother had kicked him in the shins and gotten away unscathed.

Conor had only a split second to appreciate the trap before the inevitable happened, Eichardt’s arm jerking forward as if by reflex. The chip went flying, and before it could hit the ground, Ashleigh’s fist was already swinging. It struck a glancing blow to the side of the bully’s jaw—enough to make him stagger, but not enough to knock him over. He fell back half a step, arms windmilling—

And Ashleigh screamed.

It was a raw, animal sound, shockingly loud, and it seemed like the whole circle flinched, recoiled, drawing back on itself. Eichardt regained his footing, but froze, half-crouched and uncertain, a trapped and hunted look appearing on his face as his eyes darted from side to side. He watched along with everyone else as Ashleigh crumpled to his knees, still screaming, clutching his right hand with his left—

Suddenly, Conor understood. Ashleigh had hit the bully with his swollen, ruined knuckles.

It was a situation for which no one had anything even remotely resembling a script. Long seconds passed as Ashleigh’s scream tapered to a moan, which then broke apart into sobs and finally died. Still no one moved—not Eichardt, not Conor, not even Timothy.

The teachers heard that, Conor thought desperately. They had to—they’re on their way, they’re coming—

Ashleigh rose slowly to his feet, jaw trembling, tears leaking from furious eyes. He reached up to wipe his face, and when he pulled his arm away, there was another collective gasp—

There was a second woodchip. On Ashleigh’s right shoulder this time, indicating that he would punch with his left.

“Your move,” Ashleigh said, and though his voice cracked halfway through, no one laughed.

There was a brief, wild moment in which it seemed that Eichardt would do it anyway, was going to sweep the chip off and repeat the whole performance, but then he took a step back, stumbling over Timothy’s bag, and the spell was broken. “What the fuck, man,” he shouted, to no one in particular. Still looking hunted, he grabbed his friend by the arm and dragged him out of the circle, almost taking Timothy with them. At the last second, the bully released the younger boy, who bent and began silently gathering his things.

And just like that, it was over. The crowd exploded into conversation as Ashleigh’s shoulders sagged. Conor rushed over to him as the edges of the circle began to fray—a smaller space remained, as though Ashleigh’s presence was generating some kind of force field, and no one else came close.

“What the fuck, man?” Conor echoed. He reached out tentatively, put a hand on Ashleigh’s shoulder, and was relieved when the other boy didn’t shrug it off.

“They never mess with the crazy ones,” Ashleigh said wearily. “Best I could come up with on short notice.”

“Well, it worked, but—Jesus.” Ashleigh had stuck his fist inside his shirt, wrapping the fabric tightly around it, and Conor could see fresh blood already leaking through. “We can ask the driver for some ice when he gets here—”

“Not taking the bus.” With a groan, Ashleigh began rising to his feet again; Conor put a hand under his arm, and he took the help without comment. “I’m walking.” He turned, and Conor followed his gaze to Timothy, who was standing rigidly a few feet away, trembling, his bag back on his shoulders and both fists clenched.

“Tell Mom,” Ashleigh called out.

“Fuck you,” Timothy spat. “Tell her yourself.”

And then the younger boy spun on his heel and stalked away, disappearing into the crowd. Ashleigh stiffened, a look of surprise breaking through the exhaustion on his face.

Conor blinked. “What—”

“Dunno,” Ashleigh said, sounding almost threadbare. His eyes remained fixed on the spot where Timothy had stood. Conor watched the rise and fall of his shoulders, the way each breath leaked slowly in, and left as if crushed out of him by some vast weight.

He opened his mouth to speak, to say—what—something—

The sound of a diesel engine roaring to life made both of them jump. Turning, they saw that the driver had arrived, that the bus doors were now open and there were shadowy shapes moving down the aisle within.

“I’ll go with you,” Conor said. “We need to t—”

“No,” Ashleigh said flatly. “Look after my brother.”

“Ash—”

“No.” He looked directly into Conor’s eyes for the first time, and underneath the exhaustion there was steel, and fire, and something else—something new and unfamiliar. “I’m going alone. Don’t call.”

And without another word, he was gone—turned and began walking, up the row and toward the street, his pace slow but unwavering, hand still tucked into his shirt. He didn’t look back, and Conor made no motion to follow—just watched until he disappeared over the hill, feeling lost all over again, and almost surprised when he looked down and there was no body lying at his feet.

•••

Conor was slumped at the kitchen table when his parents came home, eyes closed, head resting on his forearms. It was almost six o’clock, the sky already darkening as the two cars pulled into the garage, one right after the other. They said nothing as they came in, and neither did Conor. There were small sounds—the scrape of a drawer, the soft clunk of a knife, chair legs sliding along the floor. A plate was placed on the table, and pushed toward him.

He lifted his head. His mother had made him apple slices with peanut butter, and both she and his father were sitting across the table, arms crossed and waiting.

He picked up an apple slice and licked it, knowing without looking that his mother’s nose was wrinkling with disapproval. After another long silence, he sighed and put the whole slice in his mouth, chewing it into applesauce before swallowing.

“I’m in trouble,” he said finally, lifting his eyes to meet theirs. They sat like bookends, mirror images of stern concern on each of their faces.

“What tipped you off?” his mother asked dryly.

“You guys never say hi when you’re mad.” He reached for another apple slice and bit it in half, putting his chin back down on his forearm. He should have expected this, he realized, just like he should have expected Eichardt’s attack on Timothy. He wondered what else he wasn’t expecting, and how bad it would be when it happened.

You need sleep.

“How else did you know, Conor?” his father asked quietly.

This was how it went, with his parents. No yelling or arguing, no lies or excuses. He couldn’t recall the last time they had pronounced judgment on him; they almost always waited until he pronounced it on himself.

He tried to gather his thoughts. “You had to come pick me up from a police car.”

“Why would we be mad about that? It was an emergency, you called 911, case closed.”

“But there was a fight, and I was, um. Involved.”

“Nonsense. You told us you didn’t lay a finger on Guyler, and we believe you. Unless you were lying?”

“I wasn’t lying.” He finished off the second apple slice and reached for a third. “I was trespassing on school property?”

“Come on, Conor. You’re stalling.”

He chewed thoughtfully, wondering as he did so what answer they were looking for. Almost, he wished he could just tell them everything, have them mete out punishment and call it a day. Then it could all go back to normal, back to the way it had been, no more ambulances and assistant principals and secret bags of marijuana—

But that wouldn’t work. There wasn’t a normal to go back to. It had been an illusion all along, and the illusion was gone.

“Conor?”

“Sorry.” He popped another apple slice into his mouth, tried to force his mind back on track. “Um. I wasn’t very helpful when the AP called me into his office this morning? Did you hear about that?”

“Warmer, but you’ve gone too far. Back it up a little.”

Conor stiffened, his brow furrowing. Backing it up a little would take him to the police cordon, and the bag, and the rooftop. But there was no way they could know about those, was there?

He felt his throat constrict. No, they couldn’t. For one thing, even they wouldn’t be this calm, not if they were waiting for those particular bombs to drop. For another, he’d left before they even—

Oh.

“This morning,” he said.

“Bingo.”

“I left early.”

“Too early,” his father corrected.

“With no note,” his mother added. “After everything that happened yesterday. How do you think your father felt, when he came down to wake you up and found an empty bed?”

They all knew she was the one who came downstairs first in the morning.

“Your mother had to call the school to make sure you were all right,” his father said, picking up the thread.

“I’m sorry.”

“Oh, not yet,” his mother said cheerfully. “But you will be.”

Conor’s heart sank. “How long?”

“A couple of weeks,” his father answered. “More, if you haven’t finished all the yardwork by then.”

Conor looked at him dully. “You planned this.”

“Not at all. But it is convenient, don’t you think? Rake the leaves, mow the grass, and stack the firewood. Oh, and blow off the driveway and the roof while you’re at it. We can negotiate about any weeding once you’re through.”

“You want me to use the leaf blower on the roof?”

“What, suddenly monkey boy is afraid of heights?”

They were past the hard part now, and into banter. Conor could never decide if he liked his parents’ method or not. It was good that they never yelled, that the three of them could laugh together even in the middle of a lecture.

But still. He sometimes wished there could be sides. Something to press against, something to fight. With all of them working together, there was nowhere for his frustration to go, and so it built and built and built—

“Two weeks is a long time,” he said slowly, hoping to bargain.

“Not nearly as long as the infinity I spent between finding you gone and hearing that you were safe,” his mother shot back. “Two weeks is nothing. You’ll barely even be started with the quarter by then.”

That reminded him. “Ashleigh’s suspended for two weeks. They told me this morning.”

“Oh?”

“Can I take him his homework?”

His parents exchanged a look.

“Can’t Timothy do that, now that he’s graduated from Littleton?” his father asked.

“If I’m grounded and Ashleigh is suspended, it’ll be my only chance to see him.”

Silence.

“Twenty minutes,” he pleaded. “Walking time included, just down the street and back. Please?”

“What is there for tonight?”

“Nothing.” He knew better than to lie.

“Go to your room while we talk it over. No computer, no phone.”

He stood up.

“Yes, ma’am,” his father prompted.

“Yes, ma’am, yes, sir,” he echoed.

He walked down the hallway and into his bedroom, closing the door without turning on the light. Flopping down onto the bed, he rolled over and stared up at the ceiling, at the scattering of glow-in-the-dark stars still clinging to the light they’d gathered in the day. He could hear his parents’ voices through the wall—muffled, indistinguishable, warm, reassuring. He had heard those same voices through that same wall for as long as he could remember.

He rolled over onto his stomach, buried his face in the pillow, tried not to think.

About Ashleigh.

About Timothy.

About the bag, and the gap, and the long year ahead.

About Ashleigh.

Sighing, he got up, turned on the light, and moved over to his desk, where he fiddled with his supplies as his mind wandered. Pens, pencils, erasers, paperclips—he picked them up and put them down, switched them from one compartment to another, moved his sticky notes from the drawer to the stand and back again, rearranged the books on the bookshelf. On a whim, he got out a sheet of notebook paper and drew an 85 at the top. Thinking for a moment, he wrote the words Dear Ashleigh in the upper left corner. Then he erased the Dear and wrote Hey. Then he threw the paper away.

He pulled out the blank book that Ashleigh’s mother had given him for his last birthday—a black, leatherbound journal, the kind with a little ribbon for marking your place. He opened it to the first page, which was astonishingly, paralyzingly white.

There was a knock at his door.

“Come in,” he called. His father entered, walked around the bed, sat down on it facing him. It was low, so his eyes were exactly on level with Conor’s.

“Your mother and I agreed that you can take Ashleigh his homework. Twenty minutes, like you said. Twenty minutes and two seconds, and you’re done.”

“Thanks.”

The word came out sounding flat, but Conor meant it. He was exhausted now, drained and limp, but he would go over there soon—needed to go over there soon, he wasn’t sure exactly what it was that had fallen between him and the other boy but whatever it was, the longer it lay there the harder it would set.

He glanced at the open book on his desk. Maybe he could write something down.

His father cleared his throat. “There’s something else, Conor, and it’s awkward to try to get you to say it, so I’m going to give you one chance, and then I’m going to say it myself.”

Conor blinked, feeling whiplashed. His father was looking at him seriously, expectantly, eyes full of concern.

Oh hell.

The silence stretched out uncomfortably. Conor could feel the tension in his body, rising in tandem with the growing disappointment on his father’s face. What is it, what is it, what does he want me to say, what did I do—

When he could bear it no longer, he spoke, trying his best not to sound flippant.

“Can I have a hint?”

“Yes, you can have a hint. Smoke.”

A kind of chain reaction took place in Conor’s head. It began with they know, which became they know about the drugs, and then they know about me and the drugs, that I took drugs. From there, his brain leapt to I’m the kind of kid who does drugs, and as the whole thing reached critical mass, it overwhelmed him. He gasped out loud as what felt like fifteen different feelings slammed into him all at once—guilt mixed with shame laced with dread and smothered in self-loathing, all layered atop a vast boiling reservoir of fear and desperation. All of the emotions he’d managed to juggle (and therefore keep from crushing him) all day—they all came crashing down at once, and he burst into tears.

Instantly, his father was off the bed, was beside him on his knees, arms wrapped around Conor’s shoulders. Conor turned into the embrace, pressed his face into his father’s chest and wept, shuddering into the dark as meaningless words of comfort wrapped around him, washed over him. They sat like that for a long time—whether seconds or minutes, Conor couldn’t have said—and slowly he became aware that his father was no longer murmuring, was calling his name, calling him back.

“Conor. Conor—Conor.”

He came back to himself with a jerk, like waking up from a dream—lifted his head, saw the smears of tears and snot he’d left on his father’s shirt and felt another wave of disgust. He could feel his chin trembling, and another handful of tears rolled silently down his cheeks.

“Conor, wait.”

He looked up at his father’s nose, unable to make himself meet his eyes.

“Conor, it’s okay. This is okay. You can cry, it’s fine. But Conor—Conor! Wait. Talk, first. Tell me, first, and then we can deal with it.”

And his father’s voice was so soft, so familiar, so reassuring, that Conor did tell him, the words tumbling over one another, fighting to get out. He told him the whole story, the whole entire story, from the very first day on the rooftop, when they’d discovered the bag and conceived of the plan—that they were going to catch a bad guy.

“We waited all summer, Dad, every day we were up there waiting for somebody to come and get it, we had cameras and tripods and everything, we even spent the night up there a couple of times and we didn’t mean it, Ashleigh didn’t mean it, we just—we just thought of it, and we tried it, and it was—it was stupid, it was wrong, we didn’t mean for Guyler to end up in the hospital, it’s all my fault, I could have said no but I didn’t, I said yes and now it’s all gone crazy.” It poured out of him in a flood, and it was like poison leaving his veins—when he was done, he collapsed into his father’s arms and cried again.

And then he was finished, and in the emptiness that followed the storm he sobered up, and in that instant he was ashamed for an entirely different reason—because he had broken, broken down and cried like a baby in front of his father, it had been years since he’d done that. He felt naked, and weak, and he turned away, suddenly unable to bear the weight of his father’s gaze, and he scrubbed violently at his face with his sleeve until his eyes and nose stopped leaking.

A coward and a criminal and now a crybaby.

“Sorry,” he choked out, trying to keep his voice level.

His father burst out laughing.

Conor twisted, humiliated, furious, only to see that there was no mockery in his father’s expression, that his eyes still held the same love and care that had been there from the start. “Conor,” he said. “Didn’t I say it was okay to cry?”

Conor said nothing.

“There’s nothing wrong with being upset, kiddo. You’ve been carrying this—this—it sounds like a mountain, honestly, all day long you’ve been carrying all of this, and nobody asked you if you wanted it, and you didn’t have anybody to help you—hell, I’d be worried if you didn’t feel like crying a little, right now. That’s what would scare me, to think that you’d turned into the kind of person who just takes all of this in stride.”

“I didn’t cry just a little.”

“Forget it, Conor, there’s no shame in that. Or rather, there is shame, but it’s a good shame, a shame that means you feel bad about being bad, that you haven’t taken the badness inside of you and decided to get comfortable with it. What are you worried about—that I’ll think less of you?”

He looked into Conor’s eyes and saw the answer there, reached out and pulled him into another hug, squeezing so tight it almost hurt. “Conor, I’m just glad you told me. We smelled it on you last night, you know—your mother and I both smelled it. Probably the cop smelled it, too, that Grimes or whoever he was, but he had bigger fish to fry and he was nice enough to let it slide. We were going to talk to you about it this morning, but when we woke up, you were already gone.”

“That’s why I’m grounded, isn’t it. It’s not about me leaving early.”

“Well, that too, but no, you’re right—it wouldn’t have been such a big deal if it hadn’t been for your poor timing.”

“So you don’t think—you don’t think I’m—”

Conor tried mightily, but he couldn’t finish the sentence, couldn’t quite make it past the embarrassment that his father had assured him he didn’t need to feel. It was too childish, the question he wanted to ask—too blunt, too simple, and he couldn’t bring himself to do it, even though he burned with the need to hear the answer—

“No, Conor, you’re not a bad person. You made a mistake, that’s all. Nowhere near as bad as the one Ashleigh made. You didn’t hurt anyone but yourself—and to be honest, you probably didn’t hurt yourself, not even a little bit, one joint isn’t going to hurt you, not at all, although don’t tell your mother I said that. How do you think we both recognized the smell?”

Conor nodded, and hiccupped, and his father laughed—they both laughed, and the laughter was twice as good as anything he’d felt in the past day, better even than the moment when he’d broken through the gap. His father hugged him again, and spoke to him for a few more minutes, and promised him that he wouldn’t have to see his mother until the morning, when the shame of it wouldn’t be quite so fresh. And then he kissed him on the forehead, and ruffled his hair, and left, and Conor fell onto the bed, and for the second night in a row he drifted off without undressing, though it wasn’t even seven yet.

Two thoughts came to him, then, in the shifting fog that came just before sleep. The first was that Ashleigh—whose burden was surely even heavier than his own—did not have a father to confess to, had only a mother who worked two full-time jobs, and would not be able to show him how to set it down, to give him the relief that Conor had now felt, had not even really known he needed.

This was desperately sad, and for a moment it almost roused him—almost sent him for his jacket, his shoes—but he was tired, so tired, empty now—and besides, he was grounded—it could wait—it could wait until tomorrow, Conor would talk to him tomorrow and would drag him back, understood now just how far into despair a single mistake could drive you, and he believed (as his father believed in him) that Ashleigh was good, and could use a reminder.

And then the second thought—a lone, flickering fragment, as oblivion closed in—that in all of that confession, his father had not asked—

(And he had not volunteered.)

—what had happened to the bag.
