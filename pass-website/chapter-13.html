December passed like a dream.

Not like a dream, the way that people meant when they talked about weddings or vacations or winning a trophy. December was like a dream in the other sense—fragmented, incoherent, a puzzle put together with pieces from half a dozen different boxes. There were moments when time seemed to surge ahead and moments when it slowed to a crawl, and moments that felt like they weren’t part of anything at all, islands of time disconnected from everything else around them, as if pasted in from some alternate universe.

It started on the Tuesday after the fire, when Ashleigh said Wish me luck as the bus squealed to a stop in the parking lot, then darted suddenly up the aisle, a dozen other kids filling the space between them. Conor got off as quickly as he could, began racing through the courtyard, searching, his heart in his throat—found the other boy at last by the flagpole, at the center of a circle that also held Guyler Stevens.

“—was fucked up, and there’s no excuse,” Ashleigh was saying, as Conor broke through the wall of spectators.

(Guyler was just standing there, rigid, trembling.)

“And you don’t have to forgive me, either, and to all of you motherfuckers watching, this isn’t about, like, making me look good, or—or trying to set him up, or something, like now he has to be cool about it just because I said sorry. He can stay as mad as he wants, because me saying sorry doesn’t fix anything. I’m just saying it because it’s true. It was fucked up and I haven’t really done anything to make it right and—and he doesn’t have to let me, either, he doesn’t owe me a chance to make it right. He doesn’t owe me shit, I owe him. But I am sorry, and I was wrong, and—and that’s it, really. That’s just…how it is. It was already true—I’m just, like, admitting it. He could kick me in the nuts right this second, and I wouldn’t stop him, and I wouldn’t tell, and we still wouldn’t be even.”

And then the bell had rung, and they had all just gone to class, as if that sort of thing was allowed, as if anyone could have a normal day, after something like that. Five and a half hours later, Conor had sat down at Guyler’s table at lunch, and the other boy had snarled I don’t want to talk about it, and so they’d just sat there, silent, each in his own little bubble.

(“Why’d you do it?” Conor had asked, on the bus on the way home.)

(“Because you wanted me to.”)

Elsewhere, there were dates with Jennifer, and afterschool tutoring sessions with Jennifer, and late-night texting with Jennifer, that last a wild and delirious universe all its own. In the real world, the two of them had still done little more than make out—a stray and daring hand here and there, perhaps, for a few thrilling seconds at a time, but no more than that.

But through the magic mirror of their phones, where they could be at once inches away and safely miles apart, they were burning through walls like they weren’t even there. Jennifer had seen all of Conor, now, and Conor more than half of Jennifer, and the fire it stoked within him—the seeing and the being seen—surprised him, almost frightened him, left him a dripping mess and ravenous, grateful for the distance that meant he didn’t have to hold himself in check.

(But also, when he aced his first quiz and his parents called her parents to invite her over for dinner, it took no effort at all, felt entirely natural to be reserved—polite—friendly—appropriate. He was warm, and so was she—warm as they always were to one another—but it was the warmth of a fireplace, not a bonfire. He kissed her goodbye in the doorway, and the kiss was soft and chaste and tender—the sort of thing their parents thought was cute—and it was not an act, was every bit as real and honest as the other thing. It made no sense to Conor, but the simple fact of it was undeniable, and so he shrugged and carried on, comfortable with the mystery.)

In Ms. Palmano’s class there were windows, portals—lessons that felt like they opened up onto entire worlds where they might have spent a week, a month, a whole year unpacking what their teacher fit into an hour, a minute, sometimes a single sentence. It was barely English, barely a class at all—she was breaking them, on purpose, and breaking the world around them, too, and doing unnervingly little with the scattered, shattered pieces.

(“It’s a mistake to locate your victory conditions inside someone else’s head,” she said offhandedly one random morning, and on another “Prophets and kings are both right about the future, but for very different reasons.” Conor still had not fully recovered from “From the inside, being wrong feels like being right while everyone else is crazy,” and when she had said “Unstated expectations are premeditated resentments,” both he and Ashleigh had flinched, and the other boy had not looked in his direction for the rest of the class.)

(“Ever notice that when people get really, really upset, it’s usually not because anything out there in the world has actually changed, so much as that they had a pretty little fake world in their imagination, that was nicer than the truth, and then actual reality kicked down the door and ruined their fun?”)

There were Sundays, too—the sixth, seventh, and eighth installments of Conor’s so-called punishment, which was proving to be another little island all its own. Something had changed between Conor and his parents, something which clearly none of the three of them were quite able to put their finger on. But despite that, the Sundays were soft and safe and simple and slow in a way unlike any other part of his life, and he was coming to look forward to them the way a sea otter might look forward to its next breath.

And then there was their little conspiracy, him and Ashleigh and Timothy, which again had gone sideways before it even got off the ground, Timothy pulling things in a direction neither Conor nor Ashleigh had anticipated.

“The masks have to go,” he’d said. Not yes, I’m in or no, fuck you or only on one condition.

“Wait, what?”

“Why?”

“They’re different,” Timothy had said, with the tone of someone explaining to a toddler that they could only eat the same slice of cake once. “Nobody’s noticed yet, but if we keep this up much longer, somebody’s gonna snap a pic, and once they’re like, okay, one of these fuckers has a red mask, and the other has a black one…”

He’d had a point, and so the three of them had gone to Conor’s father’s workshop, had started making a new mold before Conor had even fully processed what was happening. They had mixed the colors and were just about to pour when Timothy had interrupted, asked how many they were going to make.

“One,” Conor said, at the same time that Ashleigh said “Three,” and Timothy had looked at both of them with disbelief.

“Twenty,” the younger boy had countered, and they had opened their mouths to argue, to push back, but the sixth grader had cut them off, had pointed out that this way, they wouldn’t have to carry the masks with them all the time, or leave them in their lockers or bookbags where Mr. Sykes could find them, but could instead stash them here and there all over the school.

“In fact, now that I think about it, thirty is better,” he’d said.

“And this wouldn’t have anything to do with, oh, I don’t know, making it easier for you to slip a few to some of your friends?” Ashleigh had asked, shrewdly.

But the younger boy had neither confirmed nor denied, had merely shrugged and said that they would come around to that eventually, and wouldn’t it be easier if they already had extras handy, at which point Conor had almost left, almost stormed out and given up on the plan entirely, except that Timothy had said cool, not like I wanted to work with you in the first place.

And so Conor had caved, with a deep and unshakeable sense that he would one day regret it—had spent the whole second week of December waiting for the other shoe to drop.

But it didn’t drop, and it kept not dropping, and as it happened none of the three of them managed a rescue in those first five school days anyway, nor in the next five after that. Slowly—inevitably—despite all his efforts to stay alert—Conor began to relax, his mind and muscles unable to maintain constant tension.

He worried about it—about the complacency, about the fact that nothing had changed in any significant way—that if it had, in fact, been right to be worried on Monday of one week, then it was probably right to still be worried on Friday of the next. That ten days of no disasters didn’t mean all that much, in the grand scheme of things, and that his brain—

(Which, Ms. Palmano had pointed out, was a hyperactive pattern-matching machine evolved mostly to find berries and run away from tigers.)

—was confusing nothing bad yet for nothing to worry about, period.

But—as with so, so many things—there was nothing Conor could do about it, so he simply set it aside and kept moving. School, homework, training, chores—he felt less shattered than he had in October, less like he himself was broken into pieces. But it was still disorienting, this feeling of constantly teleporting, of stepping from world to world—of being shoved from world to world, without warning, and having to relearn the laws of physics all over again.

He was writing in the journal to Guyler—with Guyler—was feeling vaguely dirty about the things he wasn’t telling Guyler—was wondering whether it was time to simply give the other boy his phone number, his handles, his email—maybe even invite him over to his house.

He was eating lunch with Holt, with Eddie, noticed vaguely that Nolan was not there, had not been there for days—looked around the cafeteria and saw the other boy at another table, him and his younger brother Nicholas both.

He bought a rose.

He and Ashleigh were different, now, too—had not yet hammered their newly reforged friendship into its final shape, were still sorting out the rules, the boundaries. On impulse, Conor had reached out one afternoon and begun to squeeze the other boy’s shoulders. Ashleigh had groaned, had melted under his touch, and—

(After ten or fifteen seconds, when Conor tried to pull away.)

—had reached up and seized his fingers, drawing him back.

Please, the other boy had clearly been saying.

And Conor said okay. It had turned into a proper backrub, and after five minutes or so, Ashleigh had turned, and hugged him, and said “Thanks” and then “Your turn.” He had pulled Conor down, down to the ground—deposited him, really—arranged him into a sitting position and settled himself in behind him and dug his fingers into Conor’s back for nearly half an hour, working out half a year’s worth of knots and tension. It had been great—the giving, too, but the receiving especially—and at the end Conor had felt like soft butter, had slumped back against his friend’s chest—

“Sorry,” Ashleigh had whispered, about the line of burning hardness now nestled against Conor’s lower back. “It isn’t—I mean, I wasn’t—”

“It’s fine,” Conor said firmly, slightly surprised to discover that it was true. “I don’t care.”

Another impulse seized him, then, and he turned, giving his friend a quick, mischievous kiss—just a peck, and only on the cheek, but it had seemed to unbind something in the other boy, who had wrapped his arms around Conor’s chest and squeezed, so tight it almost hurt, and had seemed unburdened, unstarving, undesperate when they separated ten seconds later, stood and stepped back into the flow of time.

They were all the way into the fourth week of December when at last Something Happened—when Timothy finally managed a rescue, sneaking up behind a bully with some kind of special rope contraption he had invented or discovered, snaring the boy’s legs. It had only hindered him for ten or fifteen seconds, but both Timothy and the victim had gotten away scot-free, and it was the talk of the school at lunch that day.

(“What happened to the rope?” Conor had wondered.)

Conor wasn’t sure whether it was luck, or whether things like that just naturally came in clusters, or whether perhaps some petty, competitive part of him had felt challenged, territorial—had gone into overdrive and reached until it found an opportunity he would have otherwise overlooked. But he took a chance himself, the very next morning—saw what looked like the barest beginnings of a shakedown, two freshmen that seemed to be working themselves up to something, needling a lone eighth grader, feeding off of his reactions like vampires.

Conor had had no plan, no ideas, but there had been an escape route handy, and so he had tried simply donning the mask and stepping into their field of vision, arms crossed and head held high. The two bullies had looked at him, looked at each other, and then—amazingly—slouched away, leaving the eighth grader to babble his thanks as Conor popped up onto the roof and crossed to the other side of the building, putting a little distance between himself and the scene before returning to human form.

They won’t all be that easy.

They wouldn’t all be that easy, but it was nice to know that some of them might be—that there was in fact something accumulating, the legend gaining power—that Ashleigh’s crazy idea was not just crazy.

And then it was Wednesday, a half-day, the last day of classes before Christmas, and once again Conor had awakened with something like high hopes, had come to school despite knowing that he could skip, that no one would notice, that there would not be any consequences. He didn’t quite know what he was expecting—

He had paced back and forth on the driveway while waiting for the bus, had bounced his leg the whole long way to school, been distracted and jumpy in the courtyard and homeroom and barely present in the conversation with Ashleigh and Jennifer and Holt as they waited in the hallway for Ms. Palmano’s door to open. When it finally did—

HOW WOULD YOU FEEL IF YESTERDAY WAS THE LAST DAY OF YOUR LIFE?

“I think people usually get this one wrong,” Ms. Palmano explained. “People say, how would you feel if tomorrow was the last day of your life, and then they think, oh, right, I would run around fixing all these little things about everything, like I would make sure to tell my friends and family that I love them and I’d eat my favorite food one last time and I’d say sorry for that stupid thing I did and I’d get all my affairs in order and tie up all the loose ends and then everything would be fine.”

She paused, her face tightening as she traced her eyes around the room, resting for half a heartbeat on each and every student. “But we don’t get to do that, in real life,” she continued. “We don’t get to know. You could get hit by a bus this afternoon, and then it wouldn’t matter what you could have done, if you’d just had a little bit more time. The only way you can really tell whether you’re living your life the way you want to, the way that makes you happy, is by looking back. If you died yesterday—if everything that happened this morning was a hallucination, and secretly it all wrapped up last night, after you fell asleep, how would you feel?”

There was the sort of silence that never happened in the other classrooms, that seemed to be Ms. Palmano’s entire reason for being a teacher.

“Let me be clear,” she said. “I’m not saying that you’re supposed to be done. If you died last night with no loose ends, that would be a tragedy all its own, because it would mean you’re the sort of person who doesn’t have anything they’re working on, anything left to hope for. You should always have unfinished business. The thing I’m trying to point at is, are you proud of your unfinished business? Does it feel like a half-built pyramid, as opposed to a pile of dishes you never got around to cleaning?”

She began her usual pacing. “I don’t regret my half-built pyramids,” she said. “Even if I die with a bunch of those, it feels right to have been working on them, up to and including yesterday. To be the sort of person who spent a good chunk of her time working on them, in the hopes of someday finishing. Unfinished pyramids aren’t a waste. What feels like a waste is the stuff I really could have already done—the things it feels like I really should have already done. When I imagine dying yesterday, there are a bunch of things that make me wince—or rather, there used to be, but then I started regularly asking myself this question, and I got my shit together.”

A soft rustle of surprise—

“Yes, that was an act of trust on my part. Don’t make me regret it.”

She had them pull out their paper and pencils—

“Twenty minutes,” she said. “If you finish identifying all the things that would bother you, spend any extra time thinking of ways to do something about it.”
