Chapter 16 — Caught

⚜

August 31st, 6:35PM

“Look, just answer me one question, okay?”

“Conor, can we please—”

“I came with you, didn’t I? We’re nice and cozy and safe and the big scary Gap is aaaaallll the way over there.”

“Sit down.”

“Sitting down is not going to change anything.”

“It’ll change how much I feel like I gotta be ready to tackle your crazy ass.”

“Fine, whatever, I’m sitting. Happy?”

“Happier, anyway.”

“Man, what is with you today?”

“You just tried to run off the goddamn roof.”

“A, no I did not, give me a goddamn break, and B, do you really actually think we can’t handle it?”

“Conor.”

“Ashleigh. I’m serious. If you had to—”

“Conor.”

“If you had a reason, something more important to you than being a little bitch—”

“What’s with you today, geez?”

“—if Timothy was over there, on fire, or like—like if somebody had a gun, or whatever, and they told you to, I think you’d just do it and it would be fine and I think you know that.”

“Conor.”

“Admit it. You can do it, and you know it.”

“Conor, why is this so goddamn important all of a sudden?”

“Because we’re ready, Ash. We can do this. It’s time.”

“I’m not ready.”

“Because you’re scared.”

“Yeah, dude! We covered that already. I’m not, like, hiding it.”

“But you’re wrong.”

“What?”

“You’re wrong to be scared. You can do this.”

“So what? There’s tons of stuff I can do that I’m not going to do.”

“So you admit it.”

“Conor—”

“You admit it, yeah? That it’s just ‘cause you’re scared? It’s not because you actually think you can’t do it.”

•          •          •

A tiny pain, sharp but not piercing, a flashlight on the edge of a supernova.

“Conor.  Can you feel that?”

He wasn’t awake.  Not really.  But he could hear, sort of.  See, sort of.  Knew that it was cold, and dark, and that Ashleigh was with him.

“Conor, come on, man, answer me.”

The tiny pain came again, like someone digging a rock into his skin.

“Yeah,” Conor said.

“Where is it?”

That one was harder, but—

“Thumb.”

“Okay.  Okay.  That’s good, okay?  That’s real good.  Just—just hang in there.”

“What’s—”

“Shhh, don’t worry.  You’re going to be okay.  I’ve got you.”

•          •          •

“—the big blue one, yeah.  And like—fuck, I don’t know, five hundred feet—”

Still dark.  Still cold.  Ashleigh was close—close enough that Conor could feel him, smell him, but his voice was far away, somehow, far away and only halfway comprehensible.

“—my sweatshirt and some pants.  Don’t fuck with me, just do it.”

The pain in his arm was vast, enormous, almost all-consuming.  But there was a tiny sliver of space for other things, a narrow corona of awareness around the otherwise total eclipse.

“Ashleigh?” he said weakly.

There was the crunch of gravel, and instantly the other boy was at his side, face shadowed in the fading moonlight.  “Hey, Batman,” the other boy said softly.  “What’s up?”

“What’s…?”

“We’re on the roof, still, buddy.  You got hurt.  We’re—I’m gonna take care of you.”

“Why are you…naked?”

“You were cold, so I wrapped you up in my jacket.  See?  Can you see my jack—no, don’t move, stay still, just…look.”

Conor looked, realizing for the first time that he was on his back, lying on his back on the gravel, his head pointed up at the deep black of the night sky.

“Hurts,” he said thickly.

“I know, buddy.  I know.  But it’s gonna be okay.”

•          •          •

There were more voices this time—voices arguing.

“—not stupid, all right?”

“You sure?  Because by my watch, it’s been like forty-five minutes—”

“Timothy.  We can do this.”

And then another presence, closer, softer, quieter—

“Conor?  Can you hear me?”

A voice he knew, a voice he had heard before—

“Drink this,” the voice said.

(She said. It was a girl.)

“Slowly, okay?”

She pressed something to his lips, and warmth trickled into him, snaking its way down into his body, branching and branching until it was too thin to feel.

“Check his pulse again.”

Ashleigh’s voice, far away, and then there were fingers pressing against his wrist, a white-hot slice of pain in his arm—

“Sorry!  Sorry, sorry, so sorry, Conor, I’ll—Timothy, can you bring me the tape?”

Gentle hands on his shoulders, on the back of his head, lifting him up, carefully holding his arm in place.  The sound of duct tape tearing, and then tugging, pressure—

“Easy, buddy.  This might hurt a little bit, but you’re okay.  We’re just taping your arm down so it doesn’t flop around and hurt worse, okay?”

More pain.

Conor sank back below the surface.

•          •          •

“—get all this?”

“Maddie and Julia went to Walmart.”

“Who?”

“Isabella’s sister Maddie, and her friend Julia.  Jennifer’s sister.”

“You looped in—”

“Look, you said to get it done, we’re getting it done.  Get off my dick, all right?  It’s already almost two o’clock—”

•          •          •

“—gotta be fucking kidding me—”

“What’s the problem?  Weren’t you literally just saying four would be better than three?”

•          •          •

This time, the awakening was sharper, clearer, the world only three-quarters fog.

Timothy.

That was Timothy’s voice he had been hearing, fighting with Ashleigh.  He was still on the roof—they were all still on the roof.  There was cloth under his head, and a heavy blanket draped over him.  Under it, he could feel that his arms had been folded across his chest like a mummy, were held in place by something snug, something that wrapped around his torso like a strait jacket.  His feet were elevated, resting on a soft object that he couldn’t see, another blanket or a towel or something. The moon was gone, and the stars were bright and piercing.

“Hey.”

Conor turned his head—

Guyler?

And—and—Isabella, that was it, that was her name.  Timothy’s girlfriend, the soft voice he’d heard earlier—

“Easy, easy.”

Isabella lifted a dark shape.  “Are you thirsty?” she asked.

“Yeah.”

She pressed the shape to his lips, and for the second time he tasted—

“Is that…hot Gatorade?”

“Ashleigh said electrolytes.  Are you cold?”

He was.

“Maddie brought heat packs,” Guyler said quietly.  “We’re gonna stick them on you, okay?  They’ll start getting hot in two minutes.  We gotta move you a bit.  It’ll probably hurt.”

Sounds of paper tearing, peeling.  Gentle hands again, turning his head, patches of tape going onto his neck, his forehead.  They rolled him onto his side, and put more patches under his shirt, on his back, his belly.

“Go ahead and use them all up,” Guyler murmured.  “And check his pulse again while you’re at it.”  And then, to Conor: “Are your legs cold?  Can you feel your toes?”

“Yeah.  I mean, yes, I can feel them.”

“Cold?”

“I’m okay.”

“Can you feel the fingers on your left hand?”

He could.

“Pulse still around eighty,” Isabella said.  “Doesn’t feel weird or weak or anything.”

“Do you know where you are?” Guyler asked.

“Auditorium.  On the roof.  How did—”

“Shh, not right now.”

“What’s going on?”

“We’re getting you down.  So we can call 911 without summoning a shitstorm.”

Conor absorbed this.

“How?” he asked.

Guyler shook his head.  “Not your job,” he said.

“But—”

“Do you trust me?”

Conor nodded.

“And you trust Ashleigh?”

“Yeah.”

“Okay, well, Timothy doesn’t trust either of us, and we’re not doing anything until he’s on board, too.”

•          •          •

“We’re not gonna make it.  It’s like three forty-five.”

“How long do you think this is actually gonna take, once we get him moving?”

•          •          •

Again, hands lifting him, turning him, resettling him.

“What are you doing?” he asked.

“Your mom,” Ashleigh murmured.  “What’s eight times eight?”

“What?”

“Eight times eight, do you remember?”

“Don’t ask him a fucking math question, that’s not going to give us any—”

“Conor,” interrupted Guyler’s voice.  “What day is it?”

“It—”

He tried to remember.

“Thursday,” he said.  “Thursday night.  I guess—I guess maybe Friday morning.”

“How’s his pulse?”

“Fine.”

“Still not sweating?”

Fingers probing at his cheek, his neck.

“Cold but not clammy.”

“Conor, can you feel the fingers in your left hand?”

“…no.”

“No?”

“No.”

He saw Timothy and Ashleigh exchanging glances, felt a shiver of fear.

“We said we’d call if his fingers went numb.”

“Yeah, but at this point, it’s not like they’d be able to get him down any faster.  We’re almost there.  Fifteen minutes, tops.”

Guyler’s face appeared in his field of view.  “We’re going to tape you to the board, now,” he said, as if Timothy and Ashleigh weren’t there, hadn’t spoken.  “You’re not going to be able to move at all, okay?  It’s so you don’t fall off.”

Time passed.

“You still with us?”

Conor nodded.

“We’re going to turn you upside down.  To make sure.  It might—your arm might hurt when we do it.  We tried to tape you down as snug as we could, but there might be some pressure.  Scream if you have to.”

•          •          •

“Okay,” said Guyler.  “We have a plan.  We think it’s solid.  You’d be on the ground in about three minutes, and then we can call 911.  Ready to hear it?”

Conor nodded.

“You’re taped and tied to a board.  That board has six ropes coming off it.  They’re wrapped up around some pipes up here on the roof, just to make extra sure.  I’ve got one, Ashleigh’s got one, Timothy’s got one, Isabella’s got one.  One of them goes to Holt and Eddie, in the tree.”

Holt?

And Eddie?

“The last one goes to Jennifer.  She’s down on the ground, waiting for you.  With her sister and Isabella’s sister and some of Timothy’s people.  Sam Marlin is down there, too.  We’re gonna slide you over the edge, feet first.  Ashleigh says you weigh about a hundred pounds.  We already picked you up, remember?  When we turned you upside down?”

He remembered.

“So you’re not too heavy.  With the board and the ropes, it’s maybe thirty, thirty-five pounds for each of us, okay?  One really full backpack.  We’re going to let you down bit by bit, and the other ropes are going to make sure you don’t scrape against the bricks.”

Conor tried to visualize it.

“We’re not going to do this unless you say it’s okay.  If you want, we’ll call 911 from up here and let the grownups figure out how to get you down.  But we’re pretty sure we got this.  We already used the ropes to pull up some stuff that was heavier than you, just to make absolutely sure we could handle it.”

Conor thought for a moment.

“Ash,” he said.

Ashleigh’s face appeared beside Guyler’s.

“Conor.”

“I’m scared.”

“I know.”

The two boys looked at each other.

But if we call the grownups while you’re up here on the roof…

“Ash,” Conor repeated.

“I got you,” Ashleigh whispered.  “I won’t let you fall.”

“None of us will,” said Guyler, his voice iron.

•          •          •

A moment of vertigo, the feeling of a rollercoaster sliding over the crest, and then—

Weightlessness.

Conor drifted through the air, bobbing softly, mostly upright, his body sagging downward against what must have been three or four whole rolls of duct tape.  He could see the leaves of the magnolia tree, the roofs of the electives building, the office, the courtyard beyond.

Down.

Down.

Down.

And then there was the ground, and on the ground was Jennifer, her lips on his, her face filling his vision, her hands like fire on his cheeks.

“It’s okay,” she said.  “We got you.”

•          •          •

“Are you sure?”

Conor nodded.  “I’m sure,” he said.  “Help me up.”

Hands—so many hands—more than a dozen hands touching him, lifting him, steadying him.  He felt his legs tremble as they took his weight for the first time in hours, felt everyone’s eyes on him, took a deep breath.  He felt sick, dizzy, weak.

But he was standing.

“I’m okay,” he said.

Guyler stepped in front of him, his face lit by the light of someone’s phone.

“Tell me again,” he said.

“I’m okay,” Conor repeated.  “I can walk.”

“So we’re skipping the whole ambulance thing, then, right?” said one of the high school girls, the one Conor was pretty sure was Jennifer’s older sister. “If he’s good enough to walk then I can just drive him to the hospital.”

“And me,” Guyler said.

Jennifer’s hand slipped into his, squeezed.

“Timothy,” said Ashleigh.  “Can your crew—”

“Yeah, we’ll take care of it.”

“Don’t forget we left our bikes—”

“I said we got it.  Get him out of here.”

Conor looked out at the sea of faces.

He didn’t even recognize a third of them.

“Thank you,” he whispered.

•          •          •

It was coming up on 5AM by the time they turned into the parking lot of the regional medical center. Conor was in the back seat, sandwiched between Jennifer and Ashleigh, still covered in duct tape and heat pads, still wearing Ashleigh’s clothes over his own. The heavy blue blanket they had used to wrap him up on the roof, that usually lived on the Falhanner couch, stretched across all three of them, cozy and familiar.

“Thanks for the ride,” Conor said, as Julia pulled into a shadowed space in the far corner of the lot, as Ashleigh and Jennifer began unfolding themselves from the car. His voice was still unsteady, took extra effort to keep from cracking. “And—and for everything.”

He had pieced together most of it, but Ashleigh had confirmed it all in quiet murmurs as they drove through the empty streets between the school and the hospital.

He had heard Conor’s strangled screams, found him cold and shaking on the roof, had been unable to get him to talk. Had taken off his jacket and pants and put them on Conor to keep him warm, tucked his t-shirt under Conor’s head.

Then he’d called Timothy, and Holt.

Holt had called Eddie, and Sam Marlin.

Timothy had called a lot of people.

“Yeah, I don’t know,” Ashleigh had said, as they drove toward the hospital. “I think there were like four or five of them, plus Isabella and her sister. Maddie, I think her name is—”

“It’s Maddie,” Jennifer confirmed.

“I kinda got the sense they were Timothy’s mask crew. The only one I recognized was Trevor Shattuck.”

Conor had glanced over at Jennifer beside him, at Guyler riding shotgun, to see if either of them had any reaction to the phrase mask crew. But if they did, they gave no outward sign.

There had been no rope in the Falhanner home, nor at Holt’s or Isabella’s. Sam had brought a pair of 100-foot heavy-duty outdoor extension cables, but that had been it, and that had not been enough.

So Maddie had woken Jennifer’s sister, Julia, who had a car and a driver’s license. The two high schoolers had gone together to the 24-hour Walmart over in Grantville, taking Jennifer with them, and bought five hundred feet of climbing rope.

And adhesive heat pads.

And glowsticks.

And walkie-talkies.

And five rolls of duct tape.

And a sled made of hard, molded plastic—the board they had taped him to—with handles for them to loop the ropes through.

(And a box of s’mores Pop Tarts. Jennifer was still something at him, they were not okay, but she had put one in his mouth after they had finished cutting him loose and it had tasted like Christmas.)

“Don’t sweat,” Julia said, as Conor carefully slid his way out of the back seat, letting Ashleigh do more than half of the heavy lifting. “Just—if you ever feel like being mean to my sister, don’t. I kept all the rope, and I think there’s still a roll of duct tape left, too.”

Conor smiled weakly.

Timothy had come straight to the school, had been the first of the rescuers to arrive, had biked to East Binder with a blanket larger than he was wrapped around his shoulders and a bag of extra clothes for Ashleigh strapped to his chest. Isabella had biked, too, pausing just long enough to microwave a quart of Gatorade and pour the steaming liquid into a thermos.

(“I Googled shock,” Ashleigh explained. “What to look out for, what to do. So we’d know if we needed to abort.”)

(They had checked his pulse every couple of minutes, and Isabella had kept an eye on his breathing. The internet had said that it would be very bad if Conor’s fingers went numb, but thankfully it hadn’t been a big problem, hadn’t been nerve damage or compartment syndrome, had gone away as soon as they cut off the tape holding his arm in place.)

Timothy had followed Ashleigh’s instructions on how to get to the roof, so that Conor would not be left alone, even for a minute—had done the entire climb, by himself, in the dark, for the very first time, with no help or guidance. Had gone back down, when his friends arrived, and had done it again, tying an extension cord to the bottom of the pipe so that Isabella could climb straight up, bypassing the shed and the awning and the magnolia.

And then—seemingly out of nowhere—Guyler Stevens had appeared, a crucial fourth pair of hands. Apparently Jennifer had called him while still at the Walmart—had overheard Isabella checking in, saying that she didn’t know what they were going to do since no one else present was willing to brave the ascent.

The other boy had said nothing at all in the car during the long, quiet drive, said next to nothing on the rooftop. But it had been Guyler who had suggested the fifth and sixth ropes, so that Conor wouldn’t be bouncing and scraping against the wall the whole way down.

(And it had been Holt and Eddie in the tree, Jennifer and Sam on the ground, who’d held them.)

And then they’d sent Conor over the edge, and by the time they freed him from the sled and helped him to his feet, the roof team had already thrown down all of the ropes, all of the cables. Ashleigh had been the last one down the pipe, had undone the final knot so that there was no sign they had ever been there, no sign at all. And there had been so many of them standing around, in that final moment—

(Conor still wasn’t sure what Timothy’s five silent friends had even done, how they had contributed, maybe they had just been there, just in case—extra hands, extra eyes, extra hearts.)

Dear Conor, how many people do you think would stick their necks out for you?

He would have guessed three, maybe four (not counting his parents). But over a dozen of them had come. Had come in the middle of the night—had snuck out of their houses at one in the morning on a school night, risking grounding or worse—had come together and pulled off an insane, impossible stunt…

(We did it! Eddie had whooped, and Conor hadn’t even felt like strangling him.)

“Yo, you hanging in there?” Ashleigh murmured, wrapping a steadying hand around Conor’s unbroken arm as he staggered.

“Yeah,” Conor breathed. “It’s just—”

A lot.

It had been a lot, and the pain was still overwhelming, was only taking up half of his brain now instead of virtually all of it but half was a lot. Conor felt like he was running twice as hard as usual just to keep up half the usual pace.

“—we should get me inside pretty quick, I think.”

Jennifer came around the back of the car, drew him into a careful hug—

“Hey, whoa, no,” came Ashleigh’s voice. “The fuck do you think you’re doing?”

They pulled apart, surprised, only to see that Ashleigh was not looking at them, was looking at Guyler, who had exited the car, who was standing on the sidewalk, hands in his pockets, breath misting in the lamplight.

He said nothing. Only shrugged, as if to say isn’t it obvious?

“The whole point of having Julia drop us off way the fuck out here is so that nobody else has to get in tr—”

“Let him come,” Conor croaked.

There was a sour sort of silence.

“Whatever,” Ashleigh said, in a voice that was halfway between a sigh and a snarl.

“Good luck,” Jennifer whispered, before drifting backward.

And then she got in the car, and Ashleigh pulled out his phone, tapping roughly at the screen. He cleared his throat, shot one more glare at Guyler, then took a deep breath, his shoulders settling.

“Mr. Moreton?” he said. “Hi—yeah, it’s me. Listen—sorry to call so early, but, um. You’re going to be mad, but—everything’s okay, okay? Conor’s fine. I mean, he’s not fine, but…yeah. Yeah. We’re at the hospital, about to check in.”

•          •          •

The arm was broken.

There were looks of deep suspicion when the three boys first walked through the doors unchaperoned, but scarcely more than ten minutes passed before Conor’s parents stormed into the waiting room, and given that they had clearly snuck out—

(As opposed to being actually neglected.)

—it seemed unlikely that child protective services would be called. For a while, everything was chaos, an endless parade of hospital staff, nurses checking his vitals and taking X-rays and putting in IVs and feeding him drugs. They numbed his arm, reset the bone, then wrapped it tight with gauze and sealed the gauze inside a cast and put the cast inside a sling.

And then—just like that—it was over. They bundled him into the backseat of his mother’s car, Ashleigh and Guyler on either side of him, his father riding in the passenger seat. They left the hospital and headed—

Oh. Right.

—for East Binder, right back toward East Binder, it was almost eight o’clock and Guyler and Ashleigh, at least, were not off the hook, were going to attend school just like they were supposed to, just like normal—

“We haven’t got our uniforms,” Ashleigh protested weakly.

“And just imagine,” Conor’s mother replied, her voice like dry ice. “You might have to do a whole detention about it.”

The rest of the drive wasn’t any better. There were questions, and lies, and threats and promises (Guyler and Ashleigh were not spared). The three boys were contrite, and respectful, the two adults restrained but clearly not buying it. The whole thing had the feel of a cold war one misstep away from turning hot.

And then they were there, pulling into the front circle along with the rest of the stragglers. And then Ashleigh and Guyler were getting out of the car, leaving Conor exposed on both sides, and it was just going to be him, just him and his parents with no one else—no one to serve as buffer, as witness—

For a wild, incoherent moment, he thought that maybe he would jump out of the car himself, and make a run for it.

But that was crazy.

“Good luck,” Ashleigh whispered, leaning in and planting a kiss on Conor’s cheek.

Guyler said nothing, just stuck a fist out for Conor to bump.

And then the doors closed, and the car began to move.

“So,” Conor’s mother said quietly.

She doesn’t know, remember that she doesn’t know, she just thinks you snuck out early and hurt your arm being Batman—

“Where’s your phone?”

Conor blinked. “I don’t know,” he answered honestly. And then, less honestly: “It must have fallen out of my pocket when I f—when I hu—when I got hurt.”

Smooth.

“…and then I guess we just didn’t notice it, in the dark.”

“And by ‘we,’ you mean Ashleigh and Guyler.”

Yes? Because who else—

Except no, that wasn’t it, that wasn’t the reason for the peculiar emphasis she had put on those two particular names.

“It’s not their fault,” he said. “They weren’t even there when I got hurt.”

“Oh? And they knew to come how, then?”

Conor winced. “Okay, Ashleigh was with me, but it wasn’t—I mean, he didn’t—it was my idea to go out, you can ask him, I’m the one who dragged him into—”

“And then you broke your arm,” his mother said, cutting him off. “And lost your phone, and that’s when Ashleigh decided to call Guyler Stevens for help.”

Conor felt a black hole forming in the back of his throat.

We really should’ve got our stories straight.

“I have to say,” Conor’s father said softly. “It’s…kind of not cool, how hard it feels like you’re working out a lie, right now.”

This time the wince was bigger.

“It was—”

Something true, he needed something true—

“I’ve been trying to get Ashleigh and Guyler to stop—”

“I cannot believe you are going to just—just brush past your father like that. Pretend like you didn’t hear what he said, just now.”

“Leslie,” his father said, his voice a strange mix of pleading and warning.

“Paul,” his mother shot back.

“Leslie, this isn’t the time for this—think about what he’s just been through—”

“I’m in this car too, Paul. My feelings are here, too.”

Silence.

“I’m sorry,” Conor whispered.

“For?” his father asked. Gently, openly, sincerely…

…which only made Conor feel worse.

“I’m sorry I snuck out,” he said. “And—and I’m sorry I got hurt. Got myself hurt. That I didn’t—didn’t stay safe. And that I took too long, just now. To, um. Think.”

The words were like ash in his mouth.

“You will promise me,” his mother said. “You will promise me right now that you will not sneak out again, ever. You will promise me, and so help me god, Conor, if you say that you won’t sneak out and then you do anyway—”

“Leslie!”

“This isn’t a punishment, Paul, this is something he never had the right to in the first place.”

“We’ve never asked him not to sneak out.”

“We’ve never asked him not to rape chickens, either, but if we caught him doing that I think it would be reasonable to be upset.”

Another silence, long and awful like no silence Conor could remember.

“Please,” he whispered—

(Because he couldn’t say nothing.)

“—don’t.”

“Don’t ask you to make this promise?” his father asked, his voice once again quiet, once again calm. “Or don’t something else?”

(Conor’s mother said nothing, stared pointedly straight ahead.)

“Don’t fight.”

The words barely managed to avoid coming out as a whimper.

There was a long silence.

“Conor,” his father said soberly, breaking the silence. “Your mother has made quite a few compromises, over the years, in how we’ve raised you. There are a lot of places where I’ve asked her to trust me, and she’s said okay, and for the most part I think we’re both pretty pleased with how you’ve turned out. But right now—right now, Conor, I think she’s scared, because this thing that just happened is pretty scary, Conor, you were out in the middle of the night, you don’t have your phone, I honestly don’t know what you were doing but whatever it was, you ended up with a broken arm and I think she’s thinking, how much worse would it have had to be, for it to have been a broken neck? Or worse?”

Conor looked in the rear-view mirror at his mother’s reflection, at her eyes that were refusing to meet his, that were glued to the road in front of them.

(Her knuckles were white, where they gripped the steering wheel.)

“I’m scared too, Conor,” his father said softly. “I wasn’t expecting this. I didn’t think this was the sort of thing I needed to worry about. I thought—”

He broke off.

“What?” Conor asked, after the pause had stretched out for a year.

“Conor, I honestly don’t want to say it. It seems like it won’t do any good.”

And now Conor felt like—like—he tried to do the thing his father had taught him, to feel his body and find words that felt like they matched, and there was nothing, the closest thing he could come up with was glass beads grinding against each other, the scream of glass on the verge of shattering—

“What?” he repeated, because he had to, because he did not have any other choice at all, would not survive an hour of trying to imagine what it was his father might have been about to say.

“I thought we could trust you,” his father said simply.

(Crying, Conor was crying now, but silently, the tears leaking down his cheeks.)

“I thought—I don’t know. I know we’re not—not your friends, I know you don’t put us in the same bucket as Ashleigh and Holt, but I thought we mattered to you enough that—”

One sob escaped.

“—I thought you might be—might be careful with yourself. For our sake, if nothing else. Because it matters to you, that we would be devastated if something happened to you.”

A minute passed in agony.

“The thing is,” his father began again, his voice even softer this time. “I think it does matter to you. I don’t think that what we’re learning here is that you don’t care, I think we’re learning that you didn’t think. That being safe—that it, I don’t know, slipped your mind, in the moment, while you were having too much fun. And that’s a different problem—”

His eyes flickered toward his wife.

“—that’s a different problem from callous disregard. Or—or willful defiance.”

Conor was in pieces, now, because what could he say, what could he do, his father had it all wrong, all backwards, but if he told them what had really happened—

He drew a blank.

He literally could not imagine it.

I wasn’t, he pleaded silently.

Because it did matter to him, actually, what his father felt, what his father believed about him, those things mattered to Conor, there wasn’t any way that he could actually explain but he still wanted his father to know. That he hadn’t been being reckless, had in fact been being extremely careful, that what he’d been doing had been safe, he’d been in a safe place, following all the rules, there was no way that he or anybody else could have predicted that out of nowhere would appear—

That’s why, another part of him said, cutting off the train of thought. That’s why people stay home in their houses at night. That’s why you were supposed to be at home.

Because—

(As his father often said, had said a thousand times, it was one of his catch phrases and for that reason Conor had never bothered to actually hear it.)

—nothing good happens outdoors after midnight.

It wasn’t true, wasn’t literally true, but the bad surprises outweighed the good ones, it was easy to see (now) (in hindsight) that something awful was bound to happen eventually, there was in fact a reason why he and Ashleigh had to sneak out when they wanted to run a night mission and it was exactly the reason you would think, boring and mundane and obvious.

“Grounded,” his mother said, breaking the silence. “Open-ended.”

Conor’s father threw her a look, but said nothing, did not object.

(Conor didn’t object, either.)

“I’ll leave it in your father’s hands to determine whether you should ever be allowed to see Ashleigh or Guyler ever again. It’s bad enough if they encouraged you to sneak out of the house and get yourself sent to the emergency room. If you’re telling the truth, and what actually happened is that your behavior dragged them out at four in the morning, that you somehow convinced them to put themselves in danger—”

She broke off, her nostrils flaring. “I am, as your father might say, too close to the issue. I don’t know what on Earth I can possibly say to Linda and Christie—”

She broke off a second time. “Be thankful that arm is in a cast,” she said finally, her voice going flat. “Otherwise who knows what lengths I’d have to go to, to satisfy myself that you aren’t out there trying to break your own neck.”

Conor tried to think of something to say, and did not succeed.

They were almost home, had turned into their neighborhood, would be in the car for at most another minute or so—

“We love you, Conor,” his father said quietly. “We try pretty hard not to make that your problem. But—”

He shrugged. “We’re not perfect, either,” he said. “Hopefully, we’ve built up enough credit that you’ll be willing to forgive us what happens next. But if not—”

He looked up into the rear-view mirror, his eyes seeking Conor’s.

Conor had no choice but to let his eyes be found.

“Well. I think we’d both rather have you alive and furious with us, than to stand over your grave and wonder whether we should have done something differently. If that sounds, I don’t know, extreme…so was waking up at five in the morning to find out that our son was in the emergency room, when we would have sworn that he was safe and sound just down the hall.”

Conor was crying again, but he nodded.

•          •          •

He slept for eight hours.

•          •          •

Conor’s father knocked softly on the door frame. The door was open—had not been allowed to be closed.

“You awake, bud?”

“Yes.”

“You’ve got a visitor.”

Conor sat up in bed.

His father was alone in the doorway.

“Waiting in the living room,” he explained. “I was trying to decide between sending him in here and letting you have your privacy, versus making you talk out there where I can hear. I’m not happy to even have to ask myself that question, and I’m even less happy to find myself seriously considering the fascist option.”

His mouth twisted, and Conor’s heart twisted with it.

“I’ve never been a parent to an almost-teenager before,” he continued, his voice quiet, looking very, very tired. “I don’t know how this works. Can I just…ask you not to do anything in this conversation that you know would make me sad? Is that—I don’t know. Is that the sort of promise you would keep?”

Something inside of Conor tore, then—just a little, but he felt it.

“Yes,” he whispered.

I’m sorry, Dad, I didn’t mean it, I didn’t do it on purpose, I was trying to do something good—

“All right, then. Dear Conor, you have a visitor. I’ll send him in. Please don’t do anything that you know would make me sad.”

And then his father vanished from the doorway.

He didn’t actually make you promise.

Which was clearly not an accident, given that it was Conor’s father who had taught him to pay attention to things like that. He’d left out the binding part intentionally, left Conor with freedom of motion—

The freedom to hurt him, on purpose, if I want to.

(The tear inside of him widened.)

And then Timothy Falhanner walked into the room, carrying a small stack of books with a bright red folder on top, and on top of that, Conor’s phone.

The two boys looked at each other for a long, long moment.

“I told your parents Ashleigh went back and found it,” Timothy said.

But that wasn’t what had happened, was not what had really happened, Timothy had gotten the phone back from Devon, somehow—

“You want to tell me what the fuck is going on?” Timothy asked, his voice like knives on sandpaper.

“Where did you—”

“You obviously know where. Don’t jerk me around.”

Conor squeezed his eyes shut.

“I can still see you even if you can’t see me, you know.”

You’ve been letting every single problem just slam into you, slam into your heart, and you haven’t been telling any of them that it’s not their turn…

“Conor—”

“No.”

“What?”

“No.”

“No, I can’t see you, or no, you’re not going to—”

“Just no.”

“What do you—”

“No, Timothy. No. I can’t. No. Stop. Stop. No. No. No. No. No. No. No.”

Conor fell silent, his breath heavy and fast, like the pumping of a bellows. He waited, taut, trembling, for the other boy to ignore him, to say something anyway, bracing himself for the snap, the shatter, it would come any second now—

Silence.

Slowly—so slowly—the tension began to ease, a thousand pounds becoming only nine hundred ninety-nine, then nine hundred ninety-eight. Conor’s breath began to slow, the sudden glasslike quality of his thoughts softening into something slightly less brittle.

Any second now.

But still there was silence, and before long there was room for thought, it was now the case that Conor only needed to send ninety percent of the available power to shields, and could spare the rest for other things.

And then eighty.

And then seventy.

Slowly, he dared to try to think, as if he might in fact get away with it—as if he had the time, the space, to reach the end of a thought without getting knocked over halfway, he wasn’t sure that he did have it, was not certain that the miracle of Timothy’s patience would hold. But he was now okay-enough that if it didn’t, he would only lose the thought itself, and not his whole mind.

Okay.

Okay.

Start at the beginning.

Devon had given Timothy the phone.

Devon had given Timothy the phone, and Timothy was asking what the fuck, which meant that Devon had not told Timothy what the fuck—

“What did he tell you?” Conor asked, his eyes still closed, his brain still sending more than half of its power to the task of merely holding itself together.

“Just that you’d want this back,” said Timothy’s voice, out of the darkness. “But—”

Conor took in a deep breath, let it out.

And another.

“But he also texted me. Last night. About two minutes before Ashleigh called.”

What.

“It said ‘you and your brother, auditorium roof, now, no police, one hundred each.’ I got up to wake up Ashleigh and he wasn’t in his room; I went down into the garage to see if his bike was still there and was just about to call him when he called me.”

“What did he mean, ‘one hundred each’?” Conor asked, pretty sure he already knew.

“Take a look.”

Conor opened his eyes.

Timothy was holding a fistful of twenties, fanned out like a hand of cards. Conor didn’t count, but there were about ten of them.

“Conor,” Timothy said—and in his voice there was a tone that Conor had heard before, that he knew intimately, but from the other side, from the inside—“how did Devon know?”

Conor didn’t answer.

“Conor, did Devon do this to you?”

Conor didn’t answer. Fast, it was happening too fast, if Conor gave the younger boy the answer he wanted he would do the wrong thing with it. Conor didn’t know what the right thing was, but he knew it was a narrow target, and he knew Timothy’s aim was poor.

“Conor—”

“Wait. Please.”

Again the miracle, the younger boy actually hearing, actually listening.

“Thank you,” Conor whispered, and then he tried to think.

Okay.

Devon.

(It was scary, turning his mind’s eye to look directly at the older boy. There was an animal terror writhing inside of him, now, a fear that squirmed and soured and twisted, that wanted only to run and hide.)

(But Conor was no longer an amateur, had spent the past seven months and change sparring, trading blows with his fear, learning how to cope. How to keep moving, keep his wits about him, keep his feet under him even as the fire alarm shrieked inside of his head.)

Devon…

…did not make sense.

The things that he had done did not make sense, taken together, and that meant—

That meant Conor was missing something.

He—

(“Told me” was not right, those were not the right words at all, but Conor didn’t have time to dig around for better ones.)

—told me not to tell anyone.

He had told Conor not to tell anyone, and then he had gone and texted Timothy, given the younger boy Conor’s phone, given him money—

Why?

Conor took another deep breath. There was a puzzle here, of a sort he was not very good at, but this one mattered, this one was important, and he had to try.

Devon had sent Timothy to save him.

(Had tried to send Timothy and Ashleigh, had not known that Ashleigh was right there, under the magnolia, just minutes away.)

And—

And he had been—not nice, but something, on the rooftop, with all those other figures looming. Had tried to—to—

Something.

Conor squeezed his eyes shut, trying to remember the details of the scene, what had happened, what had been said. And what floated to the top—

—what that other part of his mind wanted him to see—

—the part that sometimes knew things he did not—

—was that Devon was small.

Conor waited, trying not to scare the thought away.

In his memory, the shadowy figures stood next to each other, and Devon—

(Who was a high schooler, and not an especially small one.)

—was the smallest among them. The youngest among them, probably. Was definitely smaller and younger than the figure with the gun, the man with the gun, those had been the voice and manner of an adult, a gangster, a real actual criminal—

Oh.

…yeah, okay, that makes sense.

Devon had been in danger, too.

Not like Conor, not like fuckface mouth breather Snigs, but when Conor had said Devon’s name, he had responded like he was scared. Scared, and trying not to show it.

Scared, and trying to regain control.

The man with the gun had said something, something Conor couldn’t quite remember, something about it being Devon’s fault, Devon’s responsibility, about the situation being on Devon to sort out—

Yeah, because East Binder is Devon’s territory.

The whole chain of thought was tenuous, shaky. Conor wasn’t sure he trusted himself, was not sure that he believed his own beliefs. But he did find himself believing it, this story that his brain was weaving together. It fit, felt right, made sense.

And under those circumstances…

Conor opened his eyes.

Timothy was there, waiting, watching, his own eyes like flint arrowheads.

“It wasn’t Devon,” Conor said.

“Took you two whole minutes to come up with that one?”

“It wasn’t Devon,” Conor repeated.

“I don’t believe you.”

“It wasn’t,” Conor said, exasperated, and he realized as he spoke that the exasperation was real, that the words felt true, he knew they were technically a lie but it was just technically.

Devon had been caught on the rooftop every bit as much as Conor.

Hey, just asking, but like. Are you sure?

“Devon was there, but he tried to help,” Conor said. “Did help. Stopped it from being—it could’ve been a lot worse.”

Because this sure does sound crazy. Like literally actually crazy, like whadyacallit, Stockman syndrome.

“What happened, Conor?”

“I can’t tell you.”

“‘Can’t’?”

“Can’t.”

Because Timothy wasn’t—wasn’t ready, wouldn’t be able to handle it—

Yeah, see, this is the part that worries me.

Okay, fine, maybe Conor was wrong but that didn’t mean he should let Timothy go off half-cocked, which is exactly what would happen if he found out—

That Devon broke your fucking arm!

Conor closed his eyes again. Too soon, it was too soon for him to be doing this, to be trying to sort this all out, he couldn’t trust the things his brain was telling him, he needed time—

But he didn’t have it. Timothy was going to leave the room, one way or another.

So you’re just going to lie to him?

It wasn’t a lie. Devon had made it better.

Conor. This isn’t complicated. He BROKE your fucking ARM.

But that wasn’t right, was not the right way to see things, to frame things—Conor was not at all sure he’d found the right way to look at it, but he knew that way of looking at it was wrong. Because it was too late, had already been too late—from the moment they came up through the trapdoor, things were already fucked. There were no happy endings, at that point. The question hadn’t been how to get out of there in one piece, it had been how many pieces he would end up in, how bad it was going to be.

And if he really had to take at least one hit—

If Devon had stopped time, and let him choose whether to take a bat to the arm, or the leg, or the gut, or the head—

You could have talked your way out of it.

Bullshit.

Timothy cleared his throat.

“Timothy,” Conor said. “Do you trust me?”

“No.”

That’s fair.

“Can you just…wait? Like, even if you don’t believe me, can you at least act like—”

(Like you’ve got some sense.)

“—like you might be wrong? Like it could maybe be more complicated than you think, and there are reasons why I’m not just—just—”

He broke off, tried to find the end of the sentence, realized there was nothing more to add.

“Please?”

The look on Timothy’s face had no translation.

“You know,” the younger boy said, after the silence had stretched out for nearly a minute. “It wasn’t easy. What we did this morning. Some parts of it were really hard, actually.”

Conor didn’t know how he was supposed to respond.

“When I first got onto the shed—that jump, up to the awning. With the slanted takeoff, and like, nothing underneath. It’s fucking scary.”

Conor nodded slowly.

“And then the transfer from the tree to the pipe. Scary.”

“What’s—”

“But I kept thinking,” Timothy continued, talking over him. “What if somebody I cared about was hurt, on the other side? What if the only way to save them was to just do it, and every second I wasted dicking around being scared just made it worse?”

Something tightened in the center of Conor’s chest.

“You were right,” the younger boy said softly. “About the hole. When it’s real—when it matters—”

He shrugged. “It did end up feeling a whole lot smaller, somehow.”

He stood, fixing Conor with another inscrutable look.

“I won’t do anything for a week,” he said. “After that…I’m going to do what’s right.”

And then he was gone.

•          •          •

Conor slept again.

•          •          •

“Dad.”

“Mmm?”

“Is it okay if—I mean, am I allowed to use my phone?”

“Ah. Right. Hm. …give me a moment.”

Conor gave him a moment.

“All right, question,” his father said. “Is using your phone going to be good for you?”

“Huh?”

“Pause, and take three deep breaths, and think about how you plan to use your—no, wait, scratch that, think about how you actually expect to use your phone. What you think will actually happen, once you get on it.”

“Now?”

“Now.”

Conor took three deep breaths, and thought.

“Is that…good for you?” his father asked. “Will it hurt you? Is it making you more or less like the person you want to be?”

“More.”

“You’re sure?”

“Yeah.”

“You’re sure.”

“I…think so?”

His father studied him for a moment, and then slowly nodded.

“Okay,” he said. “I hope you’re right.”

•          •          •

There was a pile of notifications waiting for him, when he turned his phone back on. But the three that mattered were easy to find.

(They were all recent; Timothy must have spread the word that he’d given it back.)

I love you, Ashleigh had written. Call me when you can. If I don’t hear from you, I’ll be at your window at midnight.

And from Jennifer: I’m glad you’re okay. I have…a lot of questions. Ashleigh says you probably won’t be allowed to see him or Guyler, but he thinks your parents will still let us do math.

And from Guyler…

Guyler hadn’t sent any words. Just a picture.

It was a selfie, with only half of Guyler’s own face in the frame, his expression strangely open to interpretation, shifting from wry to sober to teasing to angry to disdainful as Conor studied it. And behind him…

(Conor counted.)

Behind him were gathered fifteen figures, lit by harsh industrial light.

Ashleigh, and Timothy. Holt, Sam, and Eddie. Isabella and Maddie, Jennifer and Julia, four sixth graders Conor didn’t know and one—Trevor—whose name he remembered only because Ashleigh had said it in the car.

They were clustered together—huddled, almost—arranged in a haphazard arc in the trampled grass, surrounded by ropes and tape and other garbage, the wall of the auditorium looming behind them. And at the center of the arc—

Conor couldn’t make out his own face, his own expression—would not have been certain it was even him, had he not been there.

But he had. Could remember the moment, the exact moment when a dozen different hands had slowly drawn back, waiting to catch him, seeing if he could stand on his own two feet.

And he had.

But not until they had saved him, first.
