The door to Conor’s bedroom creaked open, and he looked up to see his mother’s face filling the gap.

“Morning,” she said softly.

He nodded. It was Sunday, and the light streaming in through his window was young and clean, outlining everything in gold. Conor had woken up half an hour earlier and was sitting up in bed, his back against the headboard, his phone in his lap.

“I asked your father if we could have the morning,” his mother continued. “Just you and me.”

Conor said nothing.

“Would that be okay?” she asked.

It was the first she had spoken to him directly since Friday morning—at his father’s request, Conor was pretty sure—and her voice was gentle, and tentative, making the question feel real in a way her questions often didn’t.

•          •          •

An hour and fifteen minutes later, and they were squinting against the sunlight, bellies full as they emerged into the parking lot.

“I was wondering,” Conor’s mother said. “If you would maybe show me.”

“Show you?”

“Where it happened. Where you fell.”

They reached the car and separated, Conor’s mother heading right toward the driver’s side door, Conor going left toward the passenger’s. He waited for the click of the lock, slowly folded himself into the seat, pulling the door shut with his good right arm, awkwardly dragging the seatbelt across his chest and buckling it underneath his cast.

“Why?” he asked finally.

Partly to buy more time. Partly to cover his surprise—the sudden, secret scramble to think of a location, a plausible story, no one had actually asked and he somehow still hadn’t got around to sorting out the details with Ashleigh—

(Which, yes, incredibly stupid in retrospect but focusing on that now wasn’t going to help, come on, brain, we can beat ourselves up about this later—)

But also…

Why?

His mother gave a soft little shrug as she put the car in gear. “Feels…overdue, I guess,” she said. “You’ve been doing this…whatever it is…since what, April?”

Conor nodded.

“I’ve seen you jumping around in the backyard. And over next to Mr. Baker’s house. But there’s more to it than that, isn’t there?”

He nodded again.

“Well,” she said. “It just…feels like I should’ve asked, already. To see.”

Conor looked down at the sling cradling his arm. “I can’t show you anything.”

“I know. Or rather, not true—I know you can’t do anything. But you can show me, right? Point and describe?”

Conor looked over at his mother. She didn’t look back, kept her eyes on the road, but she was clearly aware that he was looking. She seemed—

Tense?

No, not quite. Conor wasn’t sure what she seemed, only that she seemed something. Was thrumming with some sort of barely-restrained energy, was waiting with bated breath.

Was waiting. Had, for the second time, asked what felt like a real question, instead of a demand in disguise.

Dad.

This was his father’s doing, somehow, or was at least downstream of his father’s influence. Which made the next few minutes uncomfortably difficult to predict, because he had no idea what his father had said to her, or how it would mix with her usual way of doing things. And now his mom-translator was going to be worse than useless, was going to confidently lead him off track…

“Left at the light,” he said quietly. “Freedom Plaza—you know it?”

She nodded, and some of the whatever-it-was seemed to ease.

Some of it.

•          •          •

“From here?” his mother asked, nudging the concrete pillar with her shoe.

Conor nodded. He had decided to take her to the place where Timothy had first broken the monkey jump—the place where he actually had fallen, on the day after Halloween, fallen and nearly broken his hip, his wrist. A part of him had winced, as they pulled into the parking lot, remembering that the police station was right there, but his mother hadn’t said anything, hadn’t seemed to notice, hadn’t asked him why he and Ashleigh and Guyler hadn’t just gone to the cops for help. She’d simply gotten out of the car, quietly following as he led her through the concrete jungle, describing various movements along the way—the vaults over the low wall, the switchovers and slipthroughs on the bars of the handicap ramps, the rebound off of the slanted surface of the fountain and the various targets to land on, after.

And now, to the monkey jump—the squat concrete pillars flanking the stairs, and the stark, featureless walls on either side.

“It’s so far,” she said, looking back and forth across the gap.

“You don’t like land on it land on it,” he said. “You do a sort of like—”

He gestured with his one good arm.

“But where do your feet go?”

“They sort of…kick into the wall?”

“Like your toes?”

He shook his head. “No, like—”

Again he demonstrated, this time stepping over to the wall and lifting up a leg, pressing the ball of his foot against the vertical surface. “Like if you were crawling up the stairs, or something,” he explained. “Only turned upright. Your toes point up like this. Sort of like the wall is the new floor.”

Again, his mother looked back and forth between the pillar and the wall. “And you didn’t make it?” she asked.

He nodded, thinking back to his fall, it was months ago but he remembered it clearly. “I came in too high,” he explained. “Too high, and leaning too far back, so I sort of bounced, and came down sideways—”

She looked down at the unforgiving concrete of the sidewalk.

There was a long silence.

“Help me up,” she said.

“Huh?”

“Onto the pillar. Help me up.”

“What?”

“Stand here, so I can put my hand on your shoulder.”

“Mom, what—”

“Come on, just do it.”

The words were light, cajoling, not commanding, and Conor felt himself moving forward in sheer cooperative reflex, presenting her with his uninjured shoulder even as his brain continued to protest, no, wait, seriously, what—

She was wearing sneakers, he realized, as she hauled herself up onto the pillar. Sneakers, and light workout clothes, he hadn’t noticed, hadn’t really registered it, but now, as she straightened, he felt his confusion turning rapidly into alarm.

“Mom, what are you—”

“Shh, let me focus.”

Thirty…nine? Was she thirty-nine? Conor was almost thirteen and when he had been born she had been…how old…he didn’t think she was forty but she wasn’t young…

“Mom—”

“Quiet, let me think.”

She had assumed what looked, to Conor’s eye, like a dangerously competent stance—feet together, knees ever so slightly bent, leaning forward like a wrestler.

No, no way, surely she isn’t—

But she did.

“Mom!” he shouted, his heart leaping into his throat. Only for a moment, though, because her jump was tiny, added barely more than a couple of inches to the height of the pillar, which was itself less than three feet tall. She landed—heavily, badly—just a couple of feet away, well shy of the looming wall, and grunted but seemed overall unhurt.

But still—!

“Mom, what the—”

And then:

Oh.

Right.

He could feel his cheeks burning as she straightened, turning to face him, just the barest hint of pain in the lines of her face. “Something the matter?” she asked, her tone artificially innocent.

Conor couldn’t speak for several seconds. “Mean,” he said eventually, although he somehow couldn’t manage to put much fire behind the words, maybe because—

“That feeling?” his mother said bluntly, skipping past the parts they both clearly already understood. “Multiply that by ten, when it’s your child. When it’s your only baby.”

“Mom, I’m—”

“Not yet,” she said, and her voice was not iron, was softer than iron, but it was still hard, unyielding, something with the feel of a glacier, maybe it was a glacier. “Not until I’ve said my piece.”

Conor’s mouth closed with a click.

“Your father has been full of advice, these past two days,” she said softly. “About how to approach you. About how I don’t listen enough. How I shut you down. And maybe he’s right. Maybe I need to do that less.”

Conor blinked. He’d never thought in that sort of detail about life after high school, after he moved out, and thinking about it now he honestly wasn’t sure if he was the kind of son who would call at all. Dad, maybe. But—

But how long has it been since you’ve, like. Wanted to talk to her about something?

She stepped closer, close enough that he was looking up at her, now—close enough for him to realize that he didn’t actually have to look up much, anymore—that he was taller, had been getting taller, was now only a few inches shorter than she was.

What is—

She reached out, took his free hand in hers. “So I’m not going to tell you what you have to do, this time,” she said. “I’m just going to try to get you to understand. I just want you to know.”

Her hand was warm, where it wrapped around his, and she stepped even closer, lifted her shirt a little—

(Squirming panic.)

—pressed his palm directly against her stomach. He wanted to run, to hide, to melt, but she held him in place, held him firm, reached down with her other hand and put a finger under his chin, gently lifting his face to where her eyes were waiting, dark and serious.

“Can you feel my heartbeat?” she asked quietly.

He nodded, because he could.

“Imagine it stopping.”

He flinched.

“There. That. That feeling. Look at me, Conor.”

He looked back up at her.

“There are things you will never know,” she said. “Things you’re not supposed to know, things that no one expects you to understand. But you should know that you don’t know them. You should know that you will never, ever know what it means to me, that I carried you, that I felt you growing inside of me, moving and kicking and coming alive—”

She squeezed her eyes shut, briefly, and when she opened them again, he could see that there were tears. Tears that hadn’t fallen, that were only maybe going to be tears, in the end. But he could see them, and he felt something in his chest lurch.

“When I opened my eyes, and I saw you for the very first time—Conor, there’s a piece of me that lives inside of you. I don’t mean your genes, I mean a piece of my own heart, when I gave birth to you, you took a part of me with you and I’ll never get it back. I don’t want it back. I mean—”

She broke off again. Conor’s mouth was dry, and getting drier.

“Right now, Conor, I am a whole, living person, because the part of me that you took with you lives in you. It’s alive because you’re alive. I get to be alive, because you’re alive. If something were to happen to you—”

She broke off a third time, and now the tears were falling, silent, unacknowledged. Her belly was hot and warm against his hand, hot and warm and soft and trembling, shaking, moving with every breath.

“I would live,” she said, and her voice had recovered a touch of its usual flatness, its usual distance. “But I wouldn’t be alive, Conor. Not in quite the same way. I know you’re maybe thinking, huh, this is a lot, this is an overreaction, all I did was break my arm—”

(That was not what Conor was thinking.)

“—but Ashleigh called us from the emergency room, Conor. At five in the morning. I’ve never had reason to second-guess the trust we put in you, the restrictions that we don’t put on you, as parents, but that morning—I know, he said you were fine, he said it right away, but I had so many thoughts, Conor, so many thoughts you don’t want to know about, I don’t ever want you to have to think thoughts like the ones I was thinking that morning, on the way over. But I want you—I need you to know that you matter to me.”

She let go of his hand, put both of hers on his shoulders. “You matter so, so much, Conor,” she said. “You cannot ever know how much. I sometimes—honestly, I try to hide it, most of the time, so you don’t feel—so you don’t have to—to carry it, to be burdened by it, I don’t want the way you matter to me to be something you have to—to deal with but I want you to understand. I want you to know that it’s a lot. It’s a lot, and when you—when you’re careless with yourself, when you’re reckless, it really isn’t just your own life you’re playing with, Conor, it’s mine, too, mine and your father’s, that’s maybe not fair, it’s maybe too much to put on a little boy but it’s true and anyway you’re not so little anymore.”

Conor was crying too, now, the tears carving their way down his cheeks. “I’m sorry,” he whispered, and of course she didn’t understand, she didn’t know what he was sorry for, because he hadn’t told her, could not tell her—

His mother shook her head. “No,” she said simply. “No, Conor, that’s not what this is about. This is about what happens next. This is about you remembering that you carry me around in your pocket, me and your father both, and we’re breakable, Conor. We’re no more invincible than you are.”

One hand slid down his shoulder, tracing a finger along the rough surface of his cast—

(Past the signature that Ashleigh had left on it, when he had visited Friday night, illegally, in defiance of Conor’s grounding. He had asked, first—asked if he could—and Conor had sighed, and allowed it, feeling it was owed, somehow.)

(His mother gave no comment, though she could not possibly have missed it.)

“This isn’t that bad,” she said. “By itself. A broken arm. You could’ve gotten a broken arm on the soccer field. But the fact that you got a broken arm out here, at four in the morning, with no grownups around—”

She gave a heavy sigh. “I don’t know what to do, Conor. I’m well aware of the limits of what I can bully or threaten you into. I know that you snuck out because you knew that if you’d asked, we would have said absolutely not. But the thing is—the thing is, there’s a reason we would have said no, and it’s not just about being old and tired and lame, it’s about the fact that we want to see you make it, Conor. We want to see you make it to adulthood, a happy and healthy adulthood, and—and this sort of thing is a bad sign, Conor, think about how furious you would be with me if I locked you down for the next three years and then realize that I know that, I get it, I am one hundred percent aware that you would never speak to me again and I’m tempted to do it anyway. Does that—do you understand? I’m trying to do this the way your father wants me to, and—can you feel how serious this is? This—”

She tapped at his cast.

“This is a warning. A chance to stop and reflect. Like your father’s street sign.”

Conor swallowed. It was a part of the legendarium of their family, the sort of story that got told over and over again at holidays, at family reunions—that his father had started stealing things when he was fourteen, started a collection of the little rectangular signs outside the movie theaters in the multiplex, sliding them out and rolling them up and hiding them in his socks. He’d collected thirty-two of them over the course of a year, and then gotten bolder, decided to sneak out one night and steal a street sign—Random Lane—and been caught by the police at two in the morning.

Caught, and scared straight.

Conor understood, he got it, he knew what his mother was trying to tell him, had had many of the exact same thoughts over the past three days—

What do you think? the man with the gun had asked. Throw him off the fuckin’ roof?

Probably he hadn’t meant it. In the cold light of day…probably it had just been a show, an act between him and Devon, both of them trying to scare him.

But it had worked, Conor had been scared. Was still scared—could feel himself retracting, withdrawing, withering, if his father had asked he would have said like a turtle, there was a part of him that was ready to promise his mother that he would never leave her sight again, that he was done with sneaking out, done with breaking rules, that he had learned his lesson and would never again do anything that Holt or Jennifer wouldn’t do because the lines on the road might just be paint but they did keep cars from crashing into one another, mostly—

But Holt and Jennifer came.

That was the thing. Holt and Jennifer had both been there, in the dark and confusion—had deemed it an acceptable risk, had not refused to answer Ashleigh’s call.

Confused, he was confused again, everything his mother was saying was right, he should agree with it, but there was something in him that was refusing to let go, some other part that was standing its ground as the rest of him rushed inward.

The mass of men lead lives of quiet desperation.

Ms. Palmano had quoted them that, and had caught Conor’s eye, at the time. Ms. Palmano, who had—had sacrificed herself, what she’d done was something she wasn’t supposed to do but she’d done it anyway…

Conor could see it, in his mother’s eyes—could see that she could see, that she had seen the struggle taking place inside him, that she knew a part of him was saying no.

“Conor—”

“Wait,” he said, cutting her off. “Please? There’s—”

He cast about.

“Can you just be Dad, for a second? I think I could figure out how to say it, to Dad.”

That…hit harder than he had intended, he winced in sympathy as he saw the words land, but whatever, don’t waste it, focus—

Seconds passed.

“Ms. Palmano,” he began—

—and then broke off, because something about that was wrong, both he and his mother had frowned at the same time, their eyes narrowing, because what did it matter what some teacher had to say, what mattered was what he believed, and who cared where it had come from originally?

“I don’t want to die,” he said slowly. “I really don’t. I really don’t.”

(He hadn’t actually known that, and it was good to hear the words as they emerged, and feel that they were true.)

“And I don’t think that I, um.”

He spared a glance at his mother’s eyes, which had the feel of a ticking clock.

“I don’t think that Thursday was—smart. I don’t think I, uh. Made good choices.”

Because he hadn’t, it wasn’t like he could have predicted gangsters on the rooftop but his reasons for being there in the first place had not been good, he could see that now. He had been flailing, trying to make himself feel better, and there were better ways, less costly ways, the things he had said to Ashleigh about wanting to clear away the fog of war had in fact been excuses, rationalizations—

Focus.

He looked down at his arm. The doctors had told him it would be a month before it could come out of the cast, and at least two before he would be able to start using it normally again.

(At least two, and more likely three. He would be thirteen before he would be able to start working up to the monkey jump again, and it would take weeks to regain the strength and stamina he would lose in the meantime.)

“But—”

Take control of your life, and spend it on something, because you can’t take it with you when you die.

That’s what Ms. Palmano had told them, all the way back at the beginning of the year, and the words had crawled inside of Conor’s soul and taken up residence—had already been there, unspoken, and just been waiting for someone to give voice to them. There was a truth, there, and it was that truth that he was reaching for, that he could not walk away from, and some part of him wanted to be honest about it, wanted his mother to actually understand—

Holt and Jennifer came.

They had come, even though they were good kids, even though they weren’t sneak-out-at-night-type Pokémon, they had come because Conor had needed them, because it had been serious, and they hadn’t just said no, are you crazy, call in the grownups.

And neither had Maddie or Julia.

And maybe they were all compromised, Conor could imagine what his mother would say if he tried to lean on the judgment of a couple of teenagers, but—

But it wasn’t the same mistake. If it was a mistake at all. Conor felt embarrassed about having gotten them all into the situation, but he didn’t feel embarrassed about what they’d done to get him out of it. If their roles had been reversed—if it had been Ashleigh, or Guyler, or Jennifer, or Holt—

I would’ve gone.

And that felt right, and it still felt right, broken arm and all. If he came out of this conversation agreeing not to go, in the future, then that would mean something had gone horribly, horribly wrong—

(His mother was still watching, still waiting, her patience visibly finite but she was trying, Conor felt a rush of gratitude, no, later, stay focused god dammit.)

“You’re saying,” he said slowly. “That I should think about you and dad. That if something happens to me, something happens to all three of us.”

He looked up.

His mother nodded.

He took a deep breath.

“But if something happens to all three of us—I mean, there are situations where—it’s not like it’s never worth—”

He faltered, and for the first time noticed how odd it was that he was trying to have the conversation at all—that instead of treating his mother as a constraint, an obstacle, a force of nature, he was in fact hoping that she would hear him. Hoping that he could make himself heard. As if she were another kid, and not a distant, unreasonable grownup.

“What if there was a fire?” he asked, abruptly changing tracks.

(Because that’s what he’d said to Timothy, that had been the thing that had finally opened the younger boy’s eyes.)

His mother frowned, but she seemed to follow, seemed to understand, because she said—

“Then you wait for the firefighters.”

Conor shook his head. “What if there aren’t any firefighters?” he argued. “What if they won’t get there in time? And you know it?”

“Conor—”

“Mom.”

She stopped. Barely, but she did stop.

“I want—if you were Dad, I’d say something like—there’s a thing I’m trying to tell you. There’s a thing that’s true, and—and I’m trying to put it into words, and—and I’m not trying to fight you. If you fight me, you’re not going to make the thing stop being true, you’re just going to stop me from telling you it.”

The silence that followed was like the aftermath of an explosion. Even though Conor hadn’t raised his voice, hadn’t shouted or cussed or waved his hands. It was the silence of his mother realizing the same thing he had realized himself, thirty seconds earlier—that for maybe the first time in his life, he was actually trying to communicate with her.

Again, he cast about, looking for the words—

“You know Alexander the Great?” he asked. “And—and Joan of Arc?”

(They were examples Ms. Palmano had given them, people she had talked about at length.)

“I know they were sixteen,” his mother said. “If that’s where you’re going with this.”

“Baldwin the Fourth, then.”

“…no, I don’t know who that is.”

“He was crowned king of Jerusalem when he was thirteen. Around, I can’t remember, the year one thousand, maybe? Like really king, not like King Tut—”

“Conor, you live in the suburbs.”

“People die in the suburbs. Cody died.”

“In a car accident, Conor, what is this?”

“Iqbal Masih,” Conor said, starting to feel desperate—because he was running out of names, there were only so many names he could remember, Ms. Palmano had mentioned Louis Braille and Blaise Pascal and Magnus Carlsen but none of them had died, none of them had been heroes, and besides, he couldn’t remember the arguments, the way she had said it—

His mother gave a heavy sigh. “Who is Iqbal Masih?” she asked.

“He’s a boy who was a slave,” Conor said. “He was a slave, and then he escaped, and then he became, like, a whatdyoucallit, a symbol, like he spoke out and stuff and then they killed him. This was recent, like probably you and Dad were already alive, they killed him when he was twelve. Assassinated him.”

“And you’re telling me about him why?”

“Because—because he was right. Because it wasn’t good for him to try to—to stay safe. Because there was something more important than that.”

Because I want to be a hero, Mom.

Because he still wanted to be a hero, because he could not stand the thought of doing what his mother and father did every day, he didn’t know how, he still hadn’t figured it out, but if he had the chance to save five people and it meant he might die, himself—

He couldn’t say it.

(And he didn’t know that he would do it, would go through with it. Didn’t actually know.)

But he hoped that he would.

And he hoped his mother would understand.

Her eyes were closed, now, her fingers pinching her forehead at the bridge of her nose. “Conor, the whole point of living in a place like this is so you don’t have to think about stuff like that. So you can have a childhood, and save those things for later. We’re trying to raise you in an environment where you don’t have to sacrifice your life to end child slavery or whatever.”

“But if it comes up.”

“It’s not going to come—”

“Mom. I’m trying to tell you something.”

She closed her mouth, opened her eyes, crossed her arms. “I’m trying to tell you something, too,” she bristled.

Another silence.

“Okay, that’s fair,” he acknowledged, once more feeling like the whole conversation was crazy, he’d never had a conversation like this with her, nor even with his father—

“Have you heard me?” she asked.

He paused.

“I don’t know,” he said.

And then—

“Have you heard me?”

“No,” she answered. “I think probably not.”

The two of them looked at each other for a long moment.

“You’re saying you don’t want me to be stupid,” Conor ventured. “You don’t want me to be stupid, and reckless, because—because I might get hurt, or I might die, and you’re worried about me getting hurt or dying for, like. No reason. Just because I didn’t—didn’t think, and did something dumb.”

He waited.

“Close enough,” she said.

“What’d I miss?” he asked.

“The part where, if I don’t think you got the message—and I mean really got the message, Conor—then I’m going to do whatever I have to do to protect you from yourself, and if that means you never speak to me again after you graduate high school then so be it. This is your street sign moment.”

The words were diamond, without an inch of give or bend, and Conor swallowed.

“Okay,” he said. “I heard you.”

And some instinct told him to stop there, to leave it at that, that if he were to try to explain his own side any further it would be misunderstood, would be taken as defiance and would land him in even deeper trouble—

“And what I think you’re trying to say to me,” his mother said—

(Impossibly, miraculously.)

“—is that you don’t agree that keeping yourself safe is the absolute highest priority.”

He stared.

“I think you’re trying to tell me that there are some circumstances where it’s worth taking a risk of getting hurt, or dying, if it means doing some other, greater good in the world.”

He nodded.

(Feeling almost dizzy, because she had heard him, and it was only now that he realized how little hope he’d had.)

“Conor,” she said, her voice turning grim. “You’re not wrong. I’d be lying to you if I said I’d ever be happy to hear that you threw yourself on a grenade, but I’d get it.”

He felt his jaw drop.

“But the thing you need to understand, Conor, is that I do not trust you to make those calls correctly. You are twelve going on thirteen, and you very recently demonstrated a spectacular lack of judgment, and it remains to be seen whether you actually know how to do better.”
