Conor held the little golden book as if it were made of porcelain, gossamer—looked at it as if it were a venomous snake.

He took a deep breath.

The last written page was not far in. More than three quarters of the book was still blank. Conor had asked his father to go retrieve it from his locker, to have whatever grownup conversations it would take to get through the police cordon, to get it and bring it here to him, so that he could read the last thing that Guyler had written to him, and write a proper response.

But now that it was here, he was finding it curiously hard to focus, was able to take in the words only a few at a time before losing the thread, Guyler’s jagged letters blurring into tangles of thorns. Something about pinball, and tryouts for the spring soccer season, and plans for the upcoming weekend which were not going to happen, now.

Conor picked up his pen.

I’m sorry, he wrote, and then paused, the copy of the other boy that lived in his head peering over his shoulder, eyes narrowed—sorry for what?

And some of the classrooms in the third and fourth buildings had been empty by the time Oliver reached them, and in others he had simply sprayed bullets for a few seconds and then left. Conor didn’t know yet—would maybe never know—whether the other boy had had…had a list, a reason, some sort of plan that meant focusing on the people in the math and civics buildings particularly, or whether it had simply…happened that way.

There had been a titanic struggle inside of Conor’s mind, Conor’s soul, during the first of those minutes, there in the bathroom, one of the loudest inner voices he’d ever heard screaming at him to shoot him shoot him just SHOOT him Ashleigh is over there DYING shoot him in the face RIGHT NOW and be DONE with it—

And Conor had come very, very close.

Had come very close, and wasn’t entirely sure what had stopped him, actually. Wasn’t sure whether it had been a good thing, the right thing, whether he had in fact made the right call. His shoulder people were confused; it was just as easy to summon a Ms. Palmano who approved as it was to summon one who didn’t, and the same was true for Ashleigh, for Guyler, even (shockingly) for Timothy.

Maybe it was just rule four.

Not the concrete framing of it, the words he had invented and polished and rehearsed over and over again, but the thing underneath—the thing the words were pointing to. The visceral recognition of a threshold that could not be uncrossed, and the seriousness with which he had learned to take such things.

(And the critical awareness that he was allowed to not do things, too.)

(On the rooftop, with Devon, hesitating had cost him his arm. But the lesson to take from that wasn’t quite “never hesitate.”)

In the end, Conor had simply backed over Ashleigh’s unconscious body, put both his best friend and the fallen shooter in his line of sight, put the gun down on the floor between them, in easy reach, and done what he could for Ashleigh, one-handed, in brief, frantic bursts, eyes and ears alert for the slightest sound, the slightest movement.

(Oliver had remained unconscious the entire time, which Conor had vaguely gathered was pretty bad, and was not particularly upset by.)

Conor’s mother was waiting for him in the hall, as he knew she would be. She didn’t speak, and neither did he. She just wrapped him up in a hug—

(Another hug.)

—wrapped him and squeezed him and held him for a long moment, silent, the breath from her nose hot upon the top of his head.

He wasn’t impatient. It wasn’t cloying or annoying, wasn’t embarrassing or too-much. Her arms, wrapped around him, were settling, rather than unsettling, and Conor waited within his mother’s embrace with something like gratitude.

But at the same time, he was waiting. The hug—

(Especially this hug, this sixth or seventh or eighth long, lingering hug.)

—was more for her than for him, even as it did in fact restore something, give him something, shore up something within him that was broken and wobbling. Conor was mostly being there for her, and he did so willingly, because he hadn’t been there for her, earlier.

Conor’s eyes jerked toward the other boy (and Timothy’s, too), but Eddie was still facing away from them, still staring at Ashleigh, did not turn to look and said nothing further.

“Sure,” he said. “Fine. Fuck it. Whatever.”

And Conor:

“Thanks.”

His father turned and led the way, weaving carefully through the crowded hallway, guiding them toward the elevator. They went up to the third floor, then crossed the pedestrian bridge to the parking structure, emerging on the top level, under the sun and the sky. It was maybe five o’clock, and the light was just beginning to turn golden, a hint of chill on the wind coming over the mountains.

The change of scenery was stark, and for a moment it unmoored him. He floated on the rooftop, directionless, found himself drifting toward the edge as if slipping down a snowbank, his eyes drawn to the flat shape of the auditorium roof, just barely visible over the trees, miles and miles away—

“How are you feeling?” his father asked.

“Fine,” Conor said absently (and then winced, because right, it was his father asking, which meant that—)

“What are you feeling?” his father asked.

Conor sighed, closed his eyes, leaned against the railing at the edge of the parking garage and bowed his head, listening.

“It feels like I’m shaking,” he said quietly.

(Like the buzz of hair clippers, or the wobble of a seismograph, as if his blood itself had been electrified.)

“You are,” his father confirmed. “What’s the shaking like?”

Conor thought.

“Too much,” he said. “Like it—like it doesn’t have anywhere to go.”

Bell-struck.

He remembered the thought, from the hallway outside the math classroom, the place where he’d seen the things he wasn’t thinking about.

There was a silence.

“How do you feel about that?” his father prompted quietly.

“What?”

“How do you feel about the fact that you’re shaking, and the shaking feels like too much, like there’s something inside you that has nowhere to go?”

Conor felt his brow furrow. It…made sense…?

Sense-making isn’t quite a feeling, said the copy of his father in his head. Not quite.

The three of them stole out of the living room in their socks, silent, sneaking. They pulled on their shoes under the stars, breath misting in the moonlight. Guyler and Ashleigh hadn’t brought coats, hadn’t brought hoodies, were wearing Conor’s extras. They were carrying their phones, and there was a note on the kitchen table in case Conor’s parents woke up.

“Where are we going?” Ashleigh had asked.

“Out,” Conor had answered. “Dunno.”

They set off slowly down the road, fading in and out of shadow as they passed beneath the streetlamps, talking in low voices—

(Because that’s what you did, on a night mission. Even if the houses were fifty feet away, at the ends of their driveways. Even if no one would hear you, if you talked normally. It just wouldn’t be right.)

—about life, about school, about turning thirteen. About Julia, and Devon, and Mr. Sykes. And also about ninjas, and Magic, and dry ice bombs (which Guyler had never heard of).

“So,” Conor murmured, as they exited the cradle of the neighborhood, climbing up and out into the city proper. “Eddie.”

Ashleigh was quiet for a long moment. “He’s nice to me,” he said finally.

“Are you nice to him?”

Another silence. Beside them, Guyler said nothing, his hands in his pockets, shoes scuffing.

“He knows he’s not you,” Ashleigh said. “I think…I don’t know…”

He shrugged. “He says he’s okay with it. I’m…trying to trust him.”

They crossed over the bridge, over the highway, the lanes below quiet and empty.

“Ms. Palmano thinks I could be a writer,” Conor said.

Guyler snorted. “No wonder they fired her.”

Conor punched him on the shoulder.

The three of them wandered for miles, following the bypass around the border of Binder, dodging into the bushes to hide from headlights. They took it easy, conscious of Conor’s arm, of Guyler’s leg, of the still-healing holes in Ashleigh’s side.

“I wish Jared was at East Binder,” Guyler said quietly.

And then:

“I mean, not really. It’s a shithole. But.”

Ashleigh and Conor exchanged glances.

On the far side of town they discovered a railroad trestle, all steel beams and triangles, crossing over the road. The side of it was a steep slant, maybe seventy degrees, a wide rusted I-beam of metal studded with rivets.

“Bet we can,” said Conor.

Ten minutes later they were on top, sitting cross-legged on the cold metal, thirty feet up over the tracks and thirty more over the asphalt below.

“Think a train’ll come?” Ashleigh wondered.

“If we sit here long enough,” said Guyler, dryly.

They stayed there for an hour, long enough for the stars to shift in the sky, talking and talking and talking. Guyler and Ashleigh were prickly at times, the shape of their new whatever-it-was still settling. Half the time, Conor tried translating, and the other half—

“It’s like he thinks we’re made of glass, or something.”

“Yeah, better wrap us up in bubble wrap and make sure we’ve got helmets and kneepads.”

“You guuyyys,” Conor groaned. “It’s my birthday.”

“Not anymore,” Guyler pointed out.

Eventually, they grew cold, climbed carefully down, began the long, slow walk home, still talking, and talking, and talking. They didn’t get anywhere—no solid conclusions, no new revelations.

(It wasn’t that kind of conversation.)

But their wandering took them new places, left them thinking new thoughts, and by the time they turned into Conor’s driveway, aching and tired, things felt different.

A little.
