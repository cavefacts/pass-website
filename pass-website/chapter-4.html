He turned on the shower as hot as it would go and leaned against the tile, watching the dirt and dried blood pooling at his feet, slowly draining away. Bit by bit, the heat leached its way into his joints and muscles, loosening him up until he was able to reach for the soap without wincing.

The younger boy gave another empty, humorless laugh. “Oh, yeah, Ashleigh’s big fight scene. The one where he didn’t stop me from getting roughed up or having all my books dumped on the ground, but he did show up in time to keep you from getting pounded.”

Conor frowned. That didn’t exactly seem fair…

“And then the next day, everybody’s talking about him like he’s some big hero, even though he threw one weak punch—not to mention that you’re the one who actually stepped up and put the whole thing on pause. Meanwhile, I’m stuck being that stupid little sixth grader who can’t take care of himself.”

“Come on,” Conor objected. “You can’t pin that on Ashleigh. It’s not like that’s the reason he stepped in.”

“Of course that’s why he stepped in,” Timothy shot back. “That’s the only reason he ever does anything, is to make himself look good, or show off. Or to impress you—though I guess that’s over, now.”

“So you’d rather take a beating than let your brother help you out?”

“I already said, didn’t I? I don’t want protection.”

You could’ve used some today, Conor thought. But he held his tongue.

“Then what do you want?” he asked carefully. “You can’t blame him for not taking care of you, and then get pissed when he does.”

“It’s not about whether he’s looking out for me. It’s about the fact that he decides one way or the other based on what’s convenient for him. He doesn’t care about what happens to me—he doesn’t give a shit about what happens to anybody. He only sticks his neck out if he’s getting something out of it.”

Conor shook his head. It was as if they were talking about two entirely different people. It wasn’t much of a stretch to accept that Ashleigh was a shitty older brother (Conor had seen them together, after all), but even off the top of his head, he could think of half a dozen occasions in which his friend had put his freedom or his reputation on the line without hesitation—taking the fall for this or that bit of mischief, making a fool of himself for the sake of solidarity, or to provide a distraction, or simply to make Conor laugh. There was the time in fourth grade when Conor had accidentally shattered the decorative clay turkey in the cafeteria, or the time when he’d yelled at the counselor at summer camp, or the time when Ashleigh had literally pissed himself to stop people laughing at Conor over the apple juice he’d spilled—

“Yeah, he’ll do that stuff for you. You’re his friend. I’m just his brother.”

Conor had no answer to that.

“Besides—what about the past two weeks? Hasn’t gone out of his way to help you much lately, has he? Doesn’t matter what you’re going through. I’m telling you, this is what he’s like with everybody. You just…aren’t immune anymore, for some reason.”

There was too much truth in that to ignore, and Conor’s brow furrowed as he fell silent, considering.

Was Ashleigh…bad?

Conor had never had to wonder, before.

The carelessness that Timothy was describing…Ashleigh had always been the kind of kid who gave no fucks. He was notorious for it. In many ways, it was his superpower.

But was that a good thing?

There was a word for people who didn’t care about anyone. Guyler had used it more than once. But Ashleigh wasn’t actually a psychopath—

Yeah, he’ll do that stuff for you. You’re his friend.

Had Conor been giving him a pass, just because he was nice to Conor?

Once more, he thought back to their adventures together—the scrapes they’d gotten into, the rules they’d bent or outright broken, the lies that they’d told together—some for their own protection, some as a means to an end, some just to see if they would be believed.

None of that seemed bad. Mean, maybe, some of it.

(But nobody was nice all the time.)

Selfish and immature, maybe, but come on, that was normal, they were kids, everybody was selfish and immature sometimes.

No, there was just one thing that Conor could not explain away, one act so monumental and terrible that it seemed beyond all reasonable defense:

Guyler.

There was no question that Timothy would point to that as evidence of moral bankruptcy. Guyler himself certainly thought so. And standing in the courtyard, looking at the aftermath still splashed across the other boy’s chest, Conor had found it hard to disagree.

But there was still—still!—the unanswered question of why. Surely that had to figure into any final judgment—didn’t it? Everything had its context.

That’s why you’ve been eyeballing me? You’re trying to figure out a way this is all my fault?

No. Not Guyler’s fault, and not right, not a good thing, but there had to be some explanation that would cause it to fall short of outright evil. Something that would make it understandable. Forgivable, even.

…right?

Not to dig myself a hole, here, but you really sound like you’re trying to talk yourself into something.

Yeah. He was. And it was getting harder and harder to do so, every time Ashleigh passed up another opportunity to defend himself.

“Maybe you’re right,” Conor said, breaking the silence.

The younger boy glanced at him, saying nothing.

“Maybe he is just a self-centered jerk. But if so, he’s not the only one.”

Conor thought about the other kids in his grade—most of whom he’d known for years, none of whom seemed even half as real, half as alive.

“…and I’d still rather be friends with him than with anybody else.”

“Oh, were we talking about you?” Timothy asked sarcastically, though without much heat. “In that case, problem solved. I guess I’ll just get over myself—not like I can go swap him out for a new brother anyway.”

“Sorry,” Conor said, feeling slightly sheepish.

Not the only self-centered jerk, indeed.

“Never mind,” Timothy said. He sighed. “It’s not like any of this is new. I should be used to it, by now. It’s just—East Binder is nothing like Littleton, you know?”

Conor knew.

“And—and whatever, I can handle it, but like, you have a big brother, you kind of expect you’re going to have a leg up on the whole situation. Friends in high places and all that. Just once, it would be nice if Ashleigh pulled his head out of his own ass long enough to make my life just a little bit easier. I’m not saying he owes it to me. I’m just saying it’d be nice, is all.”

Conor had no reply, and Timothy didn’t seem to expect one. They covered the last quarter mile to the Falhanner house in silence, each lost in his own thoughts.

“Well, anyway,” Timothy said, as they reached the foot of his driveway. “Thanks.”

“No problem,” Conor replied. “Anytime.” He winced. “I mean—you know.”

“Yeah.” Timothy looked up at the house, at the black garbage bag taped over the broken window. “He’s coming back tomorrow, you know.”

“I know.”

“You got, like…a plan?”

Conor shook his head.

Timothy’s lip twisted sympathetically. “Well, either way,” he said. “If you want to—you know, after—I mean, I wouldn’t mind jumping around some more.”

Conor nodded. “Definitely,” he said, doing his best to inject confidence into the words. “See you tomorrow, Timothy.”

“G’night, Conor.”

And the younger boy disappeared into the house.

•••

It was six o’clock in the morning, and there were choices to be made.

Conor rolled out of bed, his body abuzz with a Christmas morning tingle of anticipation. He showered quickly, donning his uniform and slipping quietly into the kitchen, where he forced down a pair of frozen waffles and half a glass of chocolate milk, pouring the rest into the sink.

It was six fifteen, and he wheeled his bike out of the garage, pausing at the end of the driveway. In one direction lay Ashleigh’s house. In the other direction lay the school—the courtyard, the shed, the rooftop—a dozen places where Ashleigh might go, each tinged with its own special significance.

It was six thirty, and he was on the road, the morning fog whipping his cheeks bright red, his heart pounding as he pedaled as hard as he could. Six thirty-five, and he was chaining his bike up in front of the cafeteria, considering his next move.

It was a quarter to seven, and he was standing on the roof, looking out at Binder’s Gap. He paced back and forth, his thoughts moving in circles, the morning ahead playing itself out in his mind in a hundred different ways. He kicked out a second runway for the jump, heedless of the noise he was making.

It was five after seven, and he was sliding back down the pipe, bracing himself for the final drop, walking past the roots of the magnolia. It was seven twenty, and he was standing in the courtyard with the other early birds, shuffling and stamping as the buses began to arrive. It was seven forty-five, and he was frozen with trepidation as bus 134 pulled into the parking lot.

Jeez, man, chill out—it’s just me.

And it was. There, emerging from the bus, two hundred yards away but utterly unmistakable, was Ashleigh. He sauntered across the blacktop, bookbag slung over one shoulder, his neatly combed hair disarranging itself in the breeze.

Conor took a deep breath. This was the first test.

He stepped away from the nearest clump of students, positioning himself out in the open, directly in Ashleigh’s line of sight. If the other boy pretended not to notice him—

Ashleigh waved.

So startled was Conor that several seconds passed before he managed to raise a hand in answer, and even from a distance he could see the other boy’s smirk. Blushing, he waited as Ashleigh closed the distance, unrushed and untroubled. He was suddenly hyperconscious of his hands, hanging awkwardly at his sides, and he felt no small amount of relief when the other boy raised a fist for their traditional greeting.

“Batman,” Ashleigh said coolly, brushing his knuckles against Conor’s.

Conor felt his mind divide in two, one half dissolving in celebration, the other sharpening into a tense and wary vigilance. After fourteen days, sixteen hours, and nine minutes, Ashleigh had acknowledged his presence—smoothly, effortlessly, without even a hint of hesitation or unease. It was better than Conor had dared to hope, and worse than he’d steeled himself to expect—out of all the angles Ashleigh might have chosen, he’d settled on pretending like nothing was up.

“Hey, Ash,” Conor replied, matching the other boy’s tone exactly. Calm. Casual. Carefree.

“What’s our spot this year?”

“There isn’t one.” Conor gestured vaguely toward the library. “Holt and them usually chill over by the bushes, but there’s nowhere to sit.”

“Lame,” Ashleigh pronounced, dropping his bookbag and sitting down on top of it. “So, what’d I miss?”

Conor twitched. This wasn’t just carefree, it was careless, the other boy’s tone almost offensively nonchalant—

“A lot,” he said simply, settling in beside the other boy. He began sifting through the events of the previous two weeks, lifting out memories and shaping them into stories, filling Ashleigh in on rumor, suspicion, happenstance. As he talked, he studied his friend’s face, furtively, looking for signals and finding very few.

“—and there was a ton of crap going on with the new kids from Central, graffiti and bullying and stuff, but that’s quieted down a little. They kicked a bunch of people out in the first week, and Mr. Sykes is nailing anybody else he catches to the wall.”

“How good is he at catching people?”

Conor grinned in spite of himself. “Not very,” he admitted.

“You said we’ve got English and Activity together?”

Conor frowned. That was right, but he didn’t remember mentioning it. “Yeah,” he answered slowly. “First and sixth.”

“I’m in second lunch. You?”

“Third.”

“Dang. What’s your locker number?”

“Four four nine.”

“I’m four eighty. Bottom—that’s going to suck.” Ashleigh glanced around at the students in their immediate vicinity. “Wonder who’s on top.”

Conor shrugged, feeling more unsettled with each passing moment. The conversation between them was flowing too easily, was almost unnaturally smooth. Every time a promising pause appeared, Ashleigh filled it, leaving Conor no openings into which he might insert a statement of substance. When the bell rang, the other boy sprang lightly to his feet, holding out a hand; taking it, Conor was irresistibly reminded of a moment two weeks earlier—Ashleigh, lying flat on his stomach on the roof of the equipment shed, his arm dangling down as Conor leapt up to grab it. The grin on his face looked exactly the same as it had then, the intervening chaos having left no visible mark.

They set off for the doors together, Ashleigh drifting slightly ahead. Sixty more seconds, and their reunion would be over, a critical window closed.

Say something!

It was his own inner voice, Ashleigh’s having disappeared from his head the moment its owner stepped into view.

Like what? he shot back. “We need to talk”?

Well, don’t you?

Conor looked at the other faces in the crowd around him. Most were preoccupied, incurious, but every now and then he thought he caught a flicker of interest, of cool hostility—the legacy, perhaps, of Guyler’s speech. The other boy took no notice, head up and eyes forward, his attention never drifting to one side or the other.

He came to me, Conor thought.

Not to Holt, not to Nolan, not to fucking Eddie—Ashleigh had made a beeline straight for the place where Conor was waiting, had greeted him as a friend and ally, shown no desire to go anywhere else. He was back, and if he needed to pretend—if he needed this first day to feel normal—

Well, Conor could play along. It was little enough, compared to everything else.

We can always talk tomorrow.

It was eight o’clock in the morning, and Conor walked down the hallway toward his locker, his best friend Ashleigh Falhanner beside him.
