Conor stood alone in the empty, echoing hallway, feeling awkward, feeling anxious, feeling trapped. There had been a note waiting on his desk when he’d walked into first period—face down, his teacher’s crisp, neat handwriting instantly recognizable when he flipped it over:

Go out into the hall and wait; I’ll be there as soon as everyone is settled.

He was still holding it—had dropped his bag and slipped out through the flow of incoming students and only realized that he’d brought the little slip of paper with him after the tardy bell had rung. It was like a magnet, a black hole, heavy in his hand and in his mind—four separate times he had spaced out and snapped back into the moment to find that his eyes were fixed on it, his gaze hanging on the semicolon like a coat on a hook.

It had not occurred to him to wonder what Ms. Palmano wanted, or whether he was in trouble, or how long he would have to wait. He was anxious, but only vaguely, distantly. He was thinking, but the thoughts were fuzzy, formless, animal. For days, the loudest parts of his brain had been quiet, as if sleeping, or possibly on strike.

The sound of the door opening drew his attention, and he looked up in time to see Ms. Palmano slow as she stepped out into the hallway—like a fight scene shifting into bullet time, her teacher-briskness molting, melting into something softer, nearly half human. Her eyes met his, and they looked at each other for long enough that some other Conor, in some other mood, might have been confused, or even uncomfortable.

“What day is it?” she asked quietly, ending the moment.

Huh?

Conor felt his head rock back slightly as his brain began rummaging through its pockets.

“Wednesday,” he said finally, knowing that it was too late, that the answer had taken him far too long.

His teacher’s face didn’t change, though—just bobbed up and down in a gentle nod, as if he had answered exactly as she’d expected him to. “What do you have fifth period?”

He was quicker this time. “Math.”

(For a moment, he felt a whisper of confusion, a tiny flicker of curiosity, but before he could decide what to do about it, it faded.)

He nodded.

“Were you planning on paying attention in math today?”

Conor’s eyes narrowed ever so slightly. She was doing it again—the thing she’d done in the hallway of his house, the being-an-actual-person thing—and he had no script to follow.

“Probably not,” he admitted.

“Fifth is my planning period,” she said. “The room is empty. If you want, you can come by straight after fourth, and skip lunch. I’ll let Mrs. Atkins know. You’d have until 1:35, that’s over an hour.”

This time, Conor’s curiosity broke the surface. “To do what?” he asked.

This ominous pronouncement produced very little reaction in the place where Conor felt things. Very little in the way of conscious thought, either—it was the wordless, animal parts of him that raised their heads, and considered, and concluded that an hour and a half alone in Ms. Palmano’s room sounded just fine, actually, thanks.

“Okay,” he said.

She held his gaze for a moment, as if searching for something, and then nodded, slowly. “As for this period. Do you want to sit at your desk, or out here?”

Again a quiet weighing.

“Out here.”

She nodded a final time, and turned, and vanished back inside the classroom, leaving him alone in the hallway.

His body knew what to do—so long as he actually ran off the edge, it would all sort itself out.

He walked back to his starting position, turned to face the jump.

Hey, so—

He took a deep breath, and then another—rubbed his hands together and shook them loose, limbering up his wrists.

—if this doesn’t work out—

He stamped his feet, twisted them, grinding the ball of his shoe against the shingle, testing the grip—

—are you gonna be, like. Even madder about it? Because last time—

“Quiet,” he said aloud.

And then, silently:

I can do this.

And he could.

Which meant that he could do it right now, no more stalling.

Left, right, left, right, left, RIGHT, LEFT—

•••

“You look cheerful,” his mother commented, glancing over from the kitchen. “Good day at school?”

“It was fine,” Conor said lightly, dropping his bookbag on the kitchen table. “Stroganoff for dinner?”

“Your favorite.”

Go do something else altogether.

Thursday, Friday, Saturday, Sunday. Conor woke up with his alarm, ate frozen waffles while his mother made her morning coffee, rode along with her to school in what was starting to feel like the new normal. He ate lunch with Holt and Eddie and Nolan and Nicholas, partnered with Jennifer in Science for a project on Mars colonization, and spent the shrinking hours between school and sunset training. He could do the monkey jump with zero prep, now, and had landed it perfectly more than a dozen times.

There was a soccer game Saturday morning, and he twice passed the ball to Guyler without either of them trying to kill the other. Afterward, he invited Holt over to play, and the two of them spent the afternoon building LEGO spaceships, playing Smash Bros. In the evening, they streamed trash movies and had a pillow fight, capping things off with ice cream before Holt’s mother came to pick him up.

On Sunday, he again spent the day with his parents. From morning until noon he re-mulched the garden beds with his father, and then after lunch the three of them got in the car and drove the forty-five minutes to the Center for Science and Discovery, listening to Paul Simon’s Graceland on the way.

Conor had more fun than he was willing to admit with exhibits and activities he’d first seen in kindergarten, building arches and manipulating magnets and touching manta rays and launching tiny balls in the giant orbital moon mission simulator. When they got tired of the main floor, they went into the planetarium to catch a show about the meteor that killed the dinosaurs. On the way out, his mother bought him a small (and surprisingly heavy) tungsten sphere from the gift shop. Halfway home, they stopped at a sushi restaurant—

“Are you guys trying to bribe me?” Conor had asked, feeling almost shy.

“Trying to cheer you up,” his father had answered, and for a moment it had seemed like that might have ruined it, like the fragile bubble of peace might not survive direct acknowledgement.

But then the egg rolls arrived, and the conversation turned to Thanksgiving, and whether Conor’s aunt and cousins might come to visit. It had been fully dark by the time they made it back to the house, and Conor had gone out to the little wall around Mr. Baker’s air conditioning unit and done topovers until he felt like stopping, not bothering to count.

“Ride tomorrow?” his mother had asked as he came in from the cold, numb and filthy.

“Yeah,” he’d said.

Sometimes these things take time to settle.

He would take the bus again eventually, would make eye contact with Ashleigh in English at some point. But not yet. He hadn’t turned on his phone in days, had opened his computer but stayed away from any of the places where Ashleigh might have tried to reach him. He knew there was a chance the other boy hadn’t left any messages, and he did his best to brace himself for that, too—

You don’t have to know where you’re going. You can take things one step at a time.

Monday. Tuesday. Wednesday…

Thursday.

Conor was in Social Studies, half-asleep, and the chrrk of the intercom jerked him awake, sending a bolt of adrenaline down his spine.

“Ms. Nease, please send Conor Moreton to the office.”

There was the inevitable oooooo and the almost perfunctory knock it off as Conor stood, took the hall pass, stepped out into the empty corridor. He briefly considered panicking—decided that there wasn’t any trouble he might be in that would be made better by a sixty-second head start—blanked his mind and thought of nothing so his brain wouldn’t spiral trying to guess.

I’ll literally know in a minute. Relax.

His trip through the hallway was uneventful, although the graffiti looked very fresh and the student sitting a few feet away from it was focusing on their textbook suspiciously hard. Entering the office through the door facing the quad, he walked down the short passage to the front desk, where the secretary handed him a phone.

“Your mom,” she said, pointing him toward one of the plush chairs lined up against the wall.

(His shoulder Ashleigh snickered.)

He took the phone, walked over, sat down.

“Hello?”

“Conor,” said his mother’s voice, taut and sounding slightly stressed. “We’ve got a bit of a perfect storm situation. Your father got called in for a big pitch on short notice and I just found out I’ll be stuck in meetings until late. Neither of us will be home before eleven at the earliest.”

“Okay.”

“You’ve got soccer practice at six. I’ve arranged with Linda to drive you over—”

“Linda?”

“Linda Stevens. Guyler’s mother.”

“Mom.”

“—you’ll go home with them afterward, and I’ll pick you up from there—”

“Mom.”

“—do not mess with me, Conor, I have neither the time nor the patience.”

“Mom, I—”

Hate him, Conor was going to say, but the words caught in his throat, some inner censor not quite willing to let them pass.

His mother took no more notice than a tank running over a toy car. “She will feed you and you will do your homework—”

“Why can’t I just walk home?” he protested. “Or go with Holt?”

“I already tried Elena, Holt has an away game tonight. And I spent twenty minutes trying to turn up a babysitter.”

“I don’t need a—”

“Conor.”

There was something dangerous in her tone, and his jaw snapped shut.

You will go with Guyler after school. You will do your homework. You will be polite. I will not have to worry about you while I am in the middle of—”

She broke off, the sound of her voice suddenly muffled as she covered the phone, spoke to someone else on her end. Conor scrambled to find words, some argument that might pierce her armor—

“Say that you have heard me,” she said, suddenly back.

There was no way out. “I heard you,” he echoed.

“Tell me what you will do this evening.”

And there goes the loophole.

“I’ll go with Guyler after school,” he said tonelessly, why, how, it had all happened so fast. “I’ll ride with him to practice, then I’ll do my homework.”

“And you will be polite.”

“And I will be polite to Mrs. Stevens.”

“Conor.”

“Mom, you can’t seriously—”

“I can and you will, because I am getting off this phone in about twelve seconds. You will be polite, period. Say it.”

There has never been a slave who did not choose to be a slave…

“I’ll be polite.”

“Good. I love you. See you tonight.”

Its not my fault, Conor wrote.

Duh, Guyler wrote back. Even you aren’t THAT much of an asshole.

I have to be polite to you or I’ll get grounded.

Guyler took nearly ten minutes to return the note the second time—

(The little leatherbound journal was still at home, so it was just an ordinary piece of paper, folded up and passed along whenever Mrs. Atkins wasn’t looking.)

—and the look on his face when he’d read Conor’s second message had been one of genuine confusion.

Okay, I give up. Like, don’t get me wrong—I’ll happily take advantage of you having both hands tied behind your back. But seriously, why give me the heads up?

Conor’s own reply was short, and to the point:

Didn’t want you to think I was being nice.

Guyler’s next message was not in words. It included at least two penises, which was all Conor registered before crumpling up the paper and dropping it to the floor.

“Where are we going?”

“Yeah, we don’t actually have to talk.”

It was 3:45 PM. Guyler had been waiting at the flagpole, had turned and begun walking without a word as soon as Conor emerged from the double doors. Now the pair of them were headed up Edgewood Avenue, toward the cluster of shops and restaurants that everyone referred to as the new mall.

You could just kill him, Ashleigh suggested. No one would know except both of your moms.

Conor rolled his eyes.

The other boy was silent, staring straight ahead as he walked, ignoring Conor completely. He kept a brisk pace, as if he had somewhere to be, and Conor let himself fall half a step behind.

His original plan for the afternoon had been to head for the playground at the park near his house. He’d spotted a new target jump the last time he was there, and had been hoping to break it. It was challenging in that the takeoff was a rail perpendicular to the target, meaning that Conor would have to leap with one foot in front of the other.

But it would have to wait. There would be no training that afternoon—they had passed multiple tempting opportunities along their walk already, several of which seemed doable even with a heavy bookbag, but trying something with Guyler there just felt like asking for it.

Asked your mom for it last night.

Bored, listless, feeling slightly resentful—and knowing it wasn’t really fair—Conor began trying to guess where Guyler was taking them, half seriously and half ludicrously.

Flute lessons.

Strip club.

Grocery shopping.

Pottery class.

Dave’s Discount Furniture Warehouse.

They weren’t headed for the YMCA—that was downtown. So was the library. There was an arcade at the new mall, but somehow it was difficult to picture Guyler Stevens playing SkeeBall, trading tickets for gumballs and glowsticks…

I could just leave. Let him go, go off on my own, meet up with him at like 5:45.

Except that, with the way his mother had sounded on the phone, he wouldn’t put it past her to call him at like 4:30 and demand that he put Guyler on the phone.

I can’t believe she did this to me.

Holt really did have an away game—Conor had checked, at lunch. And it wasn’t that crazy that they didn’t want to leave him home alone until eleven o’clock. A little bit insulting, but the normal kind of insulting, the kind Conor had learned to take in stride.

But Guyler fucking Stevens, of all people…

He had tried to figure out her angle, and the best he’d been able to come up with was that she honestly thought there was a chance the two of them might become friends, was trying to push them together so they would, what—bond?

Conor looked at the back of the other boy’s head, the hostile set of his shoulders.

At least we both agree on fuck that.

It was almost four o’clock when they turned into a small parking lot on the south side of the new mall. It was bordered by an L-shaped strip—a bead shop, an H&R Block, a Subway, a massage parlor, a hardware store—

Tae Kwon Do?

Guyler said nothing, did not slow, marched straight up to the wide glass doors and pulled them open, stepping into a kind of lobby area without looking back.

Conor hesitated.

Inside, the other boy had already vanished, stepping through another door on the side of the space that looked like it led to a bathroom or a locker room or something.

Conor gnawed at his lip.

What are you, a fucking vampire?

He let loose a long, reluctant sigh and went inside.

The lobby was a small, carpeted space, with a handful of chairs lined up along the inside of the glass wall, as if to allow parents to watch. There was a set of cubbies—a pair of water fountains—two doors which Conor could now see were marked Men and Women. On the opposite side, there was a kind of mini office area, with a desk and a computer and a rolly chair and a dozen plaques hanging on the wall. The chair was empty, the computer screen black.

There was another wall—floor-to-ceiling glass, like the doors—separating the lobby from an inner space that looked like the main training floor. There were twenty or so kids inside, ranging in age from about six to maybe sixteen. They were all wearing white uniforms and colored belts—some were stretching, others practicing kicks and punches, a few running around laughing and chasing each other. It didn’t seem like class had started yet.

There were mirrors all along the left side of the space, and a line of big heavy punching bags on the right. The floor was covered in some kind of thin puzzle matting, like in a preschool play area, wide patches of red bordered in blue in an eye-twisting combination.

Guyler emerged from the bathroom, now barefoot and wearing the same uniform as the other kids, with a wide green belt tied around his waist. He walked past Conor without a word, without even a glance, swept open the door to the inner space and stepped inside, bowing as he did so with one hand covering his heart.

So. Is a green belt, like. Good?

Conor squashed the voice. There was a quiet dignity to the whole place, to the way Guyler had bowed as he passed through the door. His shoulder Ashleigh didn’t belong here.

He turned, dropping his bag into one of the empty chairs, settling himself into another. Inside the space, a man wearing a black uniform had emerged from some back room and was striding briskly across the floor. He stopped at a large, ornate drum in the corner, resting on a gilded wooden stand, and struck it four times with a felted mallet. All of the kids came running, sorting themselves out into neat, straight rows—

—all except Guyler.

Conor frowned.

The man had turned to face the same direction as the rows of students, all of them looking up at a flag hung over the mirrors, a yin yang surrounded by clusters of thick black stripes. Their hands were over their hearts, just as Guyler’s had been as he’d walked through the inner door, and the man seemed to be reciting something Conor couldn’t quite make out.

Meanwhile, Guyler had dropped down to the ground, had stretched out horizontally, his hands curled into fists, and was now doing pushups on his knuckles.

What—

Slow, steady, up and down as the man finished his recitation, as he turned back to the students and began leading the class in a series of stretches. Conor hadn’t been counting, but it seemed like the other boy must have done at least fifty pushups before he paused, pushing himself up to a kneeling position, his face flushed and sweaty.

The class was chanting, now—loudly, in some language Conor didn’t know—loudly enough to be heard clearly through the glass as they did jumping jacks, squat thrusts, rotated their hips and their shoulders.

None of them spared Guyler a single glance.

After a time, the other boy lowered himself back to the floor and did ten more pushups, arms trembling.

Then another rest.

Then ten more.

Then another rest, and ten more.

The man at the front was dividing the class into groups, now, sending them off into different patches of red. The two oldest kids seemed to be helpers—they took charge of two of the groups while the man bounced back and forth between two others. One set of kids was practicing some kind of whirling, spinning kick. Another was working on some sort of wrestling move, the students grappling with each other, heaving one another over their hips.

Minutes passed. Still no one spoke to Guyler, who had slowed down even further, was doing pushups five at a time, now, with long breaks in between. Conor couldn’t be sure, but if he’d had to guess, he would have said that the other boy had easily done well over a hundred, maybe as many as a hundred and fifty. He noticed that Guyler wasn’t actually on the mat—had confined himself to a narrow strip of carpet just inside the door and had not stepped onto the floor proper.

Conor didn’t understand—had the distinct feeling of not understanding, an overwhelming sense that there was some thing here, some secret meaning that he was not privy to. At one point, he thought he saw the man in the black uniform glance in Guyler’s direction, saw his expression flicker, a frown tightening his face for just a moment before he looked away again—

Five more pushups.

And five more.

And five more.

At one point, two of the kids began goofing off, hitting each other with the wide, flat paddles that they had been using as targets, and the man in the black uniform called them out, sharply, gesturing for them to drop to the floor. Conor watched as they each did ten pushups, then stood and bowed respectfully before turning back to their drills.

It’s a punishment.

But no one was punishing Guyler. No one had said anything. No one was even watching. It was like he wasn’t even part of the class, part of the school—like he was just a piece of furniture that no one happened to be using.

And there he goes again…

As the hour wore on, Conor’s curiosity began to give way to something like respect—admiration—maybe even awe, though the thought left a bitter taste in his mouth. He had never been particularly impressed by wrestlers and body-builders, but he’d done enough training over the summer and fall to have a sense of just what it meant, that the other boy was still going—just how hard Guyler was pushing. He had to have done at least two hundred pushups, and while he was now down to sets of three, grinding them out with glacial slowness, his face showed no emotion—no frustration, no anger, no reluctance or pain.

Disciplined.

That’s how he looked. Disciplined. It was a word that was supposed to belong to places like this, a word Conor associated with karate and jiu jitsu and tae kwon do, with ninjas and samurai and Buddhist monks. But it wasn’t something Conor had ever seen before.

Not from the outside, anyway.

It was uncomfortable, that sense of kinship. The recognition—in Guyler Stevens, of all people—of something Conor had been painstakingly nourishing in himself, something he’d felt proud of. It clashed horribly with the scribbled penises, the ruined journal, the endless stream of taunts and jabs and acid observations.

(Even less comfortably: it somehow brought to mind the other boy’s fury with Ashleigh, which had its own echo in Conor’s own resentment, not to mention Conor’s long-running frustration with Ashleigh’s unwillingness to buckle down, his fundamental unseriousness—)

Conor shook his head, trying to clear away the traitorous thoughts. Beyond the glass, Guyler leaned forward, braced his fists against the carpet, executed a single slow, quivering pushup and then dropped his knees once more.

Eventually, the class ended, the instructor beating the drum again, summoning the students back into their neat little rows, saying a few words before dismissing them all with a bow. They streamed out through the doors, saying nothing at all as they passed by the kneeling boy, who managed one final pushup before standing, and bowing, and leaving, following the other boys into the bathroom to change.

Why?

Why, and—and what, too, Conor knew what he had seen but he didn’t know what it was, what it meant. Guyler emerged from the bathroom, his hair plastered to his forehead, and turned and walked straight out the door and into the parking lot without a word, leaving Conor to scramble after him. He wanted to understand, but the thought of simply asking was somehow impossible, was at once too aggressive and too intimate, he had no idea how to start a conversation like that, not with Guyler Stevens…
