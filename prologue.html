Prologue – Binder’s Gap

⚜

In the last hour of summer, as the classroom fluorescents flickered to life and the first of the buses coughed its way into the parking lot, Conor Moreton wriggled out from the dark space beneath the equipment shed and began to climb.

He was alone, and that was not part of the plan. Alone, getting onto the shed meant squeezing into the narrow gap where it butted up against the gym, using the pressure between the two walls and his body to inch his way upward. It was slow going, and he always slipped at the top, scraping his arms against the rough shingle as he pulled himself past the gutter. Much easier with two—one to boost, and the other to reach back and help the first up.

But Ashleigh was not coming, could not possibly be coming, was at the very least grounded and most likely suspended. Conor knew this—

(Had to know it, on some level, given how skillfully he managed to not-think it, the words somehow failing to form even as his thoughts swirled all around it, like a blind man weaving through furniture in his living room.)

—yet still he’d waited for the other boy, shivering in the dark, as the night turned grey and the teachers trickled in, as hope gave way to outright denial and even denial wore thin.

Then he’d waited longer.

(It was fear, of course. Just fear, he would have said, had anyone asked—because fear was something he knew, understood, had ways of dealing with. Proven ways. Practiced ways.)

(But this fear was different, was darker, ancient. It was Guyler’s blood staining the sidewalk, and the way his pupils had been different sizes as the paramedics loaded him onto the stretcher. It was the empty cheerfulness of Ashleigh’s voicemail, the awful silence as Conor texted him, messaged him, threw acorns at his bedroom window. It was the paralyzing realization that the rules had changed, had maybe never really been there in the first place—that the lines on the road were just paint, really, and there was nothing, nothing at all between him and the sort of things you heard about on the news.)

Ten minutes.

Guyler and Ashleigh had been alone together for ten minutes. Probably not even. Maybe less than five, once you factored out the time Ashleigh would have spent climbing down from the roof.

In five minutes, the two of them had ended the world.

(And as thoughts like that came and kept coming, it became more and more important that Conor wait for him—for his friend, who was brave, who was invincible, who would laugh and say lighten up, man, you act like we’ve never been in a little trouble before. Ashleigh was on his way, had to be, just a few more minutes and he would round the corner, a spring in his step and an easy excuse on his lips. And they’d do the climb together and it would be fine, everything would be fine, Conor just had to give him a little bit more time—)

In the end, it was the sound of car doors slamming in the front circle that got him moving, a sound that said if he didn’t start climbing right now, it wouldn’t matter either way.

With one final heave, he dragged himself past the gutter and onto the slanted roof, staying low, the shallow peak hiding him from the glass windows of the building opposite. Rolling over onto his back, he waited for his breathing to slow, picking dirt and grit out of his scabs as he stared unseeing into the heavy September sky.

Rule number one, he thought quietly. Protect yourself.

(Perhaps thought was the wrong word. The thought happened. It wasn’t quite the case that Conor thought it—was not the end result of any conscious effort. The words were simply there.)

His next checkpoint was an aluminum awning, five feet away and two feet higher up, covering the sidewalk that ran between the gym and the front office. It was an unpleasant jump even at the best of times—the slope of the roof made for an awkward takeoff, and there was something spooky about the way the awning hung in space, with no wall underneath for him to kick against if he fell short.

But the awning was the way to the electives building, and the electives building was the way to the tree, and the tree was the way to the pipe, and the pipe was the way to the roof, and the roof was—

(His thoughts stuttered.)

The roof was where he needed to be.

He rolled back over onto hands and toes, lizard-fashion, listening.

Rule two: never move without thinking.

There were no sounds, in the quad below. No laughter, no footsteps. Slowly, he pushed himself upward, peering over the peak of the roof, eyes alert for any sign of movement in the windows facing the grassy square.

Clear.

Silently, he popped to his feet, stepped to the edge, and leaped across the gap, wincing as the metal of the awning flexed and groaned under his weight. The metal was wet, last night’s rain still slicking every surface, and he picked his way with care, making his way over to the electives building where he stretched as high as he could, his fingers just barely able to reach the edge of the roof proper.

Like most of the rooftops at East Binder Secondary School, this one was covered in a thin layer of gravel, dotted here and there with air conditioning units and surrounded by a raised metal edge exactly four inches wide—

Conor’s lip twisted.

Right.

A wet metal edge.

He wiped his hands on his sweatshirt, looked down at the dull, corrugated surface.

Rule three: safety before simplicity, simplicity before speed, and silence wherever possible.

The edge would be slippery. But that didn’t necessarily mean dangerous—not if he stayed focused, stayed locked in, kept his feet under him and the rest of his body under control. And the alternative was to crunch his way through the gravel, directly over the classrooms where the teachers were this very minute preparing for the day…

Conor let out a breath.

Arms out for balance, he walked carefully along the edge, bracing himself for a shout from below, willing himself not to twitch or stumble. But no one saw him, and soon enough, he rounded the corner to the long side of the building, where the magnolia stood.

It was a gnarled and magnificent tree, far older than the structures around it, rising proudly from the space between the electives building and the auditorium beyond. Its branches poured down from its trunk like a jungle waterfall, too high to be reached from the ground but spreading carelessly over the rooftop. Stepping in among them, Conor leaned out over empty space, grabbing hold of an arm-thick limb at the edge of his reach and swinging into the foliage.

Slowly, methodically, he picked his way across the tree. Soon, he arrived at a junction—almost a fork in the trunk itself—where one massive branch split away from the rest and reached out to brush, fingerlike, against the auditorium wall.

Rule four: don’t go anywhere you can’t get back from.

The first time—ages ago, at the beginning of summer—it had taken all of his courage to creep out to the very edge, where the sagging, overloaded limb let him rest his hands against the bricks, a full sixteen feet above the ground. But that was where the prize lay, the key to the puzzle: a sturdy, three-inch metal pipe emerging mysteriously from the otherwise empty patch of wall and stretching all the way to the roof.

No way, Ashleigh had said, when Conor first suggested it. Not in a million years.

But there was no other way, as Ashleigh himself had reluctantly admitted, and so (after a week) no way became fine—you first.

Taking a deep breath, Conor readied himself, his right hand curled around the pipe, his left still braced against the wall, his body bobbing slowly up and down as the breeze whispered past the shaky branch. With one sure, swift motion, he sprang forward, planting both feet and pulling himself in to hug the pipe as the branch tore upward past him. A smattering of leaves and twigs rustled their way toward the ground, dusting his hair, his shoulders. He waited until all was still, shifting back and forth, testing the grip of his sneakers against the bricks as he leaned back, rappel-fashion.

And then he climbed.

Hand over hand, step by step, up past the leafy canopy and out into the open, using the pipe like a rope, each breath coming in animal rhythm with the scrape of his shoes and the beat of his heart. Once, he paused, shaking out the tightness in each arm and wiping the sweat from his brow, but otherwise he moved like a machine, like a clock, every action efficient and precise. Scarcely twenty seconds passed before his fingers found the sudden emptiness that marked the end of the pipe, and then he was there, chest heaving and face flushed, swaying like a boxer at the summit of the school.

Okay, he thought, as his heartbeat slowed once more, his fingers slowly coming unclenched.

Okay.

There were whispers of conversation below, a soft, tumbling chorus that came and went as the wind changed direction, floating up from the front courtyard where the earlybirds were gathering. He could hear voices but not words—there was happiness there, and excitement, a touch of nervousness and an air of expectation.

(It was the first day back, after all.)

Shoobies, whispered Ashleigh’s voice in his head, and for a moment Conor squeezed his eyes shut, feeling the other boy’s absence, the hole in the air where his best friend should be.

Rule five: use your powers for good.

Conor opened his eyes again.

Ahead, the world was a patchwork of grey, the rooftop shouldering a sky of slate and gunmetal, slowly turning silver as the sun backlit the clouds. There was the usual scattering of squat, steel structures, fans and ventilation shafts, a trapdoor set in weatherworn concrete. A pair of long, thin furrows slashed through the gravel like tire tracks, twin paths where the stones had been swept aside to reveal the black surface underneath.

Moving on autopilot, Conor took the left one, his footfalls nearly silent. Ten steps in, and the whispers faded away; thirty, and he stood in the exact center of the roof, in a place where the two paths widened, crossed, bleeding into one another to form a clear space large enough for two to sit in. There he paused, his eyes roving over the exposed shingle, searching.

There.

He crouched, reaching.

It was a twist of paper, half an inch long and narrower than a pencil, a few loose, blackened flakes still poking out of one burnt end. They fell as he lifted it, vanishing into the wind.

It isn’t dangerous, Ashleigh had said. Not if you do it just once.

He’d rolled the joint right there in front of Conor, yesterday—pulling the musty flakes from the bag they’d been haunting since June. Conor had objected, of course—Ashleigh was talking about doing drugs—about doing drugs on a rooftop—a school rooftop—about stealing drugs from god knows who—wasn’t this the literal exact opposite of why they were there? Why they’d spent nearly the whole summer baking in the sun, watching and waiting?

I just thought, Ashleigh had said.

(It had taken more words than that, but that had been the beginning, and the end had been blood and sirens and angry grownups, a thousand questions and only Conor to answer, and Conor didn’t know—)

With a sudden, violent motion, he shredded the tiny roll, scattering the bits of paper, kicking the gravel around to cover what the wind wouldn’t take. Reanimated, he turned, striding over to the nearest air conditioning unit, where he tugged at the loose panel until it came away in his hands. Reaching into the dark space inside, he groped around until his fingers touched plastic, and pulled out the bag.

Rule number one: protect yourself.

His first thought had been to destroy it—just upend the bag at the edge of the roof, watch the musty flakes disappear into the wind. But—

But?

He didn’t know. But Guyler had known they’d been on the roof, might have told—but the teachers might search up here, wondering why—but whoever owned the damn thing might hear about it, and come looking—

It didn’t quite make sense, didn’t quite hold together. But he felt, in his guts, that he couldn’t just leave it, couldn’t destroy it—that there was some narrow, twisting path between disasters, and it started with getting it out of here, before anyone else could find it.

(Rule four was don’t go anywhere you can’t get back from.)

Taking a breath, he tucked the bag into his belt, began to turn back toward the pipe—

(Again there was the blind-man feeling, the anti-thought, a sense that a part of him was trying to think something and the rest of him was saying no.)

Time to go, he thought to himself, the words almost pleading.

But his body refused to move. Not until he began turning the other way—toward the far side of the roof, where the two paths ran straight and true right to the very edge like twin rivers pouring over a cliff.

Don’t, he thought quietly. Do not.

But he was already in motion, his feet falling once again into the left of the two paths.

(His path.)

(Because the final part of rule three was silence wherever possible.)

Thirty paces and he was there—was somewhere, a place more placeful.

Binder’s Gap.

Directly in front of him was the gym, its roof seventeen feet away and twelve feet further down. In between was nothing but air—air, and a forty-eight foot drop to the sidewalk below. Conor knew the numbers by heart—had traced them, paced them, measured them over and over again until they carved themselves into his mind in the form of an inescapable truth:

It takes just under two seconds for an object to fall forty-eight feet.

And there—faint enough that he might have missed it, but clearer and clearer the longer he looked—

Something new, that hadn’t been there yesterday. A pair of shallow scars, a hint of black shingle, a slight interruption in the otherwise smooth gravel, just a couple of feet in from the edge and perfectly in line with the path that stretched back from where Conor now stood.

There, he thought—quietly, tentatively, as if giving his mind a chance to get used to it.

There.

That was where he had landed.

For a moment, he couldn’t quite process it, the impossible hugeness of it. He felt pressure against his toes, looked down to find that he had shuffled forward, his feet now pressed against the raised metal border. Half-step forward, half-step back.

Rule two: never move without thinking.

Conor took a half-step back.

I really did it.

He looked down at his palms, at the swollen bruises from the bone-rattling shock when he’d landed, when he’d rolled.

I really did it.

They had laughed as they’d broken forward, the two of them—the thick, wet breeze of the coming storm tearing at their jackets, an electric tingle of possibility in the air. They’d been neck-and-neck, their feet pounding on the rooftop—

(So loud, so reckless.)

—and Conor had pulled ahead, exhilarated, running as hard as he could, for once he was beating Ashleigh, he was winning—

Then the leap—

—the flight—

—the landing—

—and the realization, as he rolled to his feet, that something was wrong, horribly wrong. Conor had turned, and every crunch in the gravel had come from his own motion—there were no other sounds on the rooftop, none at all—he was alone, he was alone—the almost heart-stopping terror as everything else fell away and there was nothing but the thought, that Ashleigh hadn’t made it, that he was down below on the grass.

Conor had raced back to the lip, had almost thrown himself over it in his panic, and when he’d seen that the ground below was empty—

Relief.

“Ash!” he’d called out, looking up—

(Thinking that the other boy was hiding, was embarrassed, that he had chickened out but could be razzed into trying again, especially now that Conor could tell him it was easy, as easy as standing on two feet, why on Earth had they both been so afraid, waited so long?)

—but there had been no answer.

No answer, and as the seconds stretched out—as he called out a second time, and then a third—Conor had felt the dread rising once more, some sixth sense whispering, whispering. It had taken him forever to figure out a way down from the gym—

(Why had they not thought of that in advance, and planned out a way down?)

—and by the time he finally touched ground, ran around to the magnolia, it was too late. Guyler was down, Ashleigh was running, and for the next hour everything was chaos and confusion until finally he’d broken down in tears and his parents had taken him home and put him in bed. He had fallen asleep, must have slept, because he snapped fully awake at five in the morning with one thought crystal clear in his head: that he had to move the bag now, before anyone else could get to it.

And now he was here, he had it, mission accomplished—

(Or as good as, anyway.)

—and—

—and—

And what?

He didn’t know.

Didn’t know anything. Didn’t know what had happened—not really—didn’t know what would happen next, what to do next—had had only this one lone next action, and was now out of cards to play. Whatever magic had filled the rooftop yesterday was fading, fading, draining away—without being able to put it into words, Conor had the profound impression that these were the very last moments of—

Of—

Of something. Something sane and familiar, something that had been dealt its deathblow the night before, and was now breathing its final breath.

Rule five: use your powers for—

He dashed the thought, scattering its pieces, and nothing took its place.

Reaching down, he picked up a stone, tossed it gently, underhanded, out into space. It soared in a perfect arc, slicing through the wind, vanishing into the gravel on the other roof with only the smallest of sounds. He picked up another, let this one slip from his fingers and drop straight down, counting silently in his head—one Mississippi, two Mississi—

(The stone struck the sidewalk.)

Hey, Dead Poet’s Society, whispered Ashleigh’s voice. I think you might be out of time.

Give me a minute, Conor shot back.

For what?

(He tried to think of an answer. He really did. But that was the problem—there wasn’t one, not in any place his thoughts could reach alone. It was Ashleigh who had been there, Ashleigh who knew—and Ashleigh was somewhere else.)

Conor looked across the gap, and into yesterday.
